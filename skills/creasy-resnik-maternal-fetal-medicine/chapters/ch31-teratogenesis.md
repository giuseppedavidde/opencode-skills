# Chapter 31: Teratogenesis and Environmental Exposure

## Core Idea
About 10% of major birth defects are attributable to environmental exposures and are therefore partly preventable. Teratogenicity is not a property of a drug alone: it depends on genotype, dose, route, and the gestational age at exposure. Most human evidence is observational and incomplete, so no single study design is sufficient; conclusions must be confirmed across animal data, registries, cohort studies, and case-control studies. Counseling must distinguish an exposure that has already occurred from one that is anticipated, because the questions and the risk calculations differ.

## Frameworks Introduced
**Wilson's six principles of teratology.** (1) Susceptibility depends on the genotype of the conceptus and its interaction with the environment. (2) Susceptibility varies with the developmental stage at exposure. (3) Agents act by specific mechanisms on developing cells and tissues. (4) The end points are death, malformation, growth restriction, and functional deficit. (5) Access to the conceptus depends on maternal and placental pharmacokinetics. (6) The four manifestations are dose related, with a threshold below which no effect occurs.

**Sources of safety data.** Animal studies define dose and mechanism but have limited human relevance; case reports generate hypotheses but have no denominator; pregnancy registries provide early signals but small numbers and no comparison group; database cohorts are large but have limited exposure and outcome validation; case-control studies have power for rare outcomes but recall and lag limitations.

**FDA pregnancy categories.** The A/B/C/D/X system is being replaced by narrative labeling. Categories often ignore timing, dose, and route, and some X assignments (ribavirin, statins) rest on theoretical or animal concerns rather than human data. Category assignment should not be the sole basis for counseling.

## Key Concepts
- Critical periods: the embryonic period (organogenesis) is the window for structural malformations; later exposures produce growth and functional deficits. For warfarin, the nasal and bony effects occur at 6–9 weeks; CNS and eye effects occur with later exposure.
- Warfarin: a systematic review of 17 studies and 979 exposed pregnancies found a 6% incidence of warfarin embryopathy. A larger series found major defects OR 3.86 (95% CI 1.76–8.00), preterm delivery 16.0% versus 7.6%, term birth weight 3166 versus 3411 g, and spontaneous abortion 42% versus 14%; risk is higher above 5 mg/day.
- Valproic acid: neural tube defect risk about 1–2% and a fourfold to sevenfold increase in major malformations, with midface hypoplasia, epicanthal folds, short nose, thin upper lip, and reduced cognitive ability. Carbamazepine: spina bifida risk about 1%, with minor craniofacial changes and growth deficiency.
- Phenytoin: fetal hydantoin syndrome in about 10% of exposed infants (growth restriction, microcephaly, digit and nail hypoplasia, craniofacial anomalies); IQ about 10 points lower after monotherapy. Polytherapy carries a greater risk. Valproate and phenytoin risks may be mediated partly by folate antagonism.
- ACE inhibitors and angiotensin II receptor antagonists: second- and third-trimester fetopathy with renal tubular dysgenesis, oligohydramnios, hypocalvaria, growth restriction, and death. First-trimester cardiovascular and CNS signals are not consistently replicated.
- Lithium: first-trimester exposure carries at most a very small increase in Ebstein anomaly (baseline about 1/20,000).
- Isotretinoin: estimated risks of at least 22% spontaneous abortion, 28% structural defects, and 47% mild-to-moderate intellectual deficit, even without structural anomalies. The pattern includes CNS malformations, microtia or anotia, micrognathia, cleft palate, conotruncal defects, thymic and eye anomalies, and limb reduction. No malformation risk when discontinued before conception. Acitretin has a longer half-life (50–60 hours) and converts to etretinate with ethanol; stop before pregnancy and avoid ethanol for at least 2 months after treatment.
- Mycophenolate: microtia or anotia, oral clefts, and conotruncal heart disease; in one prospective series, malformations occurred in 8 of 57 pregnancies, and the miscarriage rate was 28%.
- Methotrexate/aminopterin: growth deficiency, absent or poor calvarial ossification, hypoplastic supraorbital ridges, small low-set ears, micrognathia, and limb anomalies; the dose associated with the syndrome is above 10 mg/week.
- Systemic corticosteroids in the first trimester: threefold to sixfold increase in cleft lip with or without cleft palate, translating to about 3–6 cases per 1000 exposed pregnancies. Growth restriction is dose related.
- Ionizing radiation: microcephaly, intellectual deficiency, and growth deficiency require about 50 cGy (50 rad) or more to the uterus; the highest risk is at 8–15 weeks. Diagnostic procedures are not a risk unless the cumulative uterine dose exceeds 10 cGy; guidelines recommend keeping the dose below 5 cGy.
- Methylmercury: Minamata disease with cerebral-palsy-like disorder and intellectual deficiency. The National Research Council set a maternal intake limit of 0.1 µg/kg/day. Avoid shark, swordfish, king mackerel, and tilefish; limit other cooked fish to 12 ounces (340 g) per week.
- Lead: maternal blood >30 µg/dL is associated with spontaneous abortion, preterm birth, and intellectual deficiency; >10 µg/dL with subtle neurobehavioral effects.
- Ethanol: fetal alcohol syndrome in about 1–4 per 1000 live births; 4–44% of children of heavily drinking women have the fully expressed syndrome. Features are growth restriction, microcephaly, short palpebral fissures, smooth philtrum, thin vermilion, and neurobehavioral deficits. No safe threshold has been defined; binge exposure raises the risk of spontaneous abortion, stillbirth, and sudden infant death.
- Smoking: perinatal mortality is 56% higher with one pack per day or more. Associated defects include oral clefts, gastroschisis, craniosynostosis, and clubfoot, with dose-response and gene-environment interaction.

## Anti-patterns
- Equating an FDA category with an individual patient's risk, or treating category X as uniformly proven human teratogenicity.
- Advising termination after a single case report or registry signal without confirming the signal and quantifying absolute risk.
- Assuming a drug is safe because adverse outcomes were not reported; absence of reports is not evidence of safety.
- Ignoring dose, route, and gestational timing when counseling.
- Reassuring a patient that "the placenta is a barrier."

## Key Takeaways
1. Establish the exposure's timing, dose, route, and indication before estimating risk; counsel differently for past versus anticipated exposure.
2. Use absolute risks and the best available human data; do not rely on FDA categories alone.
3. Stop known teratogens before conception when possible: isotretinoin, acitretin, warfarin, valproate, mycophenolate, methotrexate, and ACE inhibitors or ARBs.
4. Recommend folate supplementation and smoking and alcohol cessation; there is no defined safe threshold for ethanol.
5. Limit radiation to <5 cGy to the uterus and methylmercury exposure by avoiding predator fish and limiting other fish to 340 g per week.
