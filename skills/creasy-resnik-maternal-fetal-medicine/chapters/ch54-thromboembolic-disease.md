# Chapter 54: Thromboembolic Disease in Pregnancy

## Core Idea

Venous thromboembolism accounts for almost 20% of pregnancy-related deaths in the United States, and pregnancy raises the risk about sixfold. Most events occur without a prior history, so the diagnosis depends on a high index of suspicion and a rapid imaging algorithm rather than on risk factors alone. Deep vein thrombosis is predominantly left-sided and antepartum, whereas pulmonary embolism is predominantly postpartum and strongly associated with cesarean delivery. Treatment requires therapeutic heparin for at least 6 months, including 6 weeks postpartum. Prevention depends on stratifying risk by thrombophilia, prior event, and peripartum exposures.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Diagnostic algorithm for suspected deep vein thrombosis.** Compression ultrasonography (CUS) is the cornerstone. A positive study prompts treatment. After a negative study, a low-risk patient is discharged to routine follow-up, whereas a moderate- or high-risk patient undergoes serial CUS or magnetic resonance venography. D-dimer is complementary only; a negative result does not exclude DVT in pregnancy.
- **Diagnostic algorithm for suspected pulmonary embolism (ACCP).** D-dimer cannot exclude pulmonary embolism in pregnancy. CUS is the first test only when DVT signs are present. Chest radiography is the triage tool: a normal film leads to ventilation-perfusion scanning, an abnormal film leads to computed tomographic pulmonary angiography (CTPA). A nondiagnostic ventilation-perfusion scan is followed by CTPA rather than clinical management alone.
- **Anticoagulant dosing framework.** Therapeutic enoxaparin 1 mg (100 IU)/kg subcutaneously every 12 hours; tinzaparin 175 IU/kg once daily; dalteparin 200 units/kg once daily or 100 IU/kg twice daily. Prophylactic enoxaparin 40 mg daily; tinzaparin 2500 to 4500 IU daily; dalteparin 2500 to 5000 IU daily or 2500 IU twice daily.
- **Anti-Xa monitoring targets.** Therapeutic 0.5 to 1.0 IU/mL for twice-daily dosing and 1.0 to 1.5 IU/mL for once-daily dosing, drawn 4 hours after injection. Prophylactic target 0.2 to 0.4 IU/mL; subtherapeutic target 0.3 to 0.7 IU/mL. Unfractionated heparin: aPTT 1.5 to 2.5 times control, or anti-Xa 0.35 to 0.70 U/mL.
- **Mechanical heart valve regimens (ACCP).** Adjusted-dose LMWH twice daily to anti-Xa 1 U/mL, adjusted-dose subcutaneous unfractionated heparin twice daily to aPTT at least twice control, or heparin until 13 weeks followed by warfarin until close to delivery.
- **Risk-based prophylaxis.** Therapeutic anticoagulation throughout pregnancy for antithrombin deficiency, antiphospholipid syndrome, and homozygotes or compound heterozygotes for factor V Leiden or prothrombin gene mutation with prior VTE or an affected first-degree relative. Postpartum prophylaxis for all women with prior VTE.
- **Nonpharmacologic prevention.** Graduated compression stockings reduce DVT from 26% to 13% in immobilized patients (odds ratio 0.35) and from 4.3% to 0.9% in postpartum patients. Intermittent pneumatic compression reduces DVT by 62% versus placebo.

## Key Concepts

- VTE prevalence is 1 per 1627 births; 77% are deep vein thrombosis and 23% pulmonary embolism; 86% have no antecedent VTE history.
- DVT involves the lower extremities in 98.4% and the left leg in up to 80%. It is antepartum in 74% and postpartum in 26%, with a mean gestational age at diagnosis of 16.8 ± 2.4 weeks. Nearly 50% are detected by 15 weeks, 38% at 16 to 30 weeks, and 12% after 30 weeks.
- Pulmonary embolism is diagnosed postpartum in 60.5% and is strongly associated with cesarean delivery (relative risk 30.3). Among cesarean patients who developed PE, 36% were older than 35 years and 55% were obese (body mass index above 29).
- Among nonpregnant adults with fatal PE, 65% die within 1 hour of onset.
- Pregnancy raises fibrinogen and factors VII, VIII, IX, X, and XII by 20% to 1000%; protein S falls to 39% of normal in the second trimester and 31% in the third; PAI-1 rises threefold to fourfold.
- Age older than 35 years doubles VTE risk. Antiphospholipid syndrome carries a 1% to 5% VTE risk despite prophylaxis.
- Factor V Leiden is present in 40% of patients with VTE, but the pregnancy risk in heterozygotes without a personal or family history is less than 0.3%, versus more than 10% with such a history.
- CUS sensitivity and specificity are 95% and 98% for proximal DVT; for calf DVT sensitivity is 92.5% and specificity 98.7%, with indeterminate studies in 9.3% to 82.7%.
- D-dimer is 100% sensitive for DVT in pregnancy but specificity is only 6% to 23%. Normal values rise through gestation: 191 ± 25 ng/mL at 16 weeks, 393 ± 72 at 26 weeks, and 544 ± 96 at 34 weeks.
- Treated uncomplicated pulmonary embolism has mortality below 2% to 7%; mortality exceeds 50% when the patient is hemodynamically unstable.
- Thrombolysis shows no mortality benefit over heparin alone in randomized trials (odds ratio 0.89; CI 0.45 to 1.78).
- LMWH causes no heparin-induced thrombocytopenia in more than 2000 pregnancies, with osteoporosis risk 0.04% and bleeding risk 1.98%. Protamine 1 mg per 100 anti-Xa units reverses about 80% of activity.
- Regional anesthesia is contraindicated within 24 hours of therapeutic LMWH. Delivery more than 12 hours after a prophylactic dose or more than 24 hours after a therapeutic dose is not associated with treatment-induced hemorrhage.

## Anti-patterns

- Using a normal D-dimer to exclude pulmonary embolism in pregnancy.
- Omitting CUS because the patient has no leg symptoms; CUS is the first test only when DVT signs are present.
- Stopping anticoagulation at delivery instead of continuing for 6 weeks postpartum and 6 months total.
- Placing a neuraxial block within 24 hours of therapeutic LMWH.
- Assuming a negative single CUS excludes calf DVT in a high-risk patient without serial studies.
- Treating all factor V Leiden heterozygotes with therapeutic heparin despite a less than 0.3% risk without prior VTE.
- Relying on graduated compression stockings alone in a patient with a strong thrombophilia.

## Key Takeaways

1. Start therapeutic LMWH as soon as VTE is suspected unless contraindicated, and continue for at least 6 months including 6 weeks postpartum.
2. Use CUS first for suspected DVT and chest radiography followed by ventilation-perfusion scanning or CTPA for suspected PE.
3. Monitor anti-Xa when treating with LMWH in pregnancy, targeting 0.5 to 1.0 IU/mL for twice-daily dosing.
4. Switch from LMWH to unfractionated heparin at 36 weeks, or earlier if preterm delivery is expected, to permit neuraxial anesthesia.
5. Give therapeutic anticoagulation throughout pregnancy to women with antithrombin deficiency, antiphospholipid syndrome, or highly thrombogenic compound thrombophilias plus prior VTE.
6. Use graduated compression stockings and intermittent pneumatic compression in high-risk women admitted for labor, bed rest, or cesarean delivery.
7. Reserve inferior vena cava filters for absolute contraindications to anticoagulation or recurrent PE despite adequate anticoagulation.
