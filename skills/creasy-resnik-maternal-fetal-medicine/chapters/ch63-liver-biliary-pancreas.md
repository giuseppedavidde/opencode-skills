# Chapter 63: Diseases of the Liver, Biliary System, and Pancreas

## Core Idea
The liver does not enlarge in normal pregnancy, so hepatomegaly, jaundice, or scleral icterus are always pathologic. Normal gestation lowers albumin by 20–40% and raises alkaline phosphatase two- to fourfold from placental production, which distorts the interpretation of routine liver panels. The chapter separates pregnancy-specific disorders — intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy (ICP), acute fatty liver of pregnancy (AFLP), preeclampsia/HELLP — from coincidental viral, autoimmune, and biliary disease, and stresses that timing of delivery is the definitive therapy for the pregnancy-specific group. Prompt recognition matters because HELLP and AFLP overlap in about half of cases, and hepatic rupture is a catastrophic complication.

## Frameworks Introduced
**Swansea diagnostic criteria for AFLP.** Six or more criteria are required in the absence of another cause: vomiting, abdominal pain, polydipsia/polyuria, encephalopathy, elevated bilirubin, hypoglycemia, elevated urate, leukocytosis, ascites or bright liver on ultrasound, elevated transaminases, elevated ammonia, renal impairment, coagulopathy, and microvesicular steatosis on biopsy.

**ICP diagnostic and management pathway.** Pruritus without rash → measure AST/ALT plus serum bile acids (either can be abnormal first) → liver ultrasound to exclude obstruction → screen for hepatitis C, autoimmune hepatitis, and primary biliary cirrhosis → ursodeoxycholic acid → fetal surveillance → delivery.

**AFLP/HELLP differentiation.** Bilirubin, creatinine, uric acid, neutrophils, prothrombin time, acidosis, and hypoglycemia are higher or more abnormal in AFLP; isolated transaminase elevation in severe preeclampsia rarely exceeds 500 U/L.

**Gallstone disease management choice.** Conservative therapy (nil per os, intravenous fluids, nasogastric suction, analgesia, antibiotics) versus operative management (laparoscopic cholecystectomy, ideally second trimester, with low pneumoperitoneum pressure) versus ERCP for documented ductal stones.

## Key Concepts
- Liver blood flow is constant at 25–33% of cardiac output. Serum albumin falls 20–40%. Alkaline phosphatase may exceed 1,000 U/L from placental isoenzyme and returns to normal within weeks of delivery.
- Trimester-specific upper limits: AST 10–28, 11–29, 11–30 IU/L; ALT 6–32 IU/L in all trimesters; bilirubin 4–16, 3–13, 3–14 µmol/L; GGT 5–37, 5–43, 5–41 IU/L; alkaline phosphatase 32–100, 43–135, 130–418 IU/L.
- Palmar erythema and spider nevi occur in normal pregnancy; jaundice and hepatomegaly do not.
- ICP affects 0.7% of white women, about twice as many South Asian women, and up to 5% of Chilean women; recurrence is 60–90%. Parous sisters have a 20-fold increased risk. It is more common in multiple gestations, in women older than 35 years, and in winter.
- ICP genes: ABCB4, ATP8B1, ABCB11 (bile salt export pump), and the main bile acid receptor. Hepatitis C, autoimmune hepatitis, and primary biliary cirrhosis are associated and should be screened.
- Maternal bile acids >40 µmol/L are associated with meconium-stained fluid, fetal asphyxial events, and spontaneous preterm labor. Stillbirth in ICP is typically sudden, in appropriately grown fetuses, and may be arrhythmic.
- Ursodeoxycholic acid is the only drug consistently improving maternal symptoms and biochemistry: start 500 mg twice daily and increase to a maximum of 2 g/day. It improves cord blood and amniotic fluid bile acids.
- Avoid erythromycin, flucloxacillin, and amoxicillin-clavulanate in women with prior ICP. Hormonal contraception carries a 10% risk of pruritus or hepatic impairment.
- AFLP affects 1 in 7,000 to 16,000 pregnancies, more often in primigravidas and multiple gestations; fatty acid oxidation disorders account for about 20% of cases, and LCHAD/mitochondrial trifunctional protein defects (E474Q mutation) are the classic link. Screen neonates of affected pregnancies for MTP disorders; this is not recommended after HELLP.
- AFLP biochemical profile at diagnosis: AST mean 425 IU/L (range 120–2,317), ALT 423 (43–1,504), bilirubin 99 µmol/L (15–203), LDH 1,483 (244–3,992), glucose 4.5 mmol/L (0.6–8.7), creatinine 212 µmol/L (44–389), platelets 88 × 10⁹/L (33–303).
- In AFLP, transaminase level does not reflect severity; hepatocytes destroyed by severe injury cannot release enzymes. CT detects hepatic fat in only 50% of cases.
- Management of AFLP: intensive care, blood tests every 6 hours (prothrombin time, glucose, platelets, creatinine, liver tests, arterial gases), fresh frozen plasma as needed, large glucose loads, N-acetylcysteine, dialysis and ventilation for multiorgan failure, and early delivery. Encephalopathy plus elevated lactate predicts poor prognosis and need for transplantation.
- HELLP: liver involvement occurs in about 10% of severe preeclampsia; right upper quadrant pain signals hepatic ischemia, and transaminases may rise hours after pain begins. Laboratory abnormalities usually improve within 5 days of delivery but may worsen first. Hepatic hematoma and rupture require surgical exploration.
- Hepatitis B: without immunoprophylaxis, perinatal transmission is 70–90% when HBsAg and HBeAg are positive, but under 10% when HBeAg is negative. Acute infection transmits in 10% of first-trimester cases and 80–90% of third-trimester cases. Cesarean delivery and avoiding breastfeeding do not prevent infection. Tenofovir is category B with a 2.8% birth-defect rate, comparable to population baseline; lamivudine is no longer preferred because of resistance.
- Hepatitis C: vertical transmission about 1.7% when RNA negative and 19.4% with high viral load; passive maternal antibody persists up to 18 months. Cesarean delivery and avoiding breastfeeding do not reduce transmission.
- Hepatitis E: acute liver failure in about 20% of pregnant women, with mortality 10–20%, worst in the third trimester.
- Autoimmune hepatitis: 41% of pregnancies occur with cirrhosis; live birth 73%, prematurity 20%, flare in 33%, and serious maternal adverse event (death, transplantation, or decompensation) in 9 of 81.
- Cirrhosis: preterm delivery and stillbirth are increased; variceal hemorrhage risk is 10–18%; endoscopic screening for varices is required, and noncirrhotic portal hypertension has better outcomes than cirrhosis.
- Gallstones: 7% of nulliparous women versus 19% with two or more pregnancies; BMI >30 carries odds ratio 4.45; sludge or stones in 5.1% at second trimester, 7.9% at third trimester, 10.2% at 4–6 weeks postpartum. Biliary colic affects only 1.2% of women with known gallstones, but 55% of symptomatic admissions present with colic and 25% with acute cholecystitis.
- Nonoperative management of symptomatic cholelithiasis fails in 38%, with 34% relapse and more induction, cesarean delivery, and preterm birth. Ultrasound accuracy for cholelithiasis is 97%.
- Acute pancreatitis: gallstones are the most common cause in pregnancy; occurs more often in the third trimester and puerperium; serum amylase greater than three times normal suggests the diagnosis; CT is reserved for suspected necrosis. AFLP with pancreatitis has a particularly poor prognosis.

## Anti-patterns
- Treating an elevated alkaline phosphatase in the third trimester as liver disease; it is usually placental.
- Diagnosing ICP on pruritus alone without bile acids, or excluding it because AST and ALT are normal — bile acids and transaminases can each be abnormal first.
- Using transaminase height to grade AFLP severity.
- Relying on CT to exclude AFLP; it is only 50% sensitive for hepatic fat.
- Missing the overlap of AFLP and HELLP, or attributing coagulopathy and encephalopathy to preeclampsia alone.
- Offering cesarean delivery or formula feeding to prevent hepatitis B or C transmission — neither works.
- Delaying cholecystectomy indefinitely in symptomatic gallstone disease; conservative care fails in 38%.
- Overlooking hepatitis E in a third-trimester woman with acute liver failure; mortality is 10–20%.

## Key Takeaways
1. Treat jaundice, hepatomegaly, or right upper quadrant pain in pregnancy as pathologic until proven otherwise, and check transaminases, bilirubin, and bile acids.
2. For ICP, start ursodeoxycholic acid 500 mg twice daily (maximum 2 g/day), monitor bile acids, and plan delivery because no fetal surveillance reliably prevents sudden stillbirth.
3. Diagnose AFLP with the Swansea criteria (six or more) and deliver; manage in intensive care with 6-hourly coagulation, glucose, and chemistry monitoring.
4. Differentiate AFLP from HELLP using bilirubin, creatinine, urate, prothrombin time, glucose, and acidosis; both may coexist.
5. Give hepatitis B immunoglobulin plus vaccine within 12 hours of birth to infants of HBsAg-positive mothers, and use tenofovir for high viral load.
6. Perform laparoscopic cholecystectomy in the second trimester for recurrent biliary colic or cholecystitis; do not rely on conservative management alone.
