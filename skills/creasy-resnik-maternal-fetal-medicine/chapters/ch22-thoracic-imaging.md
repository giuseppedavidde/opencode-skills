# Chapter 22: Thoracic Imaging

## Core Idea
Most fetal thoracic masses present as a cystic or echogenic lesion with mediastinal shift, and the differential is narrow: congenital diaphragmatic hernia (CDH), congenital cystic adenomatoid malformation (CCAM), bronchopulmonary sequestration (BPS), and less common mediastinal or bronchogenic lesions. The diagnosis is made by identifying what the mass contains and where its blood supply comes from — bowel with peristalsis and a non-visualized stomach indicate CDH; an arterial feeder from the descending aorta indicates sequestration. Outcome is driven by pulmonary hypoplasia and pulmonary hypertension, not by the size of the cyst, and prenatal imaging cannot yet predict hypoplasia reliably.

## Frameworks Introduced
**CDH prognostic triad.** The three most important prognostic signs are liver herniation, right-sided lesion, and fetal lung volume estimated by the lung-to-head-circumference ratio (LHR). Observed-to-expected LHR refines risk estimation. Right-sided defects are harder to see because liver and lung have similar echogenicity; the clue is mediastinal shift, and Doppler can demonstrate hepatic vascularity.

**CCAM histologic classification.**
- **Type 1 (50–70%):** a small number of large cysts, 3–10 cm, or a single dominant cyst.
- **Type 2 (15–30%):** smaller cysts 0.5–2 cm with solid areas; about 60% have associated anomalies.
- **Type 3 (5–10%):** microcystic, appearing solid and echogenic and well circumscribed; highest risk of hydrops and pulmonary hypoplasia.

Separately, lesions are described as microcystic (<5 mm) or macrocystic (>5 mm).

**Sequestration classification.** Intralobar (ILS) versus extralobar (ELS). ELS is more common in fetuses, may be extrathoracic, and in 10% lies below the diaphragm, usually on the left. ELS is not associated with infection or malignant transformation and can be managed expectantly; ILS carries higher risk of pulmonary hypoplasia.

**Thoracic mass differential checklist.** For a cystic lung lesion: CDH (peristalsis, absent stomach in abdomen), congenital lobar emphysema, bronchogenic cyst (usually isolated, connected to the upper airway), intra-abdominal BPS (left-sided; distinguish from neuroblastoma, mesoblastic nephroma, enteric duplication cyst), mediastinal cystic hygroma, and teratoma (more vascular, more shadowing).

## Key Concepts
- **CDH** incidence is **2.4–4.9 per 10,000**. Distribution: **75% left**, **15% right**, **10% bilateral**; among fetal demises, 47% left, 27% right, 27% bilateral. It results from abnormal diaphragm development at **6–10 weeks** with incomplete closure of the pleuroperitoneal folds; associations include smoking, alcohol, vitamin A deficiency, thalidomide, and anticonvulsants.
- In left CDH the stomach appears as a cystic thoracic mass and is absent from its normal position; liver herniates in **50%**. In right CDH the liver herniates in almost all cases.
- CDH pathophysiology: herniated viscera reduce bronchial branching, alveolar number, and pulmonary vascularization, with overmuscularization of the pulmonary arterial tree — the substrate for pulmonary hypoplasia and pulmonary hypertension.
- Associated anomalies occur in **40–60%** of liveborn infants and in **95%** of intrauterine demises. Cardiac anomalies in **40%** (most often ventricular and atrial septal defects); gastrointestinal anomalies second; chromosomal anomalies in **10–20%** (trisomy 21, 18, 13); syndromic etiology in **10%**. Increased first-trimester nuchal translucency is associated.
- CDH prognosis: spontaneous intrauterine demise in **2%**; survival among those who reach delivery with isolated CDH is **60–80%**. Long-term pulmonary, musculoskeletal, gastrointestinal, and neurodevelopmental complications occur in **20–30%**. Antenatal testing starts at **32–33 weeks**, earlier if severe. Steroids are given below **34 weeks**; steroids above 34 weeks have not improved outcomes.
- **CCAM** incidence is **1/11,000 to 1/35,000** live births; mid-trimester incidence is higher because many lesions resolve spontaneously. Both CCAM and BPS arise during the pseudoglandular phase (**7–17 weeks**) and may share a common origin; hybrid lesions exist.
- CCAM is usually isolated and sporadic; other anomalies occur in **15–20%** (most commonly cardiac and renal), except type 2, where about **60%** have associated anomalies. There is no known association with chromosomal abnormalities.
- Polyhydramnios, mediastinal shift, pleural effusion, and hydrops are more common with type 3 CCAM, in up to **40%**.
- Natural history: CCAM grows until about **25 weeks**, then may plateau or regress. Monitor every **1–3 weeks** until the lesion is stable, watching for hydrops in type 3.
- Antenatal intervention for hydrops includes thoracentesis, shunt, laser ablation or injection of the feeding artery, open fetal surgery before **32–34 weeks** for the poorest prognosis, and steroids when other interventions are unavailable.
- CCAM outcome: survival to delivery **>95%**, and approximately **100%** if there is no hydrops. At birth **50%** are asymptomatic; resection is immediate if there is respiratory compromise, and timing is controversial in stable infants. Lobectomy is preferred over segmentectomy for CCAM and ILS because of incomplete resection risk.
- **BPS** is rarer than CCAM. The defining feature is an anomalous arterial supply from the descending aorta, shown by color flow Doppler. Associated anomalies are more common than with CCAM: **40–50%** with ELS and **15%** with ILS. Gastrointestinal connections are the most common association and increase infection risk.

## Anti-patterns
- **Calling every cystic thoracic mass a CCAM.** Peristalsis, an absent abdominal stomach, or a liver herniation indicates CDH; an aortic feeder indicates sequestration.
- **Missing right-sided CDH.** Liver and lung have similar echogenicity, so the lesion may not appear as a discrete mass; look for leftward mediastinal shift and use Doppler for hepatic vessels.
- **Using cyst size to predict outcome.** Prognosis depends on pulmonary hypoplasia, hydrops, associated anomalies, and residual lung function; antenatal imaging cannot yet predict hypoplasia.
- **Assuming a mid-trimester CCAM will persist.** Many regress; mid-trimester incidence exceeds birth incidence.
- **Treating an extralobar sequestration like an intralobar lesion.** ELS has no malignant or infectious risk and can be observed; ILS is associated with hypoplasia.
- **Delivering a CDH infant outside a tertiary center.** Survival improves with prenatal diagnosis and birth at a facility with ECMO capability.
- **Giving antenatal steroids after 34 weeks for CDH.** No outcome benefit has been shown above 34 weeks.

## Key Takeaways
- Characterize thoracic masses by content and vascular supply: bowel/peristalsis = CDH, aortic feeder = BPS, otherwise CCAM by cyst size.
- Report liver herniation, side, and LHR/observed-to-expected LHR for every CDH, because these are the dominant prognostic variables.
- For CCAM, classify by type and cyst size, monitor every 1–3 weeks until stable, and watch for hydrops in type 3 lesions.
- Expect survival >95% for CCAM overall and near 100% without hydrops; counsel accordingly.
- For CDH, quote 60–80% survival for isolated lesions, 2% risk of intrauterine demise, and 20–30% long-term morbidity.
- Plan delivery at a tertiary center with pediatric surgery and ECMO for CDH and large lung lesions.
- Distinguish ELS from ILS and document the feeding vessel; management and prognosis differ.
