# Chapter 1: Human Basic Genetics and Patterns of Inheritance

## Core Idea

Clinical genetics has two layers. The first layer is the physical organisation of DNA, genes, and chromosomes. The second layer is the pattern by which a variant segregates through a family. The chapter argues that a structured pedigree plus one cytogenetic or molecular test classifies most inherited disease into a small number of transmission models. Each model yields a recurrence risk that the obstetrician can use directly in counselling. The chapter also shows where Mendelian rules fail: imprinting, mitochondrial heteroplasmy, dynamic repeats, and multifactorial threshold traits.

## Frameworks Introduced

**Mendelian transmission models.** Four patterns are defined by pedigree criteria: autosomal dominant (AD), autosomal recessive (AR), X-linked recessive, and X-linked dominant. Each has explicit diagnostic criteria, not just a risk figure.

**Cytogenetic classification.** Numeric abnormalities divide into aneuploidy (any number not a multiple of 23) and polyploidy (whole extra haploid sets). Structural abnormalities divide into deletion, duplication, inversion, ring chromosome, isochromosome, and translocation. Translocations are further split into Robertsonian and reciprocal, and each is described as balanced or unbalanced.

**Non-Mendelian mechanisms.** Four separate models are presented: mitochondrial inheritance with heteroplasmy and a threshold effect; genomic imprinting with parent-of-origin expression; dynamic mutations with premutation, expansion, and anticipation; and multifactorial/threshold inheritance.

**Testing hierarchy.** Karyotype, FISH, genomic array (aCGH), PCR, and sequencing are presented as complementary, with a defined role for each.

## Key Concepts

- Humans carry an estimated 20,000 to 25,000 protein-coding genes.
- Base pairing: A:T has two hydrogen bonds, G:C has three. Synthesis proceeds 5′ to 3′.
- X inactivation silences about 90% of genes on one X; about 10% remain active, many clustered on the short arm. The inactivation centre is at Xq13 and expresses XIST.
- Aneuploidy is the most frequent cytogenetic abnormality and occurs in 3% to 4% of clinically recognised pregnancies. Nondisjunction and anaphase lag are the mechanisms.
- Structural chromosome defects occur in about 0.25% of newborns.
- Triploidy means 69 chromosomes per cell. It usually follows fertilisation of one egg by two sperm.
- AD transmission: 50% risk per pregnancy, equal sex ratio, father-to-son transmission, no skipping. Retinoblastoma is only 80% penetrant, so 20% of gene carriers are unaffected. About 80% of achondroplasia cases are new mutations; the affected individual still transmits at 50%.
- AR transmission: 25% recurrence after an index case; parents usually unaffected; consanguinity and shared ethnicity are clues.
- X-linked recessive: an affected father has no affected sons and all daughters are carriers. A carrier mother has a 50% risk to each son.
- X-linked dominant: about twice as common in females. All daughters of an affected male inherit the trait.
- Mitochondrial DNA exists in 100 to 100,000 copies per cell. Heteroplasmy plus replicative segregation produces tissue-specific and age-dependent disease. Organs with high energy demand (muscle, brain) are most affected.
- Imprinting: about 70% of Prader-Willi cases follow deletion of paternal 15q11-q13; about 70% of Angelman cases follow deletion of the maternal copy. Uniparental disomy produces the same phenotypes.
- Dynamic mutations: CAG/polyglutamine expansions show dominant inheritance, late onset, and anticipation. Fragile X follows CGG expansion in the 5′ untranslated region of FMR1 with hypermethylation and loss of expression. GAA intronic expansion accounts for 98% of mutant FRDA alleles in Friedreich ataxia.
- Multifactorial traits show regression to the mean, variable heritability, and threshold behaviour.
- Neural tube defects: US incidence about 1 per 1000 births. Recurrence with no affected sib and unaffected parents is 0.3% for anencephaly/spina bifida; with one affected parent it is 4.5%; with both parents affected 30%.
- Cleft lip with or without cleft palate: 0.1% with no family history; 3.0% with one affected parent; 34% with both parents affected; 3.0% with one affected sib.
- Congenital heart disease affects 5 to 7 per 1000 live births. Sibling recurrence is about 4.3% for ventricular septal defect.
- Pyloric stenosis affects 1 to 5 per 1000 live births, with a 5:1 male excess. Male relatives of a female proband have 18.2 times population risk.
- Folate supplementation of cereals in Canada was followed by a 50% fall in neural tube defect incidence.

## Anti-patterns

- Using "identical" and "fraternal" as if they were genetic categories. Monozygotic twins can be phenotypically discordant and would be misclassified.
- Treating non-penetrance as absence of the allele. A phenotypically normal carrier of an AD gene still transmits at 50%.
- Assuming a new dominant mutation removes all risk. The affected child still has a 50% risk for offspring.
- Reading a single normal karyotype as exclusion of all structural disease. Microdeletions and microduplications need array testing.
- Assuming multifactorial recurrence risk applies to a different malformation. Each trait has its own empiric table.

## Key Takeaways

1. Construct a formal pedigree before quoting any risk. Transmission pattern determines the number.
2. Quote the empiric recurrence figure for the specific malformation, not a generic "multifactorial" estimate.
3. Use array-based testing when a karyotype is normal but a microdeletion syndrome is suspected.
4. Suspect imprinting when the same deletion produces two different phenotypes depending on parent of origin.
5. Suspect a mitochondrial disorder when disease is maternally transmitted, multisystem, and threshold-dependent.
6. Offer 4 mg folic acid before conception for a prior neural tube defect pregnancy, not the standard 0.4 mg.
