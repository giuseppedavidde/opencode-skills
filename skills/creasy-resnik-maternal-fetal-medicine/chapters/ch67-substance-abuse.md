# Chapter 67: Substance Abuse in Pregnancy

## Core Idea
Substance use in pregnancy is common, underreported, and treatable, but the treatment target differs by drug: abstinence for alcohol, tobacco, and stimulants, and harm reduction through opioid maintenance for opioid dependence. The chapter argues that universal structured self-report screening, not clinician impression or biochemical testing alone, is the only reliable detection method, because 24–50% of pregnant smokers deny use. It also reframes opioid maintenance as the standard of care, since withdrawal and untreated addiction produce worse maternal and neonatal outcomes than continued, well-dosed maintenance.

## Frameworks Introduced
**Universal screening framework.** Structured, self-reported screening at first prenatal visit using validated instruments: single-item alcohol question, CAGE, T-ACE (Tolerance, Annoyance, Cut down, Eye-opener), CAGE-AID (adapted to include drugs), 4Ps, and the Fagerström Test for Nicotine Dependence. Biochemical testing is an adjunct, not a substitute.

**Harm-reduction model for opioid dependence.** Methadone or buprenorphine maintenance with counseling, prenatal care coordination, and social support; dose to eliminate craving and illicit use rather than to achieve abstinence.

**Neonatal abstinence syndrome (NAS) assessment.** Finnegan scale scoring; onset usually day 2–3 of life; treatment with morphine or methadone, with phenobarbital as adjunct; taper ends after 24–48 hours free of withdrawal signs.

**Alcohol withdrawal management.** CIWA-Ar (Clinical Institute Withdrawal Assessment for Alcohol, Revised) to titrate benzodiazepines; thiamine and multivitamin supplementation; disulfiram, naltrexone, and acamprosate have limited pregnancy data.

## Key Concepts
- 16.3% of pregnant women report continued tobacco use. Smoking is most prevalent in women younger than 25 years, single women, low-income women, those with less than high school education, American Indian/Alaskan Native women, and non-Hispanic white women; up to 90% of opioid-dependent women smoke.
- 24–50% of pregnant women do not disclose smoking. Cotinine is the measured metabolite; secondhand exposure also raises cotinine.
- The average cigarette delivers 1–2 mg of nicotine; nicotine reaches the brain within 10 seconds. Cigarettes contain about 4,000 other substances, including carbon monoxide and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons.
- Smoking increases low birth weight, ectopic pregnancy, abruption, placenta previa, preterm premature rupture of membranes, neonatal death, and sudden infant death syndrome, plus maternal thrombosis and respiratory infection. Smoking reduces preeclampsia risk.
- Amniocytes from smokers showed 12% genetic instability versus 3.5% in nonsmokers, most often at 11q23. Suggested malformations include talipes, craniosynostosis, cleft lip/palate, urinary tract defects, cardiac defects, and limb reduction.
- Nicotine concentrations in breast milk are 1.5–3 times maternal plasma; the AAP no longer regards smoking as an absolute contraindication to breastfeeding. About 45% of pregnant smokers quit by the end of pregnancy.
- Bupropion produced 45% quitting versus 14% with placebo in a small randomized trial (P < .047).
- Alcohol: 10.8% of pregnant women aged 15–44 report current use, 3.7% binge drinking, and 1% heavy drinking; 10.1% report first-trimester binge drinking. Women older than 35, non-Hispanic, with more than high school education and higher income report the highest use in some states.
- CAGE: two affirmative answers give 77% sensitivity and 79% specificity for alcohol abuse or dependence, but only 53% sensitivity and 70% specificity for unhealthy use. T-ACE was designed for pregnancy.
- Stillbirth rises with alcohol dose: 1.37 per 1,000 women at less than one drink per week, 8.83 per 1,000 at five or more drinks per week (relative risk 2.96, 95% CI 1.37–6.41).
- Low birth weight, small for gestational age, and preterm birth do not increase clearly below about one drink per day but rise with heavier consumption.
- Breastfeeding with alcohol: no more than one drink, wait at least 2 hours; more than one drink requires 4–8 hours. Pumping and dumping does not remove alcohol.
- Alcohol withdrawal: severe withdrawal (seizures, delirium tremens) occurs in 2–5% of chronic alcoholics within 3 days of stopping and lasts 3–7 days; treated mortality is 1%.
- Marijuana is the most common illicit drug: 17.4 million past-month users in 2010, with use rising from 5.8% to 6.9% between 2007 and 2010. Current THC content is 10–15%. Urine detection is 1–3 days after single use and up to 30 days after chronic use.
- Marijuana exposure is associated with gastroschisis (odds ratio 3.0, 95% CI 1.3–6.8), but most cases had co-exposures. No malformation pattern is established; breastfeeding should be avoided because THC concentrates in milk.
- Neonatal abstinence syndrome rose from 1.20 to 3.39 per 1,000 hospital births between 2000 and 2009. Average cost per infant with NAS was $53,400 (95% CI $49,000–$57,700), totaling $720 million annually.
- NAS occurs in 60–90% of methadone-exposed infants. Average methadone maintenance dose is 80–120 mg/day; less than 60 mg/day is generally insufficient. Split dosing is sometimes used because of pregnancy physiology. NAS severity is not consistently related to methadone dose.
- Buprenorphine is a partial µ-agonist with a ceiling effect at 24–32 mg/day; buprenorphine monotherapy is preferred over buprenorphine-naloxone in pregnancy.
- Cocaine causes vasoconstriction, hypertensive emergency, and placental abruption. β-Blockade for cocaine-induced hypertension is contraindicated because of unopposed α-stimulation. Cocaine reduces birth weight by 492 g (95% CI −562 to −421). Breastfeeding is contraindicated. There is no pharmacologic treatment; management is psychosocial.
- Methamphetamine is detectable in urine for up to 3 days; it concentrates in breast milk at 2.8–7.5 times maternal plasma, so breastfeeding is not recommended. Exposed infants are 3.5 times more likely to be small for gestational age (95% CI 1.65–7.33). Malformation risk is not confirmed.
- Benzodiazepines are among the most prescribed drugs in pregnancy. Diazepam crosses the placenta and accumulates at 1–3 times maternal levels; it is not considered teratogenic. Prolonged exposure can cause NAS with hypertonia, irritability, abnormal sleep, tremulousness, bradycardia, cyanosis, poor sucking, apnea, diarrhea, and vomiting; phenobarbital is the treatment of choice.
- Concomitant benzodiazepine use in methadone-maintained women increases CNS depression and fatal overdose risk.

## Anti-patterns
- Relying on clinician impression or patient appearance instead of structured screening.
- Using a positive neonatal toxicology test to infer first- or second-trimester exposure; it reflects only recent exposure.
- Advising abrupt opioid withdrawal or detoxification in pregnancy instead of maintenance.
- Underdosing methadone below 60 mg/day and producing continued craving and illicit use.
- Treating cocaine-related hypertension with a β-blocker.
- Attributing all adverse outcomes to cocaine without accounting for tobacco, alcohol, and socioeconomic confounding.
- Permitting breastfeeding during active cocaine or methamphetamine use.
- Advising "pump and dump" as a method to clear alcohol from milk.

## Key Takeaways
1. Screen every pregnant patient with a structured tool (T-ACE, CAGE-AID, 4Ps, single-item alcohol question) and document the result; biochemical testing supplements but does not replace self-report.
2. Counsel complete alcohol abstinence — no safe threshold is established — and note the dose-dependent stillbirth risk (relative risk 2.96 at five or more drinks per week).
3. Treat opioid dependence with methadone 80–120 mg/day or buprenorphine up to 24–32 mg/day; do not detoxify.
4. Anticipate NAS in 60–90% of methadone-exposed infants, score with the Finnegan scale, and treat with opioids starting around day 2–3.
5. Avoid β-blockers in acute cocaine toxicity; use psychosocial treatment, and continue fetal surveillance only for obstetric indications.
6. Counsel that breastfeeding is compatible with smoking and most maintenance therapy but contraindicated with cocaine, methamphetamine, and active benzodiazepine misuse.
