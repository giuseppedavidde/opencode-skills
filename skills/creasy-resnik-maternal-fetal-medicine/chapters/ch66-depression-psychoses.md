# Chapter 66: Management of Depression and Psychoses in Pregnancy and in the Puerperium

## Core Idea
Pregnancy does not protect against mood or psychotic illness, and the postpartum period is the highest-risk window for severe relapse, especially in bipolar disorder and schizoaffective disorder. The chapter argues that the dominant clinical error is untreated maternal illness: relapse after abrupt medication discontinuation harms both mother and fetus more than most drug exposures. Treatment therefore starts with classifying the illness — unipolar depression, bipolar disorder, or chronic psychosis — because antidepressants can trigger mania and because antipsychotics plus mood stabilizers are required for psychotic illness. Decisions must be documented, individualized, and made before conception where possible.

## Frameworks Introduced
**Symptom-based diagnostic thresholds.** Major depressive episode: at least five symptoms, including low mood or diminished pleasure, for 2 weeks. Manic episode: at least three symptoms for at least 1 week. Schizophrenia: two symptoms for 4 weeks.

**Validated perinatal screening instruments.** Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (10 items with mood and anxiety subscales), Inventory of Depressive Symptomatology (self-report, clinician, and brief versions), and PRIME-MD depression module. Screening is only justified when intervention or referral capacity exists.

**Diagnosis-driven treatment algorithm.** Determine lifetime mania or psychosis first → unipolar MDD: antidepressant or psychotherapy, plus antipsychotic if psychotic features → bipolar: mood stabilizer or antipsychotic with mood-stabilizing properties, avoid antidepressant monotherapy → schizophrenia/schizoaffective disorder: antipsychotic, plus mood stabilizer in schizoaffective disorder.

**Medication selection across trimesters.** Consider stopping or switching teratogens before conception; if lithium or valproate is essential, use the lowest effective dose and reinstate after the first trimester where possible; withhold lithium about 24 hours before delivery.

**Postpartum prophylaxis framework.** Women with prior postpartum psychosis or bipolar disorder are at high relapse risk after birth despite ongoing treatment; lithium prophylaxis is effective in women with prior postpartum psychosis, particularly those without a bipolar diagnosis.

## Key Concepts
- Approximately one in five women experiences major depressive disorder in her lifetime. Lifetime prevalence in the chapter's table is 21%. Chronic psychotic disorders affect about 1% of women; bipolar disorder about 1%.
- Best estimate of major depressive disorder at any point in pregnancy is 12.7% (95% CI 7.1–20.4); during the 3 months after delivery it is 7.1% (95% CI 4.1–11.7). Overlapping confidence intervals mean pregnancy and postpartum risk may not differ.
- In about half of postpartum depressions, onset is after delivery.
- Depression is associated with preterm birth (relative risk 1.10, 95% CI 1.04–1.17) and, in a subset of 11 studies, with low birth weight.
- Schizophrenia is associated with a 1.19–1.79 relative risk of preterm birth and a 57% higher risk of low birth weight; placental abnormalities and congenital defects are also reported.
- Antidepressant use in early pregnancy is associated with a 45% higher risk of miscarriage (95% CI 1.19–1.77); absolute risks 12.4% exposed versus 8.7% unexposed. Risk is highest with multiple antidepressants; paroxetine and venlafaxine show significant effects.
- Valproate causes neural tube defects in 5–9% of exposed offspring, with the critical window 17–30 days after conception; lumbosacral defects predominate. It also causes heart valve defects, cleft palate, hypospadias, polydactyly, growth restriction, neonatal irritability, jitteriness, feeding difficulty, abnormal tone, hypoglycemia, and reduced fibrinogen.
- Lithium: the early International Registry of Lithium Babies suggested a high Ebstein anomaly rate, probably inflated by ascertainment bias; prospective studies report malformation rates of about 2%. Neonatal risks include growth reduction, acute lithium toxicity, neuromuscular complications, respiratory difficulty, cardiac arrhythmias, and renal and hepatic dysfunction; risk rises with maternal dose.
- SSRI malformation signals: anencephaly OR 2.4 (95% CI 1.1–5.1), craniosynostosis OR 2.5 (1.5–4.0), omphalocele OR 2.8 (1.3–5.7). Sertraline: omphalocele OR 5.7 (1.6–20.7), septal defects OR 2.0 (1.2–4.0). Most experts regard SSRIs as not major teratogens.
- Persistent pulmonary hypertension of the newborn: SSRI exposure after 20 weeks carries a sixfold relative risk; absolute risk 6–12 per 1,000 exposed versus 1–2 per 1,000 unexposed, with later replications around 3 per 1,000. Exposure before 20 weeks and non-SSRI antidepressants do not increase risk.
- Neonatal adaptation syndrome: jitteriness, irritability, and rarely convulsions in 20–30% of infants exposed to TCAs or SSRIs; resolves within days to 2 weeks. Anticholinergic effects such as functional bowel obstruction and urinary retention may occur with TCAs.
- Antidepressant exposure is associated with gestation shortened by 1 week or less.
- Neurodevelopmental studies of TCA, SSRI, and lithium exposure have not shown effects on global IQ, language, or behavior, but samples are small.
- Antipsychotics may cause neonatal hypertonicity, motor restlessness, tremor, and feeding difficulty.
- Lactation: psychotropic drugs are alkaline and lipid soluble and enter milk; excretion is modest except for lithium, which equilibrates well in breast milk. Avoid lithium and clozapine (agranulocytosis risk) during breastfeeding. Infants under 2 months and those exposed in utero achieve higher drug levels.
- Monitor breastfed infants for irritability, poor feeding, difficulty with arousal, muscle rigidity, tremor, fever, and poor weight gain; consider a trial off breast milk if these occur.
- Lamotrigine must be titrated from about 25 mg daily to 200–300 mg daily, so it cannot replace a stopped mood stabilizer acutely.

## Anti-patterns
- Stopping maintenance psychotropic medication at conception without a relapse-prevention plan.
- Treating bipolar depression with antidepressant monotherapy, which can precipitate mania.
- Using the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale as a diagnostic test rather than a screening tool, or screening without capacity to treat.
- Assuming pregnancy is protective and missing new-onset psychosis or depression.
- Overweighting the Ebstein anomaly signal for lithium while ignoring the relapse risk of untreated bipolar disorder.
- Prescribing valproate for bipolar disorder in a woman of childbearing potential without documented justification and contraception counseling.
- Continuing lithium through delivery without dose adjustment or a planned 24-hour hold.
- Breastfeeding on lithium or clozapine without specialist input.

## Key Takeaways
1. Screen with the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (10 items) or the Inventory of Depressive Symptomatology, and confirm diagnosis with standard criteria: 5 symptoms for 2 weeks for depression, 3 for 1 week for mania.
2. Establish lifetime mania or psychosis before prescribing: bipolar disorder needs a mood stabilizer or antipsychotic, not antidepressant monotherapy.
3. Expect relapse after delivery in bipolar and postpartum psychosis; arrange psychiatric co-management and consider lithium prophylaxis.
4. Avoid valproate in pregnancy (5–9% neural tube defects); if lithium is essential, use the lowest effective dose, monitor levels, and hold about 24 hours before delivery.
5. Counsel that SSRIs are not major teratogens but carry a small absolute risk of persistent pulmonary hypertension (about 3–12 per 1,000 exposed) and transient neonatal adaptation symptoms resolving by 2 weeks.
6. Breastfeeding is compatible with most psychotropics except lithium and clozapine; monitor the infant for sedation, poor feeding, tremor, and poor weight gain.
