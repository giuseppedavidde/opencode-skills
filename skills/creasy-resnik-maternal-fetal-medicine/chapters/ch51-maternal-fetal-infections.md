# Chapter 51: Maternal and Fetal Infections

## Core Idea

Infectious disease is the most common problem the obstetrician encounters. The chapter reviews the 29 most common infections, separating those that threaten mainly the mother (urinary tract infection, endometritis, mastitis), those that threaten the fetus or neonate (group B streptococcus, herpes simplex, rubella, cytomegalovirus, toxoplasmosis), and those that threaten both (human immunodeficiency virus, syphilis). Each disease is approached through epidemiology, pathogenesis, diagnosis, and treatment. Prevention is weighted toward intrapartum prophylaxis, serologic screening, and avoidance counseling.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Group B streptococcal intrapartum antibiotic prophylaxis (CDC 1996, revised 2002 and 2010).** Universal rectovaginal culture at 35 to 37 weeks, with prophylaxis in labor or after rupture of membranes. Prophylaxis is also given for GBS bacteriuria at any concentration and for a prior infant with GBS disease, without repeat screening. If culture results are unknown, prophylaxis is given for delivery before 37 weeks, rupture of membranes of 18 hours or more, or temperature of 38.0°C or higher. No prophylaxis is needed for cesarean delivery before labor and before membrane rupture. Penicillin G is first-line: 5 million units intravenously, then 2.5 to 3.0 million units every 4 hours until delivery. Ampicillin 2 g then 1 g every 4 hours is an alternative.
- **Penicillin-allergy algorithm for GBS.** Cefazolin 2 g then 1 g every 8 hours if the patient is not at high risk for anaphylaxis; vancomycin if at high risk. Clindamycin is no longer reliable without susceptibility testing.
- **Syphilis staging and treatment.** Primary, secondary, and early latent syphilis: benzathine penicillin G 2.4 million units intramuscularly as a single dose. Late latent or unknown duration: 2.4 million units weekly for 3 weeks. Penicillin allergy requires desensitization, because penicillin is the only therapy with documented efficacy; oral desensitization takes about 4 hours in a hospital setting.
- **Congenital infection diagnosis by amniotic fluid.** For cytomegalovirus and toxoplasmosis, amniotic fluid culture or PCR is more sensitive than cordocentesis, which is limited before 20 weeks and by delayed fetal IgM production.
- **TORCH-style serologic interpretation.** Acute infection is supported by IgM, a very high IgG titer, IgG seroconversion, or a fourfold IgG change over 2 weeks; IgG avidity testing helps date infection.
- **Suppressive antiviral therapy for recurrent genital herpes.** Acyclovir 400 mg three times daily from 36 weeks until delivery.
- **Hepatitis B immunoprophylaxis.** Passive immunization plus vaccine for the newborn of a surface-antigen-positive mother.

## Key Concepts

- Vulvovaginal candidiasis: 75% of women have at least one episode; 40% to 45% have two or more; Candida albicans causes more than 90%; symptomatic infection affects 15% of pregnant women.
- Bacterial vaginosis: metronidazole 500 mg orally twice daily for 7 days, or clindamycin 300 mg orally twice daily for 7 days. Screen and treat only high-risk women with a prior preterm birth; routine screening of low-risk women is not recommended.
- Asymptomatic bacteriuria: without screening and treatment, up to 40% progress to acute pyelonephritis. Pyelonephritis complicates 1% to 2.5% of pregnancies (14 per 1000 deliveries in one cohort) and recurs in 10% to 18%, or up to 60% without suppression. Prophylaxis for three or more UTIs in 12 months: nitrofurantoin or trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole double strength, one tablet daily.
- Chorioamnionitis: clinical disease in 0.5% to 10% of pregnancies, histologic disease in up to 20% of term and more than half of preterm deliveries. Bacteremia occurs in about 10%. Amniotic fluid glucose below 5 mg/dL predicts a positive culture in about 90%. Treatment is intravenous antibiotics plus delivery; the odds ratio for neonatal sepsis is 25.
- GBS: rectovaginal colonization in 20% to 30% of pregnant women; without intervention more than half of infants become colonized, but sepsis develops in only 1%. Early-onset disease appears within the first week (usually 48 hours), with bacteremia in 89% and meningitis in 10%; case fatality is 4.5% early and 2% late, with up to 50% neurologic sequelae after late-onset meningitis.
- Cytomegalovirus: 5% to 15% of infants infected after primary maternal infection are symptomatic at birth; about 30% of severely affected infants die and 80% of survivors have major morbidity. Among the 85% to 90% asymptomatic at birth, 10% to 15% later develop hearing loss, chorioretinitis, or dental defects. Recurrent maternal infection transmits to 5% to 10% of infants, and few are symptomatic.
- Toxoplasmosis: about 50% of US adults are seropositive; seroconversion occurs in about 5% of pregnancies; congenital infection occurs in about 3 per 1000 infants and clinically significant disease in 1 per 8000 pregnancies. Amniotic fluid PCR has 64% sensitivity and 100% positive predictive value. About 40% of neonates born to mothers with acute infection are infected.
- Herpes simplex: HSV-2 seroprevalence is 21.9% in people aged 12 years or older. Primary lesions last 3 to 6 weeks; about 50% recur within months. In one cohort, cesarean delivery reduced neonatal infection from 7.7% to 1.2%.
- Varicella: 1 to 5 cases per 10,000 pregnancies; incubation 10 to 14 days; 20% of adults develop pneumonia and 1% encephalitis. The neonatal risk window is maternal rash from 5 days before to 2 days after delivery; up to 30% of infected neonates develop complications.
- Syphilis: transmission occurs in 50% to 60% of sexual exposures; incubation averages 21 days. Congenital syphilis fell from 3850 cases in 1992 to 451 in 2005.
- Hepatitis B: acute infection in 1 to 2 per 1000 pregnancies and chronic carriage in 6 to 10 per 1000; about 90% of adults clear the virus and about 10% become chronic carriers.

## Anti-patterns

- Relying on clinical signs alone to diagnose genital herpes; classic vesicles are often absent and clinical sensitivity is low.
- Using erythromycin for GBS prophylaxis; resistance is 25% to 30%.
- Treating GBS bacteriuria as harmless colonization instead of giving intrapartum prophylaxis.
- Assuming a negative culture more than 4 weeks old still excludes GBS in preterm labor.
- Using cordocentesis rather than amniotic fluid PCR for congenital infection diagnosis.
- Prescribing pyrimethamine in the first trimester because of teratogenicity.
- Missing listeriosis in late second or early third trimester fever; blood cultures are required.
- Delaying antibiotics in suspected chorioamnionitis while awaiting culture results.

## Key Takeaways

1. Screen every pregnant woman for GBS at 35 to 37 weeks and treat with penicillin G in labor when indicated.
2. Screen all pregnant women for syphilis; treat with benzathine penicillin G and desensitize if allergic.
3. Use amniotic fluid PCR for suspected cytomegalovirus or toxoplasmosis after 21 weeks rather than cord blood.
4. Start acyclovir suppression at 36 weeks for recurrent genital herpes, and offer cesarean delivery for active lesions or prodromal symptoms.
5. Treat asymptomatic bacteriuria to prevent pyelonephritis, and give suppressive prophylaxis for recurrent infection.
6. Vaccinate susceptible women against varicella and influenza, and give hepatitis B immunoglobulin plus vaccine to newborns of carrier mothers.
7. Treat suspected chorioamnionitis with immediate intravenous antibiotics and delivery.
