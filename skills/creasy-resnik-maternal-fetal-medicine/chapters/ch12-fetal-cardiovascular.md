# Chapter 12: Fetal Cardiovascular Physiology

## Core Idea

The fetal circulation runs in parallel, not in series. Gas exchange occurs in the placenta, the lungs are non-functional for gas exchange, and three shunts plus preferential streaming keep the most oxygenated blood directed to the brain and myocardium. The right ventricle ejects about two thirds of the combined ventricular output and the left ventricle about one third. The chapter's argument is that fetal cardiovascular function cannot be inferred from adult values, because pressure, output, oxygen content, and substrate use are all organised differently.

## Frameworks Introduced

**Parallel circulation with shunt model.** Shunts are the ductus venosus, the foramen ovale, and the ductus arteriosus. Streaming, not complete mixing, determines oxygen delivery.

**Combined ventricular output framework.** Because the ventricles eject in parallel, cardiac output is expressed as the sum of right and left ventricular output, not as a single ventricular output.

**Streaming model.** Highly saturated umbilical venous blood passes through the ductus venosus and left hepatic vein into the thoracic inferior vena cava, then crosses the foramen ovale into the left atrium. Desaturated superior vena caval and coronary sinus blood is directed across the tricuspid valve to the right ventricle and then to the placenta.

**Myocardial function framework.** Output is set by preload, afterload, contractility, and heart rate. The fetal heart operates near the top of its curve.

## Key Concepts

- About 40% of combined ventricular output, roughly 200 mL/kg/min, goes to the placenta.
- Umbilical venous blood supplies 75% to 80% of total liver blood flow; portal vein supplies about 15%; hepatic artery supplies 4% to 5%.
- About 50% of umbilical blood flow passes through the ductus venosus; the remainder enters the hepatic-portal system.
- The left hepatic lobe receives more than 95% of its supply from umbilical venous blood. The right lobe receives about 60% umbilical and 30% portal blood.
- Thoracic inferior vena caval flow is 65% to 70% of venous return to the heart. The ductus venosus contributes about one third of that flow.
- About 40% of inferior vena caval return crosses the foramen ovale, equal to about 27% of combined ventricular output.
- Pulmonary venous return is about 8% of combined ventricular output.
- Left ventricular output is about 35% of combined ventricular output; right ventricular output is about 65%.
- Combined ventricular output in fetal lambs is about 450 mL/kg/min. Right ventricular output is about 300 mL/kg/min; left ventricular output is about 150 mL/kg/min.
- Of right ventricular output, 8% goes to the lungs and 57% crosses the ductus arteriosus to the descending aorta.
- About 21% of combined ventricular output reaches the head, upper limbs, and upper thorax. About 10% crosses the aortic isthmus.
- Pulmonary blood flow rises from about 4% to 8% of combined ventricular output between 125 and 130 days in the lamb, about 0.85 of term.
- Organ blood flow (mL/100 g/min): heart 180, brain 125, kidneys 150, adrenals and spleen 200, lungs 100, gut 70, upper and lower body 25.
- Umbilical vein values: pH 7.40 to 7.43, PO₂ 28 to 32 mm Hg, PCO₂ 38 to 42 mm Hg.
- Descending aorta values: pH 7.36 to 7.39, PO₂ 20 to 23 mm Hg, PCO₂ 43 to 48 mm Hg.
- Ascending aorta values: pH 7.37 to 7.40, PO₂ 21 to 25 mm Hg, PCO₂ 41 to 45 mm Hg.
- Left hepatic venous saturation is 80% to 85%; right hepatic venous saturation is about 35%. Coronary sinus saturation is about 20%. Ascending aortic saturation is about 60%.
- Umbilical venous pressure is 3 to 5 mm Hg above inferior vena caval pressure. Right atrial pressure exceeds left atrial pressure. Pulmonary trunk and right ventricular systolic pressures are 1 to 2 mm Hg above aortic and left ventricular pressures.
- Resting fetal heart rate is about 180 beats/min. Raising it to 250 to 300 beats/min increases cardiac output by 15% to 20%. Bradycardia reduces output substantially.
- At an atrial filling pressure above about 8 mm Hg, output reaches a plateau.
- A 15 mm Hg rise in arterial pressure reduces cardiac output by 25% to 30%.
- Fetal myocardial blood flow is about 180 mL/min per 100 g, roughly 80% above adult values. Myocardial oxygen consumption is about 400 µM/min per 100 g.
- Fetal myocardial substrate use is 33% glucose, 6% pyruvate, and 58% lactate. Free fatty acid concentrations are very low.
- Ductus arteriosus patency is maintained mainly by prostaglandins, with PGE₂ acting through EP₄ and other EP receptors. Nitric oxide contributes. Indomethacin constriction is most pronounced beyond 30 weeks in humans.
- In-utero ductus constriction causes ischemic hypoxia, increased nitric oxide production, and smooth muscle cell death, which makes the ductus resistant to postnatal constriction.

## Anti-patterns

- Assuming the two ventricles eject equal volumes. The right ventricle ejects about twice the left.
- Assuming low pulmonary blood flow means no pulmonary flow. The fetal lungs receive about 8% of combined ventricular output.
- Applying adult arterial pressure or oxygen targets to the fetus.
- Treating ductal constriction from indomethacin as a benign finding. It can produce persistent pulmonary hypertension and a ductus that will not close.
- Interpreting fetal bradycardia as a pure vagal reflex without considering afterload and myocardial immaturity.
- Using adult myocardial substrate physiology. The fetal heart runs mainly on lactate and glucose.

## Key Takeaways

1. Think in combined ventricular output, not in a single ventricular output.
2. Trace oxygen delivery through the ductus venosus, foramen ovale, and ductus arteriosus in that order.
3. Use the fetal blood gas ranges, not adult ranges, when interpreting cord or fetal samples.
4. Avoid indomethacin beyond 30 weeks when ductal constriction would be poorly tolerated.
5. Expect the fetal heart to be rate-dependent and afterload-sensitive.
6. Remember that most fetal myocardial energy comes from lactate and glucose, not free fatty acids.
