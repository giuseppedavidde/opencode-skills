# Chapter 56: Malignancy and Pregnancy

## Core Idea

About 1 in 1000 pregnant women is diagnosed with a new malignancy each year, and the number is rising as childbearing shifts to older ages. Diagnosis is frequently delayed because pregnancy symptoms and breast and cervical changes mimic disease. Management must pursue curative maternal treatment while limiting fetal harm, which requires a multidisciplinary team and a stage- and gestational-age-specific plan. More than half of pregnancy-associated cancers are diagnosed within the first year after delivery and more than a quarter in the antenatal period. Surgery is preferred when it is definitive; chemotherapy is avoided in the first trimester and can generally be given in the second and third.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Incidence framework (Table 56-1).** Approximate rates per pregnancy: melanoma 1 in 1000, cervical cancer 1 in 2000, Hodgkin lymphoma 1 in 3000, breast cancer 1 in 3000, ovarian cancer 1 in 18,000, leukemia 1 in 75,000. Ranges are wide and should be quoted as ranges.
- **Breast cancer treatment algorithm by gestational age (Figure 56-1).** Before 12 to 14 weeks with operable disease: modified radical mastectomy is preferred, with adjuvant chemotherapy after the first trimester. At 12 to 34 weeks with operable disease: surgery then adjuvant chemotherapy. Inoperable disease before 12 to 14 weeks: wait until the second trimester or give primary chemotherapy. Inoperable disease at 12 to 34 weeks: primary chemotherapy. Plan delivery about 2 weeks after the last chemotherapy cycle and consider delivery after 35 weeks. Give radiotherapy and endocrine therapy after delivery.
- **Chemotherapy timing rules.** All cytotoxic agents are pregnancy category D. Exposure within 2 weeks of conception causes spontaneous abortion or no effect. The first trimester, during organogenesis, carries the highest risk of congenital malformation. Second and third trimester exposure increases growth restriction, low birth weight, spontaneous abortion, and preterm labor. Chemotherapy near delivery risks maternal myelosuppression and neonatal toxicity because the placenta no longer excretes the drug.
- **Hodgkin lymphoma staging in pregnancy.** Ann Arbor staging with computed tomography of the chest and abdominal shielding plus abdominal magnetic resonance imaging. Avoid abdominal and pelvic computed tomography and PET during pregnancy. Bone marrow biopsy is safe.
- **Cervical dysplasia management.** Bethesda cytology with colposcopy; endocervical curettage is avoided and an endocervical brush is used. Biopsy only suspicious areas. Treatment of dysplasia is deferred until postpartum, when about 70% of CIN 2 and CIN 3 lesions have regressed. Loop electrosurgical excision or cone biopsy is reserved to exclude invasion and is safest in the mid-second trimester.
- **Radiation dose framework.** One gray equals 1000 mGy or 100 rad. Diagnostic fetal doses are below 1 mGy (0.1 cGy). Therapeutic pelvic radiation for cervical cancer delivers more than 4000 cGy to the fetus. Abdominal shielding limits mantle-field fetal exposure to about 0.1 Gy (10 rad).

## Key Concepts

- Cancer is the leading cause of death in women aged 35 to 54 years and a leading nonaccidental cause of death at ages 15 to 34. The commonest malignancies at ages 15 to 34 are melanoma, breast cancer, leukemia, cervical cancer, central nervous system tumors, and non-Hodgkin lymphoma.
- Ovarian cancer complicates 1 in 18,000 pregnancies; about 1 in 1000 pregnant women undergoes surgery for an adnexal mass, and only 1% to 3% of those masses are malignant. Germ cell tumors are the commonest ovarian malignancy in pregnancy, and up to 30% are dysgerminomas.
- Gestational trophoblastic neoplasia includes partial and complete moles, choriocarcinoma, and placental-site trophoblastic tumor. Molar pregnancy occurs in 1 per 1000 US pregnancies. Transvaginal ultrasound detects an intrauterine pregnancy at a beta-hCG of 1500 to 2000 IU/mL; transabdominal ultrasound requires about 6500 IU/mL.
- Pregnancy-associated breast cancer incidence is 1.3 to 3.3 per 10,000 live births; 0.2% to 3.8% of breast cancers in women under 50 are pregnancy-associated, and up to 20% are diagnosed during pregnancy.
- Diagnostic delay in pregnancy-associated breast cancer averages 2 months. Each month of delay in treating the primary tumor increases the risk of metastases by 0.9% to 1.8%.
- Sentinel lymph node mapping is not recommended in pregnancy outside a clinical trial, and isosulphan blue dye should not be used.
- Women treated for breast cancer should delay pregnancy 2 to 3 years, because most recurrences occur within the first 2 years.
- Melanoma: surgery is the only effective cure, and pregnancy is no reason to delay excision. Interferon, ipilimumab, and vemurafenib are category C or D and are not recommended.
- Hodgkin lymphoma: ABVD may be given in the first trimester only after counseling when rapidly progressive disease threatens the mother. Antimetabolites (methotrexate, fludarabine, cytarabine) and alkylating agents (busulfan, chlorambucil, cyclophosphamide, ifosfamide) are avoided in the first trimester.
- Surgery during pregnancy is performed in fewer than 2% of women. The second trimester is preferred. Laparoscopy is feasible with open pneumoperitoneum entry, left lateral tilt, and limited uterine manipulation.
- Ovarian failure after radiation: 600 cGy can induce menopause at age 40; 2000 cGy fractionated over 5 to 6 weeks causes permanent sterility in adolescents. Ovarian transposition can preserve function.

## Anti-patterns

- Attributing a breast mass or unilateral breast refusal by an infant to lactational change instead of imaging and biopsy.
- Delaying melanoma excision because of pregnancy.
- Giving abdominal or pelvic computed tomography or PET for lymphoma staging during pregnancy.
- Starting cytotoxic chemotherapy in the first trimester for a cancer that can safely be deferred.
- Delivering immediately after chemotherapy without allowing drug clearance and marrow recovery.
- Performing conization in the first trimester for dysplasia instead of excluding invasion and deferring treatment.
- Using isosulphan blue or methylene blue for sentinel node mapping in pregnancy.
- Advising termination by default; abortion is usually not required for cancer treatment.

## Key Takeaways

1. Assemble a multidisciplinary team (obstetrics, maternal-fetal medicine, oncology, neonatology) at diagnosis and document a gestational-age-specific plan.
2. Biopsy any discrete breast mass; use ultrasound-guided core biopsy and shield the abdomen for mammography.
3. Prefer surgery in the first trimester when it is definitive; start chemotherapy after the first trimester.
4. Avoid chemotherapy within 3 weeks of delivery, and plan delivery about 2 weeks after the last cycle.
5. Stage lymphoma with chest computed tomography plus abdominal MRI, not abdominal computed tomography or PET.
6. Manage cervical dysplasia conservatively during pregnancy and re-evaluate at about 6 weeks postpartum, reserving excision for suspected invasion.
7. Delay pregnancy 2 to 3 years after breast cancer treatment and counsel about recurrence surveillance.
