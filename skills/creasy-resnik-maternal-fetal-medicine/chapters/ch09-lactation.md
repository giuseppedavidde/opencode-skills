# Chapter 9: The Breast and the Physiology of Lactation

## Core Idea

Lactation is the final stage of the reproductive cycle and is prepared long before delivery. The breast is ready for full lactation by 16 weeks of gestation. Delivery removes progesterone and permits lactogenesis stage II, and prolactin then drives synthesis while oxytocin drives ejection. The chapter argues that milk production is a supply-and-demand system with local feedback inhibition, so effective milk removal in the first 3 days determines the subsequent course. Most breastfeeding failure is a mechanical or support problem, not a glandular one.

## Frameworks Introduced

**Five-stage mammary development model.** Embryogenesis, mammogenesis, lactogenesis, lactation, and involution. Each stage has its own hormonal control and local factors.

**Lactogenesis staging.** Stage I begins in mid-pregnancy and produces colostrum. Stage II begins at parturition and produces transitional milk. Stage III is established lactation.

**Ejection reflex arc.** Nipple mechanoreceptors send afferent signals to the hypothalamus, which triggers posterior pituitary oxytocin release. Oxytocin contracts myoepithelial cells and ejects milk.

**Feedback inhibitor of lactation (FIL) model.** Local factors in accumulated milk inhibit further synthesis, so stasis reduces supply.

**Breastfeeding intensity classification.** Exclusive, almost exclusive, high partial (at least 80% human milk), medium partial (20% to 80%), low partial (less than 20%), token, and none.

## Key Concepts

- The breast is prepared for full lactation after 16 weeks of gestation.
- Lactogenesis stage I occurs in mid-pregnancy; stage II occurs at parturition.
- Milk volume rises from about 100 mL in the first 24 hours to 500 to 750 mL/day by day 4 or 5, then settles at 600 to 700 mL/day by day 8.
- Secretory IgA and lactoferrin make up 10% by weight of milk produced in the first 48 hours.
- If milk removal does not begin by 72 hours, the compositional changes of lactogenesis reverse.
- Colostrum is rich in sodium, chloride, immunoglobulins, and lactoferrin, with little lactose and no casein.
- Maternal prolactin is about 125 to 180 ng/mL in the first trimester, 200 to 250 ng/mL after delivery, and falls below 25 ng/mL over 3 to 4 weeks in non-breastfeeding mothers.
- Prolactin is released by suckling or pumping, not by the sight, sound, or smell of the infant. Oxytocin can be released by those cues.
- Pain and stress inhibit oxytocin release and therefore the ejection reflex.
- The fetus sucks in utero from about 14 weeks and receives about 1 g of protein per kg per day from amniotic fluid.
- Rooting, sucking, and coordinated swallowing are present at 28 weeks. Coordinated suck and swallow with a bottle appears at 34 weeks.
- Initiation within half an hour of birth is recommended.
- Breast size does not predict milk production. Periareolar augmentation incisions may interrupt nerves and ducts.
- Mastitis is sudden in onset, usually after 10 days, unilateral, with localized redness and fever above 38.4°C. Continue breastfeeding on both breasts and give antibiotics for at least 10 days, preferably 14.
- Engorgement is bilateral and gradual, with generalized swelling and no fever. A plugged duct is unilateral, gradual, and afebrile.
- Most drugs appear in milk at less than 1% of the maternal dose. Most have a milk-to-plasma ratio below 1.
- Drug transfer is highest during the colostral phase, when alveolar epithelium is most permeable.
- Progestin-only contraception is started at 6 weeks postpartum in exclusive breastfeeding and at 3 weeks if formula supplements are used. Combined estrogen-progestin pills are avoided until at least 6 weeks.
- Contraindications to breastfeeding include galactosemia, active untreated tuberculosis, HIV infection, Ebola or Marburg virus, and Lassa fever.
- Lactation suppresses ovulation but is not a reliable sole contraceptive.

## Anti-patterns

- Stopping breastfeeding during mastitis. Drainage is part of treatment.
- Diagnosing low milk supply from soft breasts. Once supply and demand equilibrate, the breast is soft.
- Assuming small breasts predict failure. Glandular capacity is not predicted by size.
- Withholding breastfeeding for most maternal infections. Transmission risk is usually lower than the benefit lost.
- Starting combined oral contraceptives early in lactation. They reduce milk quantity and quality and add thrombotic risk.
- Attributing sore nipples to feeding frequency. Inadequate latch is the usual cause.

## Key Takeaways

1. Establish effective milk removal within 72 hours to protect the lactational transition.
2. Treat mastitis with continued drainage plus 10 to 14 days of antibiotics.
3. Use prolactin physiology to explain supply, and oxytocin physiology to explain let-down.
4. Choose progestin-only contraception at 6 weeks in exclusive breastfeeding.
5. Avoid combined estrogen-progestin pills until lactation is established, at least 6 weeks.
6. Screen for breast surgery history and prior lactation failure before delivery.
