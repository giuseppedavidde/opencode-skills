# Chapter 24: Abdominal Imaging

## Core Idea
Abdominal imaging separates four kinds of problem: free fluid (ascites), a cystic mass, an echogenic focus, and a defect of the abdominal wall. The wall defect is the highest-stakes distinction, because omphalocele and gastroschisis differ in membrane, cord insertion, karyotype risk, and prognosis. Throughout the chapter, the diagnostic method is the same: define the anatomy, then search the rest of the fetus for associated anomalies, because associated malformations — not the abdominal finding itself — determine outcome.

## Frameworks Introduced
**Gastroschisis versus omphalocele discrimination.**
- Gastroschisis: full-thickness paraumbilical defect, typically to the **right** of the cord insertion, with bowel floating freely and no membrane; a "cauliflower" appearance; maternal serum AFP elevated; about **85%** isolated; aneuploidy not typical, so amniocentesis is unwarranted for isolated disease.
- Omphalocele: midline ventral defect at the base of the umbilicus, with bowel or liver covered by a membrane of peritoneum and amnion; the umbilical cord passes through the mass and inserts on the membranes — the key feature even when the sac has ruptured; absence of liver in the sac (intracorporeal liver) is strongly associated with aneuploidy; AFP is not uniformly elevated.

**Ascites diagnostic algorithm.** Determine whether the fluid is transudate (capillary leak with inadequate lymphatic return), exudate (infection or malignancy), urine (overdistended or ruptured bladder), meconium (perforated bowel), or chyle (peritoneal lymphangiectasia). Then search for hydrops: scalp and skin edema, pleural effusion, pericardial effusion, polyhydramnios, and placentomegaly (>4 cm thick).

**Cystic abdominal mass differential.** Physiologic fluid in stomach, bladder, renal pelvis, and gallbladder versus renal (pelvicaliceal dilation, duplication, megacystis), bowel (obstruction, duplication cyst, meconium pseudocyst), hepatobiliary (hepatic, biliary, choledochal cyst), reproductive (ovarian cyst, hydrometrocolpos, cloaca), and other (splenic, pancreatic, urachal, mesenteric, omental cysts; adrenal hemorrhage; umbilical vein varix).

**Intestinal atresia framework.** Atresia is complete obstruction; stenosis is narrowing. Duodenal atresia arises from failure of revacuolization in the solid-core stage; jejunoileal and colonic atresia usually follow a vascular accident (volvulus, intussusception, internal hernia, strangulation in a wall defect).

## Key Concepts
- Hydrops fetalis occurs in approximately **1:2,500** births, and ascites is present in **85%** of nonimmune hydrops; placentomegaly is defined as thickness **>4 cm**.
- Isolated fetal ascites resolves in **30–75%** of cases. Prognosis is best with chylous ascites (**0% mortality**), poor when ascites is diagnosed before **24 weeks** (mortality approximately **75%**), and worst with metabolic storage disease (**100% mortality**).
- Cystic abdominal masses: final diagnosis may remain unknown in **25–50%** pending postnatal investigation. Functional ovarian cysts larger than **2 cm** occur in about **4/10,000** pregnancies and appear only in the second half of pregnancy; about **30%** of female fetuses are born with small follicular cysts. About **25%** of isolated fetal abdominal cysts are ovarian, **50%** of simple anechoic ovarian cysts resolve in utero, and the risk of malignancy in an antenatally diagnosed cyst is about **5%**. Cyst aspiration before delivery is controversial because of bleeding, seeding, preterm labor, and infection.
- Gastroschisis incidence is approximately **5/10,000** live births and is increasing. Risk factors are young maternal age, smoking, vasoactive drug use, and low socioeconomic status. Physiologic gut herniation occurs between the **6th and 10th weeks** and herniation persisting beyond **12 weeks** is probably gastroschisis.
- Gastroschisis: **85%** isolated; growth restriction and oligohydramnios are common; polyhydramnios suggests bowel atresia; nongastrointestinal anomalies occur in **<5%**. Bowel dilation is not a consistent prognostic sign or delivery indication.
- Gastroschisis outcome: survival **>90%** in uncomplicated cases; average **30 days** to achieve enteral feeds; necrotizing enterocolitis **4–10%**; central line infection up to **24%**; dysfunctional bowel **50%**; short bowel syndrome **5%**. Elective cesarean delivery confers no neonatal or long-term benefit.
- Intestinal atresias occur in **1–3/10,000** live births; colonic atresia is **<10%** of all atresias. Bowel loops greater than **7 mm** suggest obstruction, though normal colon diameter varies with gestation.
- Duodenal atresia produces the "double bubble" of dilated stomach and proximal duodenum, joined by a thin echolucent pylorus. Trisomy 21 is present in up to **30%**; nearly half of all intestinal atresias have an associated anomaly; polyhydramnios is typical and marked in the third trimester.
- Omphalocele incidence is **0.5–1/10,000** live births. Risk factors are maternal age **>35 years** and assisted reproductive technology. It results from defective lateral folding with failure of closure at the umbilical ring.
- Omphalocele: about **70%** have other malformations — cardiac up to **50%**, gastrointestinal atresia **40%**, plus renal and CNS anomalies. Up to **40%** have chromosomal abnormalities (trisomy 13, 18, 21, Turner, triploidy). Syndromes include Beckwith-Wiedemann, pentalogy of Cantrell, and OEIS.
- Omphalocele outcome: increased risk of in utero and neonatal demise regardless of defect size or liver position; survival with isolated omphalocele about **80%**, falling to **10–20%** with associated abnormalities. Cesarean delivery is recommended for a large (**>5 cm**) sac, particularly if liver-containing; smaller lesions may deliver vaginally. Primary closure is possible for defects **<5 cm**; larger defects need staged or silo closure, or "paint and wait" epithelialization.

## Anti-patterns
- **Confusing gastroschisis with a ruptured omphalocele.** The cord insertion onto the membranes identifies omphalocele; the defect's position right of the cord and free-floating bowel identify gastroschisis.
- **Ordering amniocentesis for isolated gastroschisis.** Aneuploidy is not typical; the 40% figure applies to omphalocele.
- **Assuming a small omphalocele is benign.** Risk of demise is increased regardless of size or liver position.
- **Treating ascites as an isolated finding.** It is a sign of hydrops, infection, urinary leak, meconium peritonitis, or anemia and requires a full anatomic and etiologic search.
- **Using bowel dilation alone to time delivery in gastroschisis.** Data are inconsistent; dilation is neither a reliable poor prognostic sign nor an indication for immediate delivery.
- **Missing aneuploidy risk when liver is not in the omphalocele sac.** Intracorporeal liver is strongly associated with chromosomal abnormality.
- **Ignoring the colon diameter nomogram.** A loop >7 mm is abnormal, but normal diameter changes with gestational age.

## Key Takeaways
- Distinguish gastroschisis from omphalocele by membrane and cord insertion before counseling; the karyotype risk differs by an order of magnitude.
- For omphalocele, obtain karyotype and fetal echocardiogram, and quote ~80% survival if isolated versus 10–20% with associated anomalies.
- For isolated gastroschisis, expect >90% survival, plan tertiary delivery, reserve cesarean for obstetric indications, and counsel about a ~30-day course to full feeds.
- Treat ascites as hydrops until proven otherwise; measure placental thickness (>4 cm is abnormal) and look for edema, effusions, and polyhydramnios.
- Apply the 24-week and etiology-based prognostic framework for isolated ascites: 30–75% resolve; mortality ~75% before 24 weeks; metabolic disease is fatal.
- Use the double bubble plus a thin pyloric connection for duodenal atresia, and offer karyotype because trisomy 21 occurs in up to 30%.
- Measure bowel loops against gestational age; >7 mm suggests obstruction, and a normal stomach with a massively dilated colon points to a distal lesion.
