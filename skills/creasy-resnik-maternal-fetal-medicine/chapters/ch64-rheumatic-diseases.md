# Chapter 64: Pregnancy and Rheumatic Diseases

## Core Idea
Rheumatic disease in pregnancy is a problem of attribution: pregnancy-induced thrombocytopenia, proteinuria, palmar erythema, and facial flushing all mimic a lupus flare, while preeclampsia mimics lupus nephritis. The chapter argues that outcomes are good when disease is quiescent at conception, and that withdrawal of maintenance therapy — especially hydroxychloroquine — is a common and avoidable cause of flare. The dominant fetal risks are not the maternal disease itself but autoantibody-mediated syndromes: antiphospholipid antibody–mediated placental insufficiency and anti-Ro/SSA–mediated congenital heart block.

## Frameworks Introduced
**Lupus flare versus preeclampsia.** Favor lupus nephritis with active SLE signs, rising anti-dsDNA, very low complement, and urinary red cell casts. Favor preeclampsia with rapid worsening over days, hypertension, hyperuricemia, thrombocytopenia, and normal complement.

**Antiphospholipid antibody (aPL) treatment stratification.** Stratify by antibody titer (high-titer IgG/IgM anticardiolipin >40 IU or positive lupus anticoagulant versus low titer) and obstetric history (primipara, one early loss, recurrent early loss, fetal loss after 10 weeks, prior preterm birth from preeclampsia or intrauterine growth restriction).

**Neonatal lupus risk framework.** Maternal anti-Ro/SSA or anti-La/SSB → fetal surveillance for heart block; risk of rash 25%, complete heart block <3%, and recurrence of either 18%.

**Rheumatic drug safety tiers.** Compatible: hydroxychloroquine, azathioprine, sulfasalazine, TNF-α inhibitors, low-dose prednisone, NSAIDs (avoid after 34 weeks). Contraindicated: cyclophosphamide, methotrexate, leflunomide, warfarin. Avoid in renal crisis: ACE inhibitors and receptor antagonists.

## Key Concepts
- Female-to-male ratios: SLE 9:1, APS 7:1, rheumatoid arthritis and scleroderma 3:1, Takayasu 3:1. Up to 1% of all women have rheumatoid arthritis.
- One third of SLE patients have anti-Ro/SSA or anti-La/SSB antibodies; one third to one half have antiphospholipid antibodies.
- Flare is most confidently diagnosed with new or worsening rash (not erythema alone), lymphadenopathy, arthritis, fever, or rising anti-dsDNA.
- Two thirds of pregnant lupus patients with preexisting renal disease develop preeclampsia, versus less than 20% of those without prior kidney disease.
- Prednisone and methylprednisolone are inactivated by placental hydroxylase and do not reach the fetus; dexamethasone and betamethasone do cross and are used only to treat the fetus.
- NSAIDs may cause oligohydramnios, fetal renal injury, or premature ductus arteriosus closure, particularly after 34 weeks.
- Azathioprine is relatively safe (large transplant experience), though animal teratogenicity exists. Cyclophosphamide, methotrexate, and leflunomide are contraindicated in early pregnancy. TNF-α inhibitors (etanercept, infliximab, adalimumab) are rated relatively safe.
- Pulse methylprednisolone 1,000 mg IV on 3 consecutive days has unknown safety in pregnancy.
- Untreated APS with prior fetal loss: more than 80% fetal survival with treatment today, versus less than 20% in early untreated series. Lupus anticoagulant carries most of the risk; low-level IgM/IgG anticardiolipin and isolated false-positive syphilis serology do not.
- APS treatment: aspirin 81 mg/day (start while trying to conceive if indicated); add heparin 5,000 units twice daily or low-dose low-molecular-weight heparin at confirmation of fetal heartbeat and continue throughout pregnancy. With thrombocytopenia <50 × 10⁹/L, consider intravenous immunoglobulin or prednisone.
- Warfarin is teratogenic and contraindicated; fondaparinux failed in animal models. Postpartum anticoagulation for 6–12 weeks in treated women without prior thrombosis; continue aspirin at least 3 months.
- APS fetal monitoring: ultrasound for growth and placental volume, weekly antepartum heart rate testing from as early as 25 weeks. In the 25- to 32-week window a reactive nonstress test is two accelerations of at least 10 beats/min in 20 minutes; after that, 15 beats/min.
- Thrombosis risk in APS rises after delivery, especially after anticoagulation is stopped; stroke, thrombophlebitis, ischemic cardiomyopathy, and myocardial infarction can occur postpartum.
- Neonatal lupus: photosensitive rash, thrombocytopenia, hepatitis, and hemolytic anemia are transient; complete congenital heart block is permanent and usually requires a pacemaker.
- Rheumatoid arthritis usually improves in pregnancy. Continue NSAIDs, corticosteroids, hydroxychloroquine, azathioprine, and TNF-α inhibitors; low-dose prednisone is the safest option. Watch for cervical spine subluxation and temporomandibular arthritis before intubation.
- Sjögren syndrome: about 80% have anti-Ro/SSA and anti-La/SSB, so offspring are at risk for neonatal lupus. Pilocarpine and cevimeline are category C with unknown fetal effects.
- Scleroderma: preeclampsia, congestive heart failure, pulmonary hypertension, and renal insufficiency may occur; renal scleroderma can be indistinguishable from preeclampsia. ACE inhibitors and receptor antagonists are contraindicated (teratogenic risk ratio 2.71, 95% CI 1.72–4.27). Prematurity and growth restriction are the main infant risks.
- Takayasu arteritis: preeclampsia is common and blood pressure is difficult to measure with occluded arm arteries; 60% of infants have growth restriction.
- Ankylosing spondylitis fertility is normal; most patients have no change or modest worsening. Psoriatic arthritis may improve.
- Antiphospholipid antibody testing is not warranted before in vitro fertilization; there is no evidence that treatment improves IVF success (pregnancy OR 0.99, live birth OR 1.07).

## Anti-patterns
- Stopping hydroxychloroquine at conception, which precipitates rash and flare.
- Labeling every third-trimester proteinuria in lupus as preeclampsia without checking complement, anti-dsDNA, and urine casts.
- Using dexamethasone or betamethasone for maternal rheumatic disease; they cross the placenta and treat the fetus, not the mother.
- Continuing NSAIDs beyond 34 weeks, risking ductus arteriosus closure and oligohydramnios.
- Prescribing ACE inhibitors for scleroderma renal crisis in pregnancy.
- Using warfarin for APS in pregnancy.
- Reassuring on the basis of a low-titer anticardiolipin antibody alone; risk tracks with lupus anticoagulant and high titer.
- Measuring aPL antibodies to explain IVF failure.

## Key Takeaways
1. Aim for quiescent disease before conception; continue hydroxychloroquine, azathioprine, sulfasalazine, and TNF-α inhibitors through pregnancy.
2. Distinguish lupus nephritis from preeclampsia with complement, anti-dsDNA, urine sediment, and tempo of worsening.
3. In APS, give aspirin 81 mg plus prophylactic heparin or low-molecular-weight heparin from confirmation of cardiac activity, and continue anticoagulation 6–12 weeks postpartum.
4. Screen all SLE and Sjögren pregnancies for anti-Ro/SSA and anti-La/SSB, and monitor the fetal heart rate for congenital heart block, which is permanent in <3% but recurs in 18%.
5. Avoid cyclophosphamide, methotrexate, leflunomide, warfarin, and ACE inhibitors; stop NSAIDs after 34 weeks.
6. Anticipate cervical spine and temporomandibular disease before anesthesia in rheumatoid arthritis, and growth restriction in scleroderma and Takayasu arteritis.
