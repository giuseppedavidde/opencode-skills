# Chapter 21: Imaging of the Face and Neck

## Core Idea
The face and neck are imaged to answer three questions: is there a cleft, is the mandible normal, and is there a neck mass that threatens the airway. Each of these findings is a marker rather than an isolated defect — orofacial clefting is associated with spine, cardiac, and chromosomal anomalies; cystic hygroma carries a 50% aneuploidy rate; micrognathia is associated with abnormal karyotype in two thirds of cases. The practical consequence is that every facial or neck finding triggers a structured search: detailed anatomy, fetal echocardiography, karyotype, and airway planning at delivery.

## Frameworks Introduced
**Cleft lip/palate classification (four types).**
- Type 1: unilateral cleft lip, no cleft palate.
- Type 2: unilateral cleft lip with cleft palate.
- Type 3: bilateral cleft lip and palate.
- Type 4: midline cleft lip and palate.

**Embryologic timing framework.** The upper lip and primary palate fuse by week 7; the secondary palate forms by fusion of the palatal shelves by week 12. Clefting results when the frontonasal process fails to join the lateral maxillary prominences at about week 7, and the developing brain induces frontonasal development, so a midline cleft can signal an underlying brain abnormality by week 4. The incisive foramen divides anterior (primary) from posterior (secondary) clefting.

**Micrognathia measurement framework.** Jaw index = (anteroposterior mandibular diameter ÷ biparietal diameter) × 100, measured on a true sagittal plane from the symphysis mentis to a line between the mandibular rami. The key confounder is pseudo-micrognathia from an oblique plane or abnormal head shape.

**Neck mass differential.** Cystic hygroma versus thickened nuchal translucency, posterior encephalocele, cervical meningocele, cystic teratoma, hemangioma, thyroglossal duct cyst, branchial cleft cyst, laryngocele, and twin-twin transfusion in monochorionic-diamniotic twins.

## Key Concepts
- Cleft lip with or without cleft palate occurs in about **1/1,000** births and isolated cleft palate in about **5/1,000**; 60% of facial clefts are isolated, with ethnic variation from 1/600 in Asians to 1/2,500 in African Americans.
- Clefts are typically not detectable before about **18 weeks**. Lips are best seen coronally and the palate axially; 3D improves palatal assessment, showing alveolar ridge disruption or premaxillary protrusion in bilateral cleft lip and palate.
- Unilateral cleft lip is usually left-sided; the gap is obliquely aligned from lip to nose, the profile may show a hooked nose, and a maxillary–palatal gap may be present. Bilateral clefting shows a central premaxillary mass protruding below the nose. Midline clefting shows absent central maxilla and lip, and a deformed or absent nose that may be replaced by a proboscis.
- Associated anomalies: lumbar and cervical spine **33%**, cardiac **24%**, chromosomal **10%** (especially trisomy 13). Midline clefts warrant specific suspicion of holoprosencephaly and trisomy 13. Polyhydramnios occurs if swallowing is impaired; growth is usually normal with isolated clefts.
- Up to **25%** of infants with orofacial clefting have an associated malformation discovered only postnatally, which justifies a pediatric dysmorphology examination.
- Recurrence risk: up to **4%** if parents are unaffected with no family history; **4%** if one parent is affected and no prior child; **12%** with one affected child; **25%** with two affected children. When both parents are affected: **35%** with no affected children, **45%** with one, **50%** with two.
- Cystic hygroma occurs in **1 in 100** first-trimester pregnancies; septated hygroma in **1 in 285**. It arises from failure of the primitive lymphatic tree to connect to the venous system, producing a blind lymphatic pouch; a thick midline band (nuchal ligament) may separate bilateral jugular sacs.
- Associated anomalies in first-trimester cystic hygroma: structural anomalies in **33.8%**; aneuploidy in **50%** — trisomy 21 **37.3%**, Turner syndrome **28.3%**, trisomy 18 **19.4%**, trisomy 13 **9.0%**, triploidy **4.5%**. Cardiac anomalies are **72.7%** of structural anomalies; in euploid fetuses major congenital heart disease occurs in **4.3%**.
- Septated compared with nonseptated hygroma confers higher risk of aneuploidy (OR **5.2**), cardiac malformation (OR **12.4**), and fetal or neonatal death (OR **6.0**).
- Prognosis: mortality for hygroma diagnosed before 30 weeks is **93%** in older series, with **84%** of deaths associated with progressive nonimmune hydrops. In the FASTER trial, with termination in **59.8%**, spontaneous intrauterine demise occurred in **37.7%** of continuing pregnancies; **16.7%** of first-trimester hygromas had a normal pediatric outcome, and two thirds of live births were normal. Spontaneous in utero resolution is associated with improved survival.
- Management of hygroma: detailed anatomy, fetal echocardiogram, serial growth and hydrops surveillance, late third-trimester antenatal testing, definitive karyotype, fetal MRI for airway relationship, and EXIT procedure if tracheal impingement is suspected.
- Micrognathia: jaw index **<23** has **100% sensitivity** and **98.7% specificity**; jaw index **<21** has **100% positive predictive value**. Polyhydramnios occurs in up to **70%**, from impaired swallowing.
- In sonographically "isolated" micrognathia: **54%** have airway difficulty at birth requiring intervention, **31%** have feeding difficulty, and **38%** have developmental delay. Chromosomal abnormalities are found in **66%**; karyotype should include 22q11.2 deletion testing.
- Delivery planning for micrognathia: tertiary center, anticipation of difficult intubation, EXIT procedure consideration, and neonatal airway support ranging from nasal trumpet or oral airway with bag-mask ventilation to tracheostomy.

## Anti-patterns
- **Diagnosing micrognathia on an oblique plane.** Pseudo-micrognathia from incorrect imaging plane or abnormal head shape is the principal error; a true midline sagittal view is required.
- **Treating a cleft as an isolated surgical problem.** Screen spine, heart, and karyotype; 33% have spine anomalies, 24% cardiac, and 10% chromosomal abnormalities.
- **Reassuring after a normal-appearing lip.** Up to 25% of associated malformations are found only after birth.
- **Calling a septated neck cyst benign.** Septation raises the odds of aneuploidy 5.2-fold and of death 6.0-fold.
- **Assuming resolution of a hygroma eliminates risk.** Resolution improves prognosis but does not remove the need for karyotype and cardiac evaluation.
- **Overlooking airway obstruction in micrognathia.** Glossoptosis and mandibular hypoplasia can make intubation impossible; plan the airway before delivery.
- **Using older mortality figures without context.** The 93% figure predates contemporary termination and neonatal care; the FASTER data give a more current picture.

## Key Takeaways
- Time the anatomy scan for clefts at or after 18 weeks; use coronal views for lips and axial views for palate, with 3D for posterior palate and alveolar ridge.
- For any cleft, obtain karyotype, fetal echocardiogram, and genetic counseling, and prescribe 4 mg folic acid before a future pregnancy.
- Quote recurrence risks by family structure: 4% baseline, 12% with one affected child, 25% with two, and up to 50% when both parents are affected.
- Measure the jaw index on a true sagittal plane; <23 is sensitive, <21 is highly predictive, and either warrants airway planning.
- For cystic hygroma, obtain karyotype and fetal echocardiogram regardless of septation, and monitor for hydrops and polyhydramnios.
- Plan delivery in a tertiary center with airway expertise for micrognathia or giant neck masses, and consider EXIT when tracheal compression is likely.
- Expect polyhydramnios with both clefting and micrognathia when swallowing is impaired, and monitor the amniotic fluid index monthly.
