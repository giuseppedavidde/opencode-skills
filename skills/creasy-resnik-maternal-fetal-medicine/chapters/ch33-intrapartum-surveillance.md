# Chapter 33: Intrapartum Fetal Surveillance

## Core Idea
Intrapartum electronic fetal monitoring (EFM) detects hypoxemia with high sensitivity but very low specificity. Heart rate patterns must be interpreted physiologically, in the context of uterine activity, maternal hemodynamics, and the duration of the abnormal pattern. A normal tracing is highly reassuring; an abnormal tracing is not a diagnosis of asphyxia. The chapter's central argument is that EFM should be used with structured categorization, intrauterine resuscitation, and selected adjuncts, not as a standalone trigger for operative delivery.

## Frameworks Introduced
**Three-tier fetal heart rate interpretation (ACOG/NICHD 2008).**
- Category I: baseline 110–160 beats/min; moderate variability (6–25 beats/min); no late or variable decelerations; early decelerations present or absent; accelerations present or absent.
- Category II: all tracings not in category I or III. Examples include bradycardia without absent variability, tachycardia, minimal variability, absent variability without recurrent decelerations, marked variability, absence of induced accelerations, recurrent variable decelerations with minimal or moderate variability, prolonged deceleration ≥2 minutes but <10 minutes, recurrent late decelerations with moderate variability, and variable decelerations with slow return, overshoot, or shoulders.
- Category III: absent baseline variability with recurrent late decelerations, recurrent variable decelerations, or bradycardia; or a sinusoidal pattern.

**Deceleration definitions.** Late: gradual decrease, onset to nadir ≥30 seconds, nadir after the contraction peak. Early: gradual, nadir coincident with the contraction peak. Variable: abrupt decrease (<30 seconds to nadir), at least 15 beats/min, lasting at least 15 seconds and no more than 2 minutes. Prolonged: at least 15 beats/min below baseline, lasting 2–10 minutes; 10 minutes or more becomes a baseline change.

**Umbilical cord acid-base criteria for intrapartum hypoxia (all four required).** Metabolic acidosis in umbilical artery blood at delivery (pH <7.00 and base deficit ≥12 mmol/L); early-onset moderate or severe neonatal encephalopathy at ≥34 weeks; cerebral palsy of spastic quadriplegic or dyskinetic type; exclusion of other causes such as trauma, coagulation disorder, infection, or genetic disease.

## Key Concepts
- Baseline heart rate: 110–160 beats/min. Tachycardia >160 for at least 10 minutes; bradycardia <110. Fetal tachyarrhythmia is usually >240 beats/min.
- Variability: absent, minimal (≤5 beats/min), moderate (6–25), marked (>25). Moderate variability, regardless of other patterns, indicates that cerebral tissue is not acidemic because the fetus can centralize oxygen delivery.
- Nonhypoxic causes of absent variability: anencephaly, narcotics or sedatives, vagal blockade (atropine, scopolamine), and complete heart block (rate 60–80 beats/min).
- Recurrent decelerations occur with more than 50% of contractions in a 20-minute period.
- Reflex late decelerations have normal variability and indicate compensated chemoreceptor-mediated vagal response. Nonreflex late decelerations occur without variability and indicate myocardial depression and decompensation.
- In chronically instrumented monkeys, late decelerations appeared when aortic Po2 fell from 28 to 24 mm Hg with a normal acid-base state; accelerations disappeared at Po2 19 mm Hg and pH 7.22, and death followed about 36 hours of persistent late decelerations without accelerations.
- Variable decelerations reflect umbilical cord compression. If the fetus was previously normoxic, position change and amnioinfusion often abolish the pattern.
- Sinusoidal pattern: smooth sine wave, 3–6 cycles/min, amplitude up to 30 beats/min, persisting 20 minutes or more. It requires rapid intervention in an Rh-sensitized patient and evaluation for severe anemia or asphyxia.
- Umbilical artery pH: mean 7.28 ± 0.07 at term; venous mean 7.34. The old cutoff of 7.20 is no longer appropriate because most newborns below 7.20 are vigorous. pH <7.00 is the clinically significant threshold; neonatal complications rise sharply below it.
- Base deficit: 12–16 mmol/L is associated with about 10% neonatal encephalopathy, respiratory complications, or composite morbidity; >16 mmol/L with about 40%.
- EFM versus intermittent auscultation: no measurable reduction in cerebral palsy. The positive predictive value of a nonreassuring tracing for cerebral palsy is 0.14% (1–2 per 1000), and the false-positive rate exceeds 99%.
- ST-waveform analysis (STAN) with cardiotocography reduced the need for fetal blood sampling and metabolic acidosis (pH <7.05 and base deficit >12 mmol/L) in experienced hands, but a large randomized trial found no significant reduction in the primary outcome.
- Fetal scalp blood sampling is rarely performed in the United States. Typical scalp pH is about 7.33 in early first stage, 7.32 in late first stage, and 7.29 in second stage.
- Intrauterine resuscitation: intravenous fluids and position change for hypotension; reduce or stop oxytocin and use a lateral position for excessive uterine activity; position change and amnioinfusion for cord compression; tocolysis (terbutaline) for tachysystole; maternal oxygen and lateral positioning for prolonged asphyxia. Push only with alternate contractions when head compression causes decelerations.

## Anti-patterns
- Treating a category II tracing as an automatic indication for cesarean delivery; most category II tracings are not acidemic.
- Ignoring moderate variability because decelerations are present. Variability is the best single indicator of central oxygenation.
- Calling a deceleration prolonged when it lasts less than 2 minutes, or missing that a deceleration lasting 10 minutes is now the baseline.
- Using an umbilical artery pH of 7.20 as proof of asphyxia, or a pH below 7.00 without a base deficit as proof of hypoxic injury.
- Attributing cerebral palsy to intrapartum events without meeting all four criteria and excluding other causes.
- Continuing oxytocin in the face of recurrent late decelerations or tachysystole.
- Relying on EFM alone to prevent cerebral palsy; the false-positive rate is greater than 99%.

## Key Takeaways
1. Categorize every tracing as I, II, or III, and document baseline, variability, accelerations, and decelerations.
2. Treat the pattern, not the category: start intrauterine resuscitation for any nonreassuring feature, and reassess.
3. Use moderate variability as the strongest evidence of preserved central oxygenation.
4. Interpret cord gases with pH and base deficit: pH <7.00 with base deficit ≥12 mmol/L defines metabolic acidosis.
5. Do not promise that EFM prevents cerebral palsy; use it to detect hypoxemia and to guide timely delivery.
