# Chapter 70: Anesthesia Considerations for Complicated Pregnancies

## Core Idea
Pregnancy converts routine anesthesia into a high-risk procedure: functional residual capacity falls 20%, oxygen consumption rises 20%, airway mucosa becomes edematous, and the failed intubation rate is roughly ten times that of the general operating room. The chapter's central claim is that most maternal anesthesia deaths are avoidable through preparation — a planned neuraxial technique, a difficult-airway algorithm, a massive transfusion protocol, and early involvement of the anesthesiologist. Physiologic values that are "normal" in a nonpregnant woman (PaCO₂, creatinine, hemoglobin) indicate decompensation in a parturient.

## Frameworks Introduced
**Physiologic change → anesthetic implication table.** Blood volume +40%, plasma volume +50%, heart rate +15 beats/min, cardiac output +40%, systemic resistance −20%, alveolar ventilation +70%, minute ventilation +50%, FRC −20%, metabolic rate +20%, renal blood flow and GFR +60% (creatinine halved), MAC reduced 30–40%, local anesthetic requirement reduced 30%.

**Obstetric difficult-airway algorithm.** Failed intubation → call for help → mask ventilation with oral/nasal airways and two-handed jaw thrust → if mask ventilation fails, place laryngeal mask airway → video laryngoscope → fiberoptic intubation through LMA → surgical airway. For elective cesarean, consider awakening the patient for neuraxial or awake intubation.

**Massive transfusion protocol.** Early component therapy at a 1:1:1 ratio of packed red cells, fresh frozen plasma, and platelets; permissive hypotension to 80–100 mm Hg until surgical bleeding is controlled; avoid massive crystalloid; thromboelastography to guide fibrinogen and component replacement.

**ACLS-modified maternal cardiac arrest sequence.** Left uterine displacement, cricoid pressure until the airway is secured, bag-mask ventilation only until intubation, chest compressions midsternum, no paddle repositioning, defibrillate before delivery, full standard drug doses, and perimortem cesarean within 4–5 minutes of unsuccessful resuscitation at more than 20 weeks' gestation.

## Key Concepts
- Maternal cardiac arrest complicates 1 of 12,300 delivery admissions; about half follow hemorrhage; only 55% survive to discharge. Simulated labor-room perimortem cesarean took 4 minutes 25 seconds versus 7 minutes 53 seconds in the operating room; 57% of labor-room teams met the 5-minute target versus 14% of operating-room teams.
- Left uterine displacement was performed in only 44% of simulated resuscitations despite current ACLS certification.
- Failed intubation occurs in about 1 of 275 obstetric attempts, roughly 10 times the general operating room rate. Large breasts, mucosal edema, left lateral positioning, and emergency conditions all increase difficulty.
- Neuraxial anesthesia is preferred for most cardiac lesions. Use it cautiously or not at all in severe pulmonary hypertension, Eisenmenger syndrome, and preload-dependent lesions.
- Carboprost (Hemabate) and methylergonovine increase pulmonary and systemic vascular resistance; nifedipine, β-agonist tocolytics, magnesium boluses, oxytocin boluses, and spinal/epidural sympathectomy reduce systemic vascular resistance.
- Infective endocarditis prophylaxis is no longer recommended for delivery except in the highest-risk groups: prosthetic valves, prior endocarditis, unrepaired cyanotic congenital heart disease, repaired congenital disease with prosthetic material within 6 months, and repaired disease with residual defects at or adjacent to a patch.
- In preeclampsia, many anesthesiologists place neuraxial blocks with platelet counts as low as 75,000/mm³ if the count is stable, not falling, and there is no clinical bleeding. No platelet count or platelet function test reliably predicts neuraxial bleeding.
- Phenylephrine is preferred over ephedrine for spinal hypotension when maternal heart rate is above 70 beats/min because it produces better umbilical pH. Crystalloid or colloid preloading has little or no benefit.
- Magnesium therapy: have additional uterotonics available for atony; if the patient cannot lift her head for 5 seconds, stop the infusion before induction; avoid vecuronium and rocuronium because of difficulty with reversal.
- About one third of eclamptic seizures occur postpartum, and 79% of those occur more than 48 hours after delivery, often without an antepartum diagnosis.
- Regional anesthesia for placenta previa is associated with less blood loss and less transfusion than general anesthesia; for accreta, use epidural or combined spinal-epidural to allow conversion and duration.
- Placenta accreta: 95% require red cell transfusion, 39% massive transfusion, and 10% more than 20 units. Prophylactic iliac balloon catheters showed no outcome improvement in a case-control study and carry vascular injury risk.
- Cell salvage is safe in obstetrics and can be lifesaving when cross-matched blood is unavailable or transfusion is refused.
- Recombinant factor VIIa is not first-line: 80% improved after a single dose in a European registry; median dose 92 µg/kg with 76% response in the Australian registry; thrombotic events occur.
- Severe preeclampsia stroke: 93% hemorrhagic, 54% mortality, nearly all survivors severely disabled; all had systolic pressure above 155 mm Hg, and only 12% had diastolic above 110 mm Hg. Target systolic below 160 and diastolic below 110 mm Hg.
- LMWH and neuraxial blockade: hold prophylactic dose 12 hours before block and resume no sooner than 2 hours after catheter removal; hold therapeutic dose 24 hours before and resume at least 12 hours after removal.
- Asthma affects 4–8% of pregnant women. Avoid carboprost, unopposed β-antagonists, and NSAIDs in aspirin-sensitive patients. Normal nonpregnant PaCO₂ and PaO₂ values in an exacerbation indicate decompensation.
- Sepsis: temperature above 38°C or below 36°C, heart rate above 90, respiratory rate above 20, white cell count above 12,000 or below 4,000 cells/mm³. Group A streptococcus causes 50% of deaths. Give antibiotics within 1 hour; target MAP above 65 mm Hg, CVP 8–12 mm Hg, urine output at least 0.5 mL/kg/h, and central venous oxygen saturation at least 70%.
- Renal insufficiency in pregnancy: creatinine 0.9–1.4 mg/dL is mild and above 3.0 mg/dL is severe. Avoid gentamicin and NSAIDs; adjust renally cleared drugs such as magnesium sulfate.
- Induction agents: propofol or ketamine for bronchospasm; etomidate or ketamine for hemodynamic instability; succinylcholine for rapid sequence relaxation. Verify tube placement with end-tidal CO₂ and auscultation.

## Anti-patterns
- Accepting a "normal" PaCO₂ of 40 mm Hg or PaO₂ of 90 mm Hg in a decompensating pregnant asthmatic.
- Interpreting a creatinine of 1.0 mg/dL as normal renal function in pregnancy.
- Giving a labor epidural without checking the platelet trend in preeclampsia.
- Using ephedrine as the default pressor when the maternal heart rate is already above 70.
- Continuing magnesium at full rate into general anesthesia without anticipating uterine atony and prolonged weakness.
- Rushing to intubate an obstetric patient without a pre-formulated failed-airway plan and the most experienced operator first.
- Massive crystalloid resuscitation in obstetric hemorrhage rather than early blood products.
- Delaying perimortem cesarean beyond 4–5 minutes while moving the patient to an operating room.
- Restarting therapeutic heparin too soon after epidural catheter removal.

## Key Takeaways
1. Treat the pregnant airway as difficult: preoxygenate, have a video laryngoscope and laryngeal mask available, and let the most experienced provider make the first attempt.
2. Use neuraxial anesthesia whenever feasible, including preeclampsia with a stable platelet count of at least 75,000/mm³ and no bleeding.
3. For hemorrhage, activate a massive transfusion protocol early at 1:1:1, target permissive hypotension of 80–100 mm Hg, and use thromboelastography to guide fibrinogen replacement.
4. In maternal arrest, maintain left uterine displacement and deliver by cesarean within 4–5 minutes if resuscitation fails.
5. Time heparin around neuraxial procedures: 12 hours for prophylactic and 24 hours for therapeutic doses before block; resume no earlier than 2 and 12 hours after catheter removal, respectively.
6. In sepsis, give broad-spectrum antibiotics within 1 hour, target MAP above 65 mm Hg and urine output above 0.5 mL/kg/h, and use norepinephrine first.
