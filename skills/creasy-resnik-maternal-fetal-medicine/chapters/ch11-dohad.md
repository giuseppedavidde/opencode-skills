# Chapter 11: Developmental Origins of Health and Disease

## Core Idea

The environment in utero shapes susceptibility to chronic disease in adult life. Birth weight was the first marker of that effect, but it is a weak index of fetal nutrition and growth. The chapter argues that the relevant signal is the trajectory of growth and the match between the predicted and the actual postnatal environment. A fetus that prepares for scarcity and is born into plenty carries the highest risk. The implication is that prevention begins before conception.

## Frameworks Introduced

**Fetal origins of adult disease (Barker hypothesis).** Low birth weight is associated with coronary heart disease, hypertension, stroke, type 2 diabetes, and osteoporosis in later life.

**Thrifty phenotype hypothesis.** Fetal phenotypic changes in response to nutritional imbalance confer immediate survival advantage but increase later disease risk. Reduced skeletal muscle mass is the proposed mediator of later insulin resistance.

**Predictive adaptive response hypothesis.** The fetus is not set to cope with a prenatal challenge but to respond to a predicted postnatal environment. Disease arises from a mismatch between the prediction and the actual environment.

**Developmental plasticity and epigenetic model.** Sustained changes in DNA methylation, histone structure, and noncoding RNAs alter gene expression permanently. Some changes are reversible by dietary methyl donors.

## Key Concepts

- Early cohort studies linked low birth weight to cardiovascular death and to every component of the metabolic syndrome.
- A Finnish cohort of 13,830 men and women showed an association between low birth weight and all-cause mortality in women.
- A follow-up of 10,803 Aberdeen children born in the 1950s confirmed an inverse association between birth weight and later coronary artery disease and stroke.
- A Swedish cohort of almost 90,000 army recruits born between 1973 and 1981 showed lower birth weight linked to higher adult blood pressure, independent of socioeconomic factors.
- Among 8760 Finnish men and women born between 1934 and 1944, coronary events were more common in those who were small at birth, thin at 2 years, and had a greater rise in body mass index by 11 years.
- Rapid postnatal growth after small birth size is associated with insulin resistance and raised abdominal girth at 8 years in the ALSPAC cohort.
- Birth weight is a weak proxy because small size can follow poor early growth or late growth restraint, and the two have different causes.
- Maternal obesity and gestational diabetes are associated with offspring obesity, insulin resistance, and higher blood pressure. Some associations persist after adjustment for maternal body mass index.
- Excessive gestational weight gain is consistently associated with greater childhood adiposity.
- A Danish cohort of 28,354 mother-child pairs found antibiotic use in the first 6 months associated with childhood overweight in children of normal-weight mothers but not obese mothers.
- Children born by cesarean section may be at greater risk of later obesity, possibly through the infant gut microbiome.
- Antenatal glucocorticoids: neonatal blood pressure was elevated by 2 standard deviations in 67% of children exposed to multiple courses and 24% exposed to a single course. A randomized trial of a single betamethasone course found no difference in psychological function or quality of life at 31 years.
- In animal models, maternal undernutrition and maternal obesity both produce offspring hypertension through increased sympathetic outflow and renal mechanisms.
- Maternal vitamin D status influences neonatal fat mass and adiposity at ages 4 and 6 years.
- The "thin-fat" phenotype describes greater abdominal adiposity with lower muscle mass in Indian compared with Caucasian neonates.
- Low birth weight is also associated with reduced adult bone density, schizophrenia, breast cancer, and asthma.

## Anti-patterns

- Using birth weight as a complete measure of fetal nutrition. Body composition is the better index.
- Treating developmental programming as irreversible destiny. The mismatch model leaves room for intervention.
- Adjusting for maternal body mass index in every analysis. This may be overadjustment when obesity is part of the causal pathway.
- Recommending caloric restriction in pregnancy. Improve insulin sensitivity instead.
- Assuming the historical cohorts apply unchanged to contemporary populations. Nutrition and survival differed.
- Ignoring paternal, environmental, and epigenetic contributions.

## Key Takeaways

1. Interpret birth weight together with growth trajectory and body composition, not alone.
2. Treat mismatch between predicted and actual postnatal nutrition as the modifiable element.
3. Counsel obese women to reach a normal body mass index before conception.
4. Use a single course of antenatal corticosteroids when indicated; the long-term signal is reassuring.
5. Follow offspring of diabetic and obese pregnancies for metabolic risk.
6. Frame prevention as a life-course intervention, starting before conception.
