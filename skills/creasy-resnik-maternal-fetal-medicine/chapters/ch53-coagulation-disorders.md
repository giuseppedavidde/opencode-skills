# Chapter 53: Coagulation Disorders in Pregnancy

## Core Idea

Pregnancy shifts hemostasis toward thrombosis while simultaneously creating a high risk of hemorrhage at placentation and in the third stage of labor. Inherited and acquired thrombophilias are therefore associated with venous thromboembolism, the leading cause of maternal death in the United States, and possibly with placenta-mediated complications. Bleeding disorders, chiefly von Willebrand disease and immune thrombocytopenia, threaten peripartum hemorrhage. Diagnosis requires laboratory confirmation and an understanding of which tests change in normal pregnancy. Management balances thromboprophylaxis against bleeding at delivery and neuraxial anesthesia.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Antiphospholipid antibody syndrome (APS) criteria.** At least one clinical and one laboratory criterion. Clinical: one or more objectively confirmed arterial, venous, or small-vessel thromboses; or pregnancy morbidity, defined as one or more unexplained fetal deaths at 10 weeks or later, one or more premature births before 34 weeks from eclampsia, severe preeclampsia, or uteroplacental insufficiency, or three or more unexplained consecutive spontaneous abortions before 10 weeks with other causes excluded. Laboratory: lupus anticoagulant, anticardiolipin IgG or IgM at medium or high titer (above 40 GPL or MPL, or above the 99th percentile), or anti-beta-2-glycoprotein-1 IgG or IgM above the 99th percentile, each confirmed on two occasions at least 12 weeks apart. The test and the clinical event must be less than 12 weeks and less than 5 years apart.
- **Inherited thrombophilia risk stratification (Table 53-1).** Relative risks are reported for factor V Leiden (FVL), prothrombin gene mutation (PGM), antithrombin deficiency, protein S deficiency, and protein C deficiency, with and without a personal or first-degree family history of venous thromboembolism.
- **ITP diagnostic and treatment thresholds.** ITP is a platelet count below 100,000/µL, diagnosed by exclusion. Treatment aims for more than 30,000/µL during pregnancy, more than 50,000/µL near term, and 75,000 to 80,000/µL before epidural placement.
- **von Willebrand disease classification.** Type I (80% of cases, autosomal dominant, quantitative deficiency), type II (qualitative defects; IIA loses high-molecular-weight multimers, IIB has increased platelet affinity, IIM has reduced GpIb interaction, IIN has impaired factor VIII binding), and type III (autosomal recessive, severe deficiency).
- **TTP versus HUS versus HELLP differentiation (Table 53-3).** Uses neurologic symptoms, fever, hypertension, renal dysfunction, platelet count, liver enzymes, lactate dehydrogenase, coagulation studies, von Willebrand multimer pattern, and ADAMTS13 activity.

## Key Concepts

- FVL occurs in about 5% of Europeans; heterozygotes have a fivefold VTE risk and about 35% lifetime incidence, homozygotes a 25-fold risk and about 65% lifetime incidence. FVL accounts for about 40% of VTE events in pregnancy.
- Without a personal or family history, heterozygous FVL carries less than 0.3% pregnancy VTE risk; with a prior VTE or affected first-degree relative, at least 10%. Homozygotes without such history have about a 1.5% risk.
- Antithrombin deficiency (activity below 60%) carries a relative risk of 119 and a greater than 40% VTE probability with a personal or family history.
- Protein C deficiency (below 50%) carries a relative risk of 13.0; protein S deficiency (below 55%) has an absolute risk of about 6.6% with history.
- Double heterozygosity for FVL and PGM carries a relative risk of 84.
- FVL is associated with pregnancy loss after 9 to 10 weeks (odds ratio 3.46; CI 2.53 to 4.72) but not with losses at 3 to 9 weeks.
- In APS, about two thirds of thromboses are venous, deep leg veins are the commonest site, and up to 50% of venous events are pulmonary emboli. Lupus anticoagulant carries a venous thrombosis odds ratio of 6.32 and a recurrent venous thrombosis odds ratio of 11.6 in SLE.
- VTE risk in pregnancy and the puerperium in APS is estimated at 5% to 12%. Up to 50% of pregnant women with APS develop preeclampsia and one third have growth restriction.
- ITP: gestational thrombocytopenia affects up to 5% of pregnant women, accounts for more than 70% of maternal thrombocytopenia, is mild (above 70,000/µL), and needs no special care.
- Prednisone 2.0 mg/kg/day (or the therapeutic equivalent) is first-line for ITP; response occurs within 1 week and peaks in 2 to 3 weeks. Platelet transfusion raises the count by about 10,000/µL per unit in normal patients but not in ITP.
- von Willebrand disease occurs in 1.3% of the population. Postpartum hemorrhage odds ratio is 1.5 (CI 1.1 to 2.0) and transfusion odds ratio is 4.7 (CI 3.2 to 7); mortality is 10-fold higher.
- vWD treatment: desmopressin 60 minutes before delivery or at cord clamping for type I or responsive patients; target vWF:Ac of 50% for prophylaxis and 100% for bleeding or surgery, maintained 3 days after vaginal and 5 days after cesarean delivery.
- TTP occurs in 1 per 25,000 births; early diagnosis and plasma exchange can reduce mortality by 90%. A high lactate dehydrogenase to AST ratio favors TTP over HELLP.
- Hemophilia A: severe disease is factor VIII below 2%, mild is 2% to 25%, carriers about 50%. Hemophilia B follows the same pattern for factor IX.

## Anti-patterns

- Treating every low platelet count in pregnancy as ITP; gestational thrombocytopenia is far more common and benign.
- Withholding steroid therapy in ITP because of fear of preterm rupture of membranes when the count is dangerously low.
- Placing an epidural without confirming a platelet count of at least 75,000 to 80,000/µL.
- Diagnosing APS from a single positive antibody test without confirming it 12 weeks later.
- Assuming all thrombophilias cause recurrent early miscarriage; the association is with fetal loss after 9 to 10 weeks.
- Using desmopressin near delivery without monitoring for fluid retention and hyponatremia.
- Missing TTP because the picture resembles HELLP syndrome.

## Key Takeaways

1. Test for APS with lupus anticoagulant, anticardiolipin, and anti-beta-2-glycoprotein-1 on two occasions at least 12 weeks apart, and treat with heparin plus aspirin in pregnancy.
2. Give full-dose heparin throughout pregnancy to APS patients with prior thrombosis, and prophylactic heparin for 6 weeks postpartum to those without prior thrombosis.
3. Screen for inherited thrombophilia only when the result will change management, such as a strong family history or prior unprovoked VTE.
4. Manage ITP to a target above 30,000/µL in pregnancy, above 50,000/µL near term, and 75,000 to 80,000/µL before neuraxial anesthesia.
5. Characterize von Willebrand disease before pregnancy, and use desmopressin or von Willebrand factor concentrate to cover delivery.
6. Distinguish TTP from HELLP with the lactate dehydrogenase to AST ratio, ADAMTS13 activity, and the peripheral smear, and start plasma exchange early for TTP.
