# Chapter 50: Maternal Mortality

## Core Idea

Maternal mortality is both a public health statistic and a driver for improving maternity care. Definitions matter: deaths occur in nonviable pregnancies that are absent from the live-birth denominator, so the true measure is a ratio, and the CDC extends surveillance to 365 days after delivery. Cardiovascular disease now leads, and most causes are at least partly preventable. Prevention concentrates on hemorrhage and preeclampsia, where the gap between women who died and women who survived the same diagnosis is largest. Case review must be converted into practice change through a structured surveillance and response cycle.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **WHO maternal death definition.** Death of a woman while pregnant or within 42 days of termination of pregnancy, from any cause related to or aggravated by the pregnancy or its management, but not from incidental or accidental causes. Case ascertainment uses the death certificate and ICD-10 obstetric ("O") codes; O96 designates late obstetric death.
- **CDC Pregnancy Mortality Surveillance System (1985).** Links birth and death certificates and therefore finds more deaths than death certificates alone. It extends the window to 365 days and defines pregnancy-associated mortality (any death within 1 year of termination) and pregnancy-related mortality (within 1 year, related to or aggravated by pregnancy).
- **Direct versus indirect death.** Direct death results from obstetric complications of pregnancy, labor, puerperium, or their management. Indirect death results from pre-existing or newly developed disease aggravated by pregnancy. Late maternal death occurs between 42 and 365 days.
- **Preventability assessment.** North Carolina used a binary "preventable" judgment; California used graded evidence (strong, good, some, none); the United Kingdom asked whether substandard care contributed, and whether the contribution was major or minor.
- **WHO surveillance and response cycle.** Identify cases, collect data, analyze cases, issue recommendations for action, then evaluate and refine during the next cycle.
- **Condition-specific improvement bundles (Boxes 50-1 to 50-6).** Hemorrhage and massive transfusion, amniotic fluid embolism, venous thromboembolism, preeclampsia, cardiovascular disease, and suicide/homicide.

## Key Concepts

- US maternal mortality rate fell from about 900 per 100,000 births in 1901 to 9 per 100,000 in 1991, then rose to 12.7 in 2007.
- The United Kingdom reported 6.8 per 100,000 for 2006 to 2008 with nearly 100% ascertainment, roughly half the US figure, despite comparable disadvantaged minority populations.
- Age gradient: 12.4 per 100,000 for women in their twenties, 24.1 at ages 35 to 39, and 54.9 at age 40 or older.
- African-American maternal mortality is threefold to fourfold higher than other groups, the largest disparity of any US health indicator.
- Cardiovascular disease is the leading cause; cardiomyopathy accounts for two thirds of cardiovascular deaths.
- Hemorrhage causes more than 10% of US maternal deaths, with a cause-specific ratio of 1.6 per 100,000 versus 0.4 in the United Kingdom, yet it has the lowest case-fatality of all causes.
- Venous thromboembolism causes about 10% of deaths: 0.79 per 100,000 in the United Kingdom after a national prevention push, versus 1.5 in the United States.
- Amniotic fluid embolism occurs in 2 to 6 per 100,000 live births; case-series mortality is 25% to 40%, and population-specific mortality is 0.57 per 100,000 (UK) and 1.1 (US).
- Preventability varies by cause (North Carolina / California / UK): hemorrhage 93/69/44%; preeclampsia 60/50/64%; amniotic fluid embolism 0/0/15%; deep vein thrombosis or pulmonary embolism 17/53/33%; cardiomyopathy 22/25/25%; sepsis 43/50/46%.
- A hospital-based review judged only 28% of deaths potentially preventable; population-based reviews find more.
- Suicide causes 2.0 and homicide 2.9 deaths per 100,000 in pregnancy or the first postpartum year. Intimate-partner violence contributes to 45% of homicides and 54% of suicides. Serious mental illness was present in 57% of UK suicides. 77% of homicides occurred during pregnancy; 42% of suspects were current or former intimate partners.

## Anti-patterns

- Calling the maternal mortality rate a rate when live births are only an approximation of the denominator.
- Comparing national rates without accounting for ascertainment; US data under-ascertain deaths.
- Delay and denial during obstetric hemorrhage, assuming bleeding will resolve.
- Attributing new wheeze or dyspnea in pregnancy to asthma rather than cardiac dysfunction.
- Accepting a systolic pressure of 150 mm Hg as nonsevere.
- Discharging third-trimester nausea, vomiting, or malaise without a complete blood count and liver function tests.
- Assuming amniotic fluid embolism is uniformly fatal and withholding aggressive coagulation-factor replacement.

## Key Takeaways

1. Report maternal mortality with the definition, window, and ascertainment method stated, and prefer the CDC 365-day pregnancy-related definition for review.
2. Maintain unit protocols for hemorrhage response, massive transfusion, preeclampsia antihypertensives (intravenous labetalol or hydralazine), and amniotic fluid embolism.
3. Treat confirmed systolic hypertension of 150 mm Hg lasting 5 to 10 minutes as an emergency.
4. Start thromboprophylaxis about 12 hours after delivery in high-risk women and use sequential compression devices after all cesarean births.
5. Deliver women with placenta previa and more than one prior cesarean in a center with a large blood bank and capability for rapid cesarean hysterectomy.
6. Screen for intimate-partner violence and major psychiatric illness throughout pregnancy and the first postpartum year.
7. Lead local and state mortality review, and convert each case into a measurable practice recommendation.
