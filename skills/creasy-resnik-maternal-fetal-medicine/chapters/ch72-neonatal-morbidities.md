# Chapter 72: Neonatal Morbidities of Prenatal and Perinatal Origin

## Core Idea
Neonatal outcome is decided largely before and during delivery, and it is stratified by gestational age and birth weight more than by any single intervention. The chapter's central argument is that the obstetrician and neonatologist must act as one team: antenatal corticosteroids, maternal transfer to a tertiary center, and delivery-room management are the interventions with the strongest evidence for improving survival and neurodevelopment. It also insists that survival and intact survival are not the same outcome — mortality falls with each additional week of gestation, but neurodevelopmental impairment does not improve proportionately.

## Frameworks Introduced
**Viability and outcome framework.** At 22 weeks survival is rare and usually excluded from outcome studies; at 23 weeks survival is 15–30%; at 24 weeks 30–55%. Birth weight and sex modify survival. Between 30% and 50% of infants born before 25 weeks have moderate to severe disability.

**Preterm complication profile.** Expected rates below 32 weeks or 1500 g: respiratory distress syndrome 65% (80% below 27 weeks), bronchopulmonary dysplasia 23%, retinopathy of prematurity about 40%, intraventricular hemorrhage 11% with ventricular dilatation or parenchymal involvement, necrotizing enterocolitis 5–7%, patent ductus arteriosus 30%, feeding difficulty over 90%. Late preterm (32–38 weeks or 1500–2500 g): RDS 5%, hypoglycemia 10–15%, feeding difficulty 10–15%.

**RDS prevention and treatment bundle.** Antenatal corticosteroids → delivery-room CPAP → early rescue or prophylactic surfactant → volume-control ventilation with low tidal volumes. Combined antenatal steroids and postnatal surfactant outperform surfactant alone.

**Intraventricular hemorrhage grading.** Grade I: germinal matrix only. Grade II: intraventricular blood without ventricular distension. Grade III: intraventricular blood with ventricular dilation. Grade IV: intraparenchymal extension.

**Sarnat staging of hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy.** Stage 1 (mild) usually favorable; stage 2 (moderate) leads to long-term compromise in 20–25%; stage 3 (severe) carries the worst prognosis. Therapeutic hypothermia is standard care for infants of 36 weeks or more with moderate to severe HIE.

**Hyperbilirubinemia risk framework.** Screen before discharge for jaundice and risk factors; interpret bilirubin against hour of life using AAP nomograms; treat with phototherapy or exchange transfusion according to gestational age, risk factors, and hour of life.

## Key Concepts
- Extreme prematurity (before 32 weeks or under 1500 g) accounts for 1.5–2% of deliveries; late preterm birth accounts for 8–9%. Mortality at 34–36 weeks is three times that at 40 weeks; infants born at 35 weeks are nine times more likely to need mechanical ventilation than term infants and have about half the brain volume of a term infant.
- A 401–500 g infant (mean 23.3 weeks) had 17% survival to discharge in the Vermont-Oxford Network.
- Late preterm infants born at 36–37 weeks have an almost sixfold increase in significant hyperbilirubinemia.
- RDS risk factors: prematurity, low birth weight, maternal diabetes, perinatal asphyxia, cesarean delivery without labor, white race, male sex, family history of RDS, surfactant protein B deficiency, and pulmonary hypoplasia (e.g., congenital diaphragmatic hernia).
- RDS presents with tachypnea, nasal flaring, retractions, cyanosis, and expiratory grunting from expiration against a partially closed glottis.
- Surfactant improves oxygenation, reduces ventilator support and air leaks, and lowers mortality. CPAP prevents atelectasis, preserves surfactant, and reduces the need for intubation; complications include pneumothorax, pneumomediastinum, and rarely reduced venous return and cardiac output.
- Early delivery-room CPAP and early prophylactic surfactant are equivalent for extremely low birth weight infants when the outcome is death or BPD.
- BPD: the "new" form affects smaller, extremely preterm infants exposed to antenatal glucocorticoids, with simplified alveoli, impaired capillary configuration, and interstitial cellularity. Oxygen exposure in the first 2 weeks and chronic oxygen therapy correlate with severity; targeting higher saturations worsened pulmonary outcomes in two large trials. Chorioamnionitis is strongly associated with impaired pulmonary and vascular growth.
- IVH occurs within 6 hours of birth in most cases and within 5 days in 90%. Incidence below 1000 g was 43% in 1990–1999 (13% grade III–IV) and 22% in 2000–2002 (3% severe). About 35% of grade III survivors have adverse neurologic outcome; disability reaches about 60% when posthemorrhagic hydrocephalus requires surgery; grade IV carries 80–90% poor outcome.
- Antenatal corticosteroids and maternal transfer to a tertiary center are the only proven IVH preventive strategies. Prophylactic indomethacin reduces severe IVH but not cerebral palsy.
- Posthemorrhagic hydrocephalus: serial lumbar punctures, diuretics, and intraventricular fibrinolysis are ineffective or harmful; shunt placement remains definitive.
- NEC affects 1–5% of NICU admissions and 4–13% of infants below 1500 g; onset is usually within 2–3 weeks; mortality is 10–30%, up to 50% with surgery. Bloody stools occur in 25%; pneumatosis intestinalis or portal venous gas is the radiographic hallmark; pneumoperitoneum is the single absolute indication for surgery.
- Human milk reduces NEC fourfold (relative risk 0.25, 95% CI 0.06–0.98). Indomethacin tocolysis has been associated with early-onset NEC (adjusted odds ratio 7.2, 95% CI 2.5–20.6) in one cohort, though meta-analysis found no association.
- Jaundice occurs in 60% of term and 80% of preterm infants in the first week; fewer than 3% exceed 15 mg/dL. Kernicterus occurred in 8% of infants with Rh disease and bilirubin 19–24 mg/dL, 33% at 25–29 mg/dL, and 73% at 30–40 mg/dL. No bilirubin level reliably predicts outcome, and 140 term infants with levels above 25 mg/dL without hemolysis had no kernicterus.
- Hyperbilirubinemia risk factors: jaundice in the first 24 hours, visible jaundice before discharge, previously jaundiced sibling, exclusive breastfeeding, bruising or cephalohematoma, East Asian/Mediterranean/Native American race, maternal age over 25, male sex, unrecognized hemolysis (ABO, Rh, anti-c, C, E, Kell), G6PD deficiency, and infant of a diabetic mother.
- Asphyxia: umbilical arterial pH below 7.00. The combination of a 5-minute Apgar score of 5 or less, delivery-room intubation or CPR, and cord pH below 7.00 confers a 340-fold increased risk of seizures with moderate to severe encephalopathy.
- Electronic fetal monitoring has a high false-positive rate for cerebral palsy and has not reduced its incidence; meconium-stained fluid and Apgar scores alone do not predict neurologic outcome.
- Therapeutic hypothermia reduces death or major neurodevelopmental disability at 18 months in term infants with moderate to severe HIE.
- Group B streptococcus colonizes 10–30% of pregnant women; intrapartum penicillin prophylaxis prevents early-onset disease, and most remaining cases occur in women with negative screening cultures.
- Hepatitis B: perinatal transmission is 70–90% when mothers are HBsAg and HBeAg positive and 5–20% when HBeAg negative; more than 90% of perinatally infected infants become chronic carriers.
- Congenital CMV infects about 1% of liveborn infants; 10–20% of infants of mothers with primary infection have neurodevelopmental impairment or hearing loss, and about 10% have profound findings at birth.

## Anti-patterns
- Equating improved survival at 23–24 weeks with improved intact survival.
- Delivering an extremely preterm infant at a non-tertiary center when maternal transfer is possible.
- Using Apgar scores alone to diagnose asphyxia or predict cerebral palsy.
- Relying on electronic fetal monitoring to prevent cerebral palsy.
- Withholding antenatal corticosteroids because delivery seems imminent.
- Treating progressive posthemorrhagic hydrocephalus with serial lumbar punctures or diuretics.
- Delaying surgery in NEC when pneumoperitoneum is present.
- Discharging a jaundiced infant without hour-of-life bilirubin interpretation and follow-up.

## Key Takeaways
1. Transfer mothers at risk of delivery before 32 weeks to a tertiary center and give antenatal corticosteroids; these are the most effective interventions for RDS and IVH.
2. Manage RDS with delivery-room CPAP, early surfactant for established disease, and low-tidal-volume ventilation.
3. Grade IVH by cranial ultrasound and counsel that grade III carries about 35% adverse outcome and grade IV 80–90% poor outcome.
4. Cool infants of 36 weeks or more with moderate to severe HIE; the therapeutic window is measured in hours.
5. Interpret neonatal bilirubin against hour of life and gestational age; treat with phototherapy or exchange transfusion per AAP nomograms, and follow up within days of discharge.
6. Prevent NEC with human milk and cautious use of indomethacin; operate urgently for pneumoperitoneum.
