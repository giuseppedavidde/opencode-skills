# Chapter 68: The Skin and Pregnancy

## Core Idea
Most cutaneous change in pregnancy is physiologic and regresses after delivery: hyperpigmentation, melasma, striae, vascular proliferation, and hair-cycle shifts. A smaller group of pregnancy-specific dermatoses requires diagnosis because treatment and fetal risk differ, and because intense pruritus can be the presenting sign of intrahepatic cholestasis rather than a primary rash. The chapter's practical argument is that the presence or absence of a primary lesion, the onset trimester, and the distribution — umbilicus, striae, extremities — separate the specific dermatoses without biopsy in most cases.

## Frameworks Introduced
**Diagnostic classification of pregnancy rashes.** (1) Pruritus gravidarum/intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy — pruritus without rash; (2) pruritic urticarial papules and plaques of pregnancy (PUPPP) or polymorphic eruption of pregnancy — urticarial papules in striae, sparing the umbilicus; (3) atopic eruption of pregnancy — prurigo gestationis and pruritic folliculitis, excoriated papules on extremities from mid-pregnancy; (4) pemphigoid gestationis — periumbilical urticarial plaques progressing to vesicles and bullae; (5) impetigo herpetiformis — sterile pustular psoriasis.

**Pruritus work-up.** Pruritus without rash → check bile acids and liver enzymes for cholestasis; pruritus confined to the anterior abdominal wall is usually striae-related distention, not cholestasis.

**Antihistamine and topical approach to pruritus.** Diphenhydramine is the traditional first-line; cetirizine and loratadine are relatively nonsedating alternatives (category B); hydroxyzine is not recommended in pregnancy; topical corticosteroids for localized inflammatory rashes.

## Key Concepts
- Hyperpigmentation occurs in at least 91% of pregnant women; it is most marked in areolae and genital skin. Linea nigra is midline abdominal hyperpigmentation.
- Melasma occurs in about 70% of pregnant women and persists for months or years in about 30%. Sun protection with SPF 15 or higher reduces formation.
- Striae occur in 50% to 80% of pregnancies. Risk factors include family history of striae and greater infant head circumference. No topical therapy prevents or alters their course.
- Vascular changes include telangiectasias, spider angiomas, palmar erythema, and pyogenic granuloma (lobular capillary hemangioma), including epulis gravidarum on the gums.
- Skin tags (acrochordons) are common and often persist postpartum; they can be snipped or electrocoagulated.
- Pruritus gravidarum with cholestasis affects about 0.02% of pregnancies. It is associated with twin pregnancy and carries increased rates of premature birth.
- PUPPP is the most common pregnancy rash. Itching is severe in 80%; lesions begin on the abdomen in 80–90%, often sparing the umbilicus; striae are involved in 67%. It is most common in primigravidas and is associated with increased weight gain, twins, hypertension, and induction of labor. It does not usually recur and does not increase fetal morbidity or mortality.
- PUPPP histology is nonspecific; direct immunofluorescence is usually negative, which distinguishes it from pemphigoid gestationis. Topical corticosteroids are adequate for most patients.
- Atopic eruption of pregnancy (formerly prurigo gestationis and pruritic folliculitis) begins in mid-pregnancy with excoriated papules or nodules on the extremities and usually clears by 3 months postpartum. Recurrence is low.
- Pemphigoid gestationis is a rare autoimmune blistering dermatosis with strong HLA-B8 and DR3/DR4 association. It begins in the second or third trimester, often around the umbilicus, and spares the face and mucous membranes. Reported infant mortality may be as high as 30% but is probably inflated; prematurity and small-for-gestational-age infants are increased.
- Transient urticarial and vesicular lesions from transplacental IgG4 transfer occur in 5% to 50% of infants of affected mothers.
- Dapsone is contraindicated in pregnancy because it can cause hemolytic disease of the newborn. Prednisone, plasmapheresis, and intravenous immunoglobulin are used for severe pemphigoid gestationis; monitor infants of prednisone-treated mothers for adrenal insufficiency.
- Impetigo herpetiformis is pustular psoriasis of pregnancy, usually beginning in the third trimester, with hundreds of sterile translucent pustules on erythematous plaques in axillae, inframammary folds, umbilicus, groin, and gluteal cleft. It is associated with hypoparathyroidism, hypocalcemia, hypophosphatemia, low vitamin D, elevated ESR, and leukocytosis. Complications include fever, chills, nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, dehydration, delirium, tetany, convulsions, and death from hypocalcemia or septicemia.
- Treatment of impetigo herpetiformis: prednisone 20–40 mg/day controls new lesions; antibiotics for secondary infection; intravenous fluids and electrolytes; cyclosporine for severe cases. Acitretin and methotrexate are contraindicated.
- Autoimmune progesterone dermatitis is a rare cyclic luteal-phase eruption; intradermal progesterone testing (0.01% to 1% in 60% ethanol-saline) with readings at 10, 30 minutes, 4, 24, and 48 hours; a reaction persisting 24–48 hours is positive.
- Acne in pregnancy: benzoyl peroxide (category C), azelaic acid (B), topical erythromycin or clindamycin (B), topical metronidazole (B). Avoid sulfonamides near term, tetracycline (fatty liver, dental staining), and oral isotretinoin (teratogenic). Topical tazarotene is category X.
- Psoriasis affects 1% to 2% of the population; the pustular form is managed as impetigo herpetiformis; topical corticosteroids are the mainstay in pregnancy.
- Melanoma: pregnancy can enlarge nevi and produce new nevi, but melanoma incidence is not increased. Suspicious lesions (asymmetry, irregular border, variegated color, diameter >6 mm) should be excised immediately; lidocaine is safe.

## Anti-patterns
- Treating generalized pruritus in pregnancy as an allergy without checking bile acids and liver enzymes.
- Diagnosing PUPPP when the umbilicus is involved and bullae are present; consider pemphigoid gestationis and obtain immunofluorescence.
- Using the term herpes gestationis, which invites inappropriate antiviral treatment.
- Prescribing dapsone for pemphigoid gestationis in pregnancy.
- Using tetracycline or oral isotretinoin for acne in pregnancy.
- Using acitretin or methotrexate for pustular psoriasis in pregnancy.
- Reassuring that striae can be prevented with topical therapy; no agent alters their course.
- Delaying excision of a suspicious pigmented lesion until after delivery.

## Key Takeaways
1. Treat pruritus without rash as possible cholestasis: obtain bile acids and liver enzymes before attributing it to dry skin or striae.
2. Diagnose PUPPP clinically — abdominal striae, umbilical sparing, primigravida, third trimester — and treat with topical corticosteroids; fetal risk is not increased.
3. Suspect pemphigoid gestationis with periumbilical urticarial plaques and bullae; confirm with immunofluorescence and treat severe cases with prednisone.
4. Recognize impetigo herpetiformis as pustular psoriasis with hypocalcemia; treat with prednisone 20–40 mg/day and correct electrolytes.
5. Recommend SPF 15 or higher for melasma and reassure that most pigmentary change regresses, though 30% persists for months to years.
6. Excise suspicious nevi promptly using lidocaine; pregnancy does not increase melanoma incidence but can delay diagnosis.
