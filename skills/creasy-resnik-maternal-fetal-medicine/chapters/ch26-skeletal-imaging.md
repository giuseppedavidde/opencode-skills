# Chapter 26: Skeletal Imaging

## Core Idea
Skeletal anomalies are approached as patterns: abnormal limb posturing, abnormal digit number, abnormal foot position, spinal dysraphism, sacrococcygeal masses, and short-limb dysplasia. A single short femur is common and nonspecific, so the diagnosis of skeletal dysplasia requires a systematic survey of every long bone, bone shape, mineralization, thorax size, and craniofacial features. Prognosis turns on whether the dysplasia is lethal, which is predicted by extreme micromelia, a small thorax, poor mineralization, and specific circumference ratios rather than by the name of the syndrome.

## Frameworks Introduced
**Skeletal dysplasia survey (modified Krakow guidelines).** Confirm dates; measure all long bones bilaterally (femur, humerus, radius, ulna, tibia, fibula, clavicle) plus foot length, scapula, and head/abdomen/chest/cardiac circumferences. Calculate four ratios: thoracic circumference-to-abdominal circumference (normal ≥0.8), cardiac-to-thoracic circumference (normal <0.6), femur length-to-abdominal circumference (normal >0.16), and femur-to-foot length (normal ≥1.0). Note bone shape, echogenicity, metaphyses, posturing, digits, cranium, vertebral bodies, profile, and associated anomalies.

**Limb shortening classification.** Rhizomelia (humerus, femur), mesomelia (radius, ulna, tibia, fibula), acromelia (hands, feet), micromelia (whole limb; mild <2 SD, severe <3 SD).

**Lethal skeletal dysplasia criteria.** Early severe micromelia (<3 SD), femur length-to-abdominal circumference ratio below the normal threshold, thoracic circumference <5th percentile, thoracic circumference-to-abdominal circumference <0.79, cardiac-to-thoracic circumference >0.60, and poor bone mineralization. Lethal diagnoses include achondrogenesis, homozygous achondroplasia, thanatophoric dysplasia, osteogenesis imperfecta type II, hypophosphatasia, short-rib polydactyly, and variably camptomelic dysplasia and chondrodysplasia punctata.

**Sacrococcygeal teratoma (AAPSS) types.** Type I external with minimal presacral involvement; type II external with intrapelvic extension; type III external with abdominal extension; type IV presacral with no external component.

## Key Concepts
- Arthrogryposis: 1/3000 live births; two or more fixed joint contractures; caused by akinesia from intrinsic, extrinsic (oligohydramnios, teratogen), neuromuscular, connective tissue, infectious, or maternal myasthenic causes. Fixed "waiter's tip" wrist, clenched hands, and crossed legs are typical.
- Polydactyly: 1/3000; preaxial (radial/tibial), postaxial (ulnar/fibular), central. Postaxial is 10 times more common in African Americans (1/300 live births). Isolated in 85%; associated anomalies in 20% of preaxial and 12% of postaxial cases.
- Clubfoot (talipes equinovarus): 1–3/1000, male-to-female 2:1, two thirds bilateral. Prenatal false-positive rate up to 30%. Diagnosis requires tibia and fibula in coronal plane with the sole visible in the same plane, persisting through the examination. Two thirds are isolated; up to 30% have aneuploidy when other anomalies are present. About 90% need orthopedic treatment; 10% are positional; about 40% need tendon-release surgery.
- Open neural tube defect (ONTD): about 1/1000 live births; neural tube closes by day 25 after conception. Associated with elevated maternal serum alpha-fetoprotein. Lemon sign, banana sign, ventriculomegaly (atrial width ≥10 mm), and clubfeet. Aneuploidy in 10%; other anomalies in 15–30% of euploid fetuses. Fetoscopic repair halves shunt placement and improves motor function but raises preterm delivery to 80% versus 15%. Perinatal mortality for isolated ONTD is 10–15%.
- Sacrococcygeal teratoma: 1/40,000, female-to-male 3:1; solid or cystic mass extending from coccyx; high-output hydrops, placentomegaly, polyhydramnios. Sacral agenesis: 1–5/100,000, classically about 1% of diabetic pregnancies; abrupt termination of the spine with hypoplastic, crossed, clubbed lower limbs.
- Skeletal dysplasia: 2.4/10,000 live births; more than 350 disorders. Achondroplasia, the most common dwarfism, may not be detectable until the third trimester. Thanatophoric type I shows "telephone receiver" femurs; type II shows a cloverleaf skull.
- Long-bone reference values: mean standard deviation about 2.5–3.0 mm; femur 50th percentile is approximately 8 mm at 12 weeks, 20 mm at 16 weeks, 36 mm at 22 weeks, 44 mm at 25 weeks, 56 mm at 30 weeks, and 74 mm at 40 weeks.

## Anti-patterns
- Calling a short femur a dysplasia without measuring all bones and calculating the thoracic and femur ratios.
- Excluding achondroplasia in the second trimester; it commonly appears only in the third trimester.
- Treating a transient abnormal foot posture as clubfoot; the deformity must persist throughout the scan.
- Missing associated anomalies once one defect is found. In ONTD, examine the cranium and spine; in polydactyly, examine the heart, kidneys, and skull.
- Planning vaginal delivery in a fetus with fractures or poor mineralization without considering cesarean delivery.
- Predicting lethality from a syndrome name alone; use the objective lethal criteria.

## Key Takeaways
1. Measure every long bone and compute thoracic/abdominal, cardiac/thoracic, femur/abdominal, and femur/foot ratios in any fetus with a short femur.
2. Use the lethal criteria to counsel: severe micromelia, thoracic circumference <5th percentile, ratio <0.79, cardiac/thoracic >0.60, poor mineralization.
3. Count digits and examine hands and feet in every anatomic survey; polydactyly plus a cardiac defect suggests trisomy 13.
4. Consider sacral agenesis when the spine ends abruptly, especially in a diabetic pregnancy.
5. Refer suspected skeletal dysplasia for genetic and molecular testing, and consider cesarean delivery when fractures or poor mineralization are present.
