# Chapter 36: Hemolytic Disease of the Fetus and Newborn

## Core Idea
Red cell alloimmunization occurs when fetal erythrocytes bearing a paternal antigen enter the maternal circulation and provoke an IgG response. Maternal IgG crosses the placenta, binds fetal red cells, and causes extravascular hemolysis in the fetal spleen, producing anemia that progresses to hydrops and death if untreated. Prevention with anti-D immune globulin has reduced but not eliminated RhD disease, and non-D antibodies now account for a substantial share of cases. The chapter's clinical argument is that anemia can be monitored noninvasively with middle cerebral artery peak systolic velocity, so invasive testing is reserved for fetuses with evidence of moderate to severe anemia.

## Frameworks Introduced
**Anemia surveillance pathway.** Antibody screen at the first prenatal visit. In an alloimmunized pregnancy, obtain a maternal titer. Critical titer: 1:32 for anti-D and most other antibodies; 1:8 for anti-Kell. Below the critical value, repeat monthly until 24 weeks, then every 2 weeks. Above the critical value, begin serial middle cerebral artery peak systolic velocity (MCA-PSV) measurements.

**MCA-PSV interpretation.** A value >1.5 multiples of the median (MoM) predicts moderate to severe anemia, defined as fetal hemoglobin <0.65 MoM. Values are reported in MoM to account for gestational age. The positive likelihood ratio is 8.45 (95% CI 4.7–15.6) and the negative likelihood ratio is 0.02 (95% CI 0.001–0.25). Overall accuracy is 85%, compared with 76% for the Liley curve and 81% for the Queenan curve.

**Intrauterine transfusion (IUT) approaches.** Intravascular transfusion (IVT) through the placental cord insertion or intrahepatic vein is preferred; intraperitoneal transfusion (IPT) remains an option when cord access fails in a nonhydropic fetus. Transfusion volume is calculated from the target hematocrit increase and a transfusion coefficient; IVT is often carried to a final hematocrit of 50–65%, accepting a decline of about 1% per day. A combined approach (IVT to 35–40% followed by IPT) slows the daily decline.

**Neonatal follow-up thresholds.** Top-up transfusion for hematocrit <30% in the symptomatic infant or <20% in the asymptomatic infant.

## Key Concepts
- Epidemiology: widespread anti-D prophylaxis has reduced RhD alloimmunization, but cases persist from first-trimester sensitization, omitted prophylaxis, and inadequate dosing after large fetomaternal hemorrhage.
- In a Dutch analysis of 305,000 women in a first-trimester screening program, anti-E was the most common antibody associated with hemolytic disease, followed by anti-D, anti-Kell, and anti-c.
- Pathophysiology: reticulocytosis begins when the fetal hemoglobin deficit exceeds 2 g/dL; erythroblastemia appears at a deficit of 7 g/dL or greater. Hydrops, defined as fluid in at least two serous compartments, typically occurs at a hemoglobin deficit of 7 g/dL or greater. Ascites is usually the first sonographic sign, followed by pleural effusion and scalp edema.
- Fetal sex affects outcome: the chance of hydrops was 13-fold higher in RhD-positive male fetuses, with an adjusted odds ratio for perinatal mortality of 3.38.
- Fetal RHD genotyping from cell-free DNA in maternal plasma has a sensitivity of 97.2%, specificity of 96.8%, and accuracy of 97.1% in one series. When the father is heterozygous, this eliminates testing for the 50% of fetuses who are antigen-negative. Amniocentesis for fetal DNA is an alternative for other antibodies.
- Kell: K1 is present on red cells of 9% of whites and 2% of blacks; about 97.8% of white antigen-positive individuals are heterozygous. Because anti-Kell causes both splenic sequestration and suppression of fetal erythropoiesis, a lower critical titer of 1:8 is used.
- Anti-C, anti-c, anti-E, anti-e, anti-Duffy, anti-Kidd, and anti-MNS antibodies vary in severity; anti-c can cause severe disease, anti-C titers may equal or exceed anti-D titers, and anti-Duffy and anti-Kidd usually cause mild disease.
- RhIG: 300 µg at 28 weeks and again postpartum, with the postpartum dose based on fetomaternal hemorrhage testing. RhIG should be given after any invasive procedure in an Rh-negative unsensitized woman.
- IUT technique: access the cord at its placental insertion. Umbilical artery puncture causes bradycardia in 21% versus 3% for the umbilical vein; intrahepatic vein access has a 2.3% bradycardia rate but is technically harder. Avoid the cord midsegment, which carries a 2.5-fold higher complication rate.
- Outcomes: in the largest reported series, overall perinatal survival after IUT was 89%; hydrops reduced survival to 78%; procedure-related loss was 1.6% per procedure. Perinatal loss was 3.4% for IUTs before 32 weeks and 1% after 32 weeks. Most centers perform the final IUT up to 35 weeks and deliver at 37–38 weeks.
- Hydrops usually reverses after one or two intravascular transfusions. After IVT, up to 75% of infants may need top-up transfusions during the first 1–3 months because passively acquired antibody persists.
- Long-term outcome after IUT for hydropic fetuses: cerebral palsy 2.1%, severe developmental delay 3.1%, bilateral deafness 1.0% — above general-population rates of 0.2–0.7% for cerebral palsy and 2.3% for severe delay. Severe hydrops is the only independent preoperative predictor of neurodevelopmental impairment (OR 11.2).

## Anti-patterns
- Using maternal titers to guide surveillance in a previously affected pregnancy. Start MCA-PSV at 16–18 weeks regardless of titer.
- Using a critical titer of 1:32 for anti-Kell; the threshold is 1:8.
- Relying on the Liley curve alone when MCA-PSV is available and technically adequate.
- Measuring MCA-PSV during fetal activity or with an angle of insonation that is not near zero; accelerations can falsely lower the peak velocity.
- Transfusing to a hematocrit above 65% without accounting for the rise in whole-blood viscosity that begins above 50%.
- Omitting RhIG after amniocentesis, CVS, PUBS, or any fetomaternal hemorrhage in an Rh-negative unsensitized woman.
- Assuming a negative cell-free DNA result is valid when the fetal fraction is too low to confirm the presence of fetal DNA.

## Key Takeaways
1. Screen all pregnancies for red cell antibodies; use a critical titer of 1:32 for anti-D and 1:8 for anti-Kell.
2. In a previously affected pregnancy, skip titers and start MCA-PSV surveillance at 16–18 weeks.
3. Use MCA-PSV >1.5 MoM as the indication for cordocentesis and possible IUT.
4. Give RhIG 300 µg at 28 weeks and postpartum, with dosing guided by fetomaternal hemorrhage testing.
5. Counsel that IUT yields about 89% survival overall and 78% with hydrops, with 1.6% procedure-related loss.
