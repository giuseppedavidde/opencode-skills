# Chapter 48: Pregnancy-Related Hypertension

## Core Idea
Hypertensive disorders of pregnancy are classified by timing and by the presence of proteinuria or end-organ involvement, and the classification determines management. Preeclampsia is a systemic endothelial disorder that begins long before clinical signs, so treatment directed at blood pressure or natriuresis after diagnosis does not reverse the disease. Delivery is the only definitive treatment, and the clinical task is to time it so that maternal end-organ damage is prevented while prematurity is minimized.

## Frameworks Introduced

**NIH Working Group classification.** Four diagnoses: chronic hypertension, gestational hypertension, preeclampsia-eclampsia, and preeclampsia superimposed on chronic hypertension.

**Definitions.** Chronic hypertension: persistent blood pressure above 140/90 before pregnancy or before 20 weeks, or persisting beyond 84 days postpartum. Gestational hypertension: new-onset elevation without proteinuria; normalization by 12 weeks postpartum makes it transient hypertension of pregnancy. Preeclampsia: new-onset 140/90 or higher on two measurements plus proteinuria of 300 mg or more in 24 hours or 30 mg/dL (1+ dipstick).

**Severe preeclampsia criteria.** Systolic 160 mm Hg or higher or diastolic 110 mm Hg or higher on two occasions at least 6 hours apart on bed rest; proteinuria 5 g or more in 24 hours or 3+ or greater on two random samples at least 4 hours apart; oliguria under 500 mL in 24 hours; cerebral or visual disturbances; pulmonary edema or cyanosis; epigastric or right upper quadrant pain; impaired liver function; thrombocytopenia; fetal growth restriction.

**Eclampsia.** Seizures not attributable to another cause in a woman with preeclampsia. Edema is not a criterion because it occurs in 10% to 15% of normotensive pregnancies.

**Magnesium sulfate protocol.** Loading dose 4 to 6 g intravenously over 15 to 30 minutes, then 1 to 2 g per hour by mechanically controlled infusion. Monitor deep tendon reflexes and respiratory status at least every 2 hours. Limit to 1 g per hour if creatinine exceeds 1.0 mg/dL. Antidote is calcium gluconate 10 mL of 10% solution intravenously over 3 minutes.

**Hypertensive emergency protocol.** Treat persistent systolic above 160 mm Hg or diastolic above 105 to 110 mm Hg for more than 15 minutes. Goal is 135 to 145 mm Hg systolic and 95 to 100 mm Hg diastolic. Options: hydralazine 5 to 20 mg intravenously every 3 to 6 hours, labetalol 20 to 80 mg intravenously every 3 to 6 hours, nifedipine 10 mg orally every 4 to 8 hours, or sodium nitroprusside infusion 0.25 to 8 micrograms/kg per minute.

## Key Concepts
- Preeclampsia occurs in about 4% of pregnancies; two thirds of cases are nulliparous. A rise of 30 mm Hg systolic or 15 mm Hg diastolic is no longer diagnostic because it does not predict adverse outcome.
- Delivery is the only definitive treatment. Therapy before delivery is justified only to allow fetal maturation.
- Delivery should be considered for all severe preeclampsia at or beyond 32 to 34 weeks. Before 32 to 34 weeks, expectant management in a tertiary center may be attempted with corticosteroids and daily reassessment.
- At 36 to 41 weeks, a randomized trial showed progressive reduction in maternal risk with delivery after 37 weeks.
- The vaginal delivery rate after induction falls to about 33% before 28 weeks; some recommend scheduled cesarean under 30 weeks with an unfavorable Bishop score.
- Magnesium sulfate is more effective than phenytoin or benzodiazepines for eclamptic seizures. A breakthrough seizure is terminated with diazepam 5 to 10 mg, lorazepam 4 mg, or pentobarbital 125 mg intravenously after airway protection. Magnesium needs no titration to a specific level; fetal levels approximate maternal, and preterm dosing provides cerebral palsy neuroprotection.
- Antihypertensives are not routine; they do not prevent seizures, and the goal is prevention of intracranial bleeding and stroke.
- Diuretics are avoided because they further contract plasma volume. Oliguria is managed with cautious fluid, targeting urine output of 20 to 30 mL per hour.
- Eclampsia mortality fell from 20% to 30% with expectant management, to 10% to 15% with sedation, about 5% with magnesium, and near zero with magnesium plus antihypertensives and timely delivery.
- Recurrence after preeclampsia delivering after 37 weeks is 14.1%; after delivery before 28 weeks it is 37.9% (OR 3.90). After eclampsia, 33% have a later hypertensive disorder and 5% recurrent eclampsia.
- Low-dose aspirin reduces preeclampsia; 72 women must be treated to prevent one case, with a 14% reduction in fetal and neonatal deaths (NNT 243). PARIS found a 10% reduction, with NNT 500 in low-risk and 50 in high-risk women for preterm birth.

## Anti-patterns
- Excluding HELLP because blood pressure and protein are normal.
- Treating hypertension aggressively to "cure" preeclampsia; the disease progresses until delivery.
- Using diuretics or large hypotonic fluid loads in oliguric preeclampsia.
- Giving antihypertensives for mild hypertension in preeclampsia; treatment is reserved for severe ranges.

## Key Takeaways
1. Classify every hypertensive pregnant patient into chronic, gestational, preeclamptic, or superimposed preeclampsia at diagnosis.
2. Diagnose preeclampsia with 140/90 or higher plus 300 mg protein in 24 hours; classify severe disease by the listed end-organ criteria.
3. Give magnesium sulfate 4 to 6 g load then 1 to 2 g per hour for seizure prophylaxis in severe disease and for eclampsia.
4. Treat severe hypertension above 160/105 to 110 mm Hg with hydralazine, labetalol, or nifedipine, aiming for 135 to 145/95 to 100.
5. Deliver at 37 weeks or later for preeclampsia at term, at 32 to 34 weeks or later for severe disease, and individualize earlier delivery after corticosteroids in a tertiary center.
6. Counsel recurrence risk based on gestational age at prior delivery, start low-dose aspirin in high-risk women, and arrange long-term cardiovascular follow-up.
