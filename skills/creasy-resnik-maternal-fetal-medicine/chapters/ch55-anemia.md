# Chapter 55: Anemia and Pregnancy

## Core Idea

Anemia is a sign, not a diagnosis, and its cause matters more than its severity. Pregnancy produces dilutional anemia from plasma volume expansion, which is benign, while iron deficiency, folate or vitamin B12 deficiency, hemoglobinopathy, hemolysis, and marrow disease each require different treatment. The CDC defines anemia in pregnancy as hemoglobin below 11 g/dL in the first and third trimesters and below 10.5 g/dL in the second. Iron deficiency causes about 75% of anemia in pregnancy, and hemoglobin alone is an inadequate screen because most iron-depleted women are not yet anemic. Evaluation therefore pairs the complete blood count with the reticulocyte count and iron studies.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **CDC pregnancy-specific hemoglobin thresholds.** Below 11 g/dL in the first and third trimesters; below 10.5 g/dL in the second trimester. The nonpregnant adult female reference is 14.0 ± 2.0 g/dL.
- **Physiologic classification (Box 55-1).** Anemia from dilution, decreased red cell production (marrow failure, erythropoietin deficiency, iron, folate, or B12 deficiency), increased red cell loss (acute or chronic hemorrhage), or increased destruction (intrinsic membrane, enzyme, or hemoglobin disorders; acquired immune, mechanical, or infectious causes).
- **Morphologic and kinetic classification.** Microcytic, normocytic, or macrocytic red cells combined with a low or high reticulocyte count. Hypochromic microcytes with a low reticulocyte count suggest iron deficiency, thalassemia trait, sideroblastic anemia, or lead poisoning; oval macrocytes with hypersegmented neutrophils suggest B12 or folate deficiency; spherocytes with reticulocytosis suggest hereditary spherocytosis.
- **Iron deficiency laboratory criteria.** Low serum ferritin (12 µg/L or lower is consistent, below 20 µg/L is generally diagnostic), low serum iron, high total iron-binding capacity, elevated free erythrocyte protoporphyrin, and microcytic hypochromic indices. Ferritin behaves as an acute-phase reactant and rises after iron ingestion, so it should be drawn before treatment.
- **Thalassemia screening algorithm (Figure 55-3).** Mean corpuscular volume below 80 fL triggers hemoglobin electrophoresis and iron studies. Hemoglobin A2 above 3.5% with normal iron studies suggests beta-thalassemia; a normal A2 with low iron studies suggests iron deficiency; persistent microcytosis with normal iron studies suggests alpha-thalassemia, confirmed by family studies, deletion testing, or cord blood for hemoglobin Barts.
- **Deficiency definitions.** Folate deficiency: serum folate below 2.5 to 3.0 ng/mL, red cell folate below 165 ng/mL. Vitamin B12 deficiency: serum level below 160 to 200 pg/mL.
- **Iron replacement protocol.** Ferrous sulfate 325 mg one to three times daily; WHO 60 mg elemental iron daily with folic acid (30 mg may suffice); ascorbic acid 500 mg with each dose to enhance absorption; continue for 6 months after the anemia resolves.

## Key Concepts

- WHO estimates that 20% to 52% of pregnant women are anemic. In US women under 50 years, anemia (hemoglobin below 12 g/dL) affects 6.9%, and 24% of black versus 3% of white women.
- At least 20% of prenatal patients are anemic at some point; 32% of women presenting before 7 weeks have hemoglobin below 12 g/dL.
- One third of women in early pregnancy have ferritin below 20 µg/L and 15% have ferritin below 12 µg/L, but only 6% of those with ferritin below 20 µg/L are anemic (hemoglobin below 11 g/dL).
- Total body iron deficiency rises from 18% in the first trimester to 29% in the third trimester.
- Menstrual loss of 25 to 30 mL blood equals 12 to 15 mg elemental iron, requiring absorption of 1.5 to 2.0 mg daily. Only about 10% of dietary iron is absorbed, and the average diet contains 6 mg per 1000 kcal.
- Blood volume rises about 36%, reaching its maximum at 34 weeks. Plasma volume rises 47% while red cell mass rises only 17%, producing physiologic hemodilution.
- Reticulocytosis appears 7 to 10 days after starting iron, and hemoglobin can rise by up to 1 g per week in severe anemia.
- Parenteral iron is reserved for malabsorption or refusal of oral iron with hemoglobin below 8.5 g/dL. Anaphylaxis occurs in about 1% with iron dextran, so a test dose is required.
- Oral iron and intravenous iron sucrose produce a similar 2 g/dL hemoglobin rise.
- Severe anemia (hemoglobin below 4.7 g/dL) in Africa, Asia, and Latin America raises maternal mortality with a relative risk of 3.5. Moderate anemia (4.0 to 8.0 g/dL) is not associated with increased mortality.
- WHO attributes 18% of maternal mortality to anemia, mostly iron deficiency.
- Hemoglobin above 14.5 g/dL early in pregnancy is associated with stillbirth, low birth weight, and small-for-gestational-age infants, probably as a marker of failed plasma volume expansion.
- Folic acid 400 µg daily reduces neural tube defects by 36%; 5 mg daily reduces them by 85%. After a prior affected pregnancy, use 4 mg daily starting at least 4 weeks before conception through the first 3 months.
- Sickle cell trait affects about 1 in 12 African Americans. Sickle cell anemia requires an extra 1.0 mg/day of folic acid, pneumococcal vaccination if not given within the past year, and discontinuation of hydroxyurea.

## Anti-patterns

- Screening for iron deficiency with hemoglobin alone; most depleted women are not yet anemic.
- Treating dilutional anemia with escalating iron without checking ferritin.
- Measuring ferritin after starting iron, when it is falsely elevated.
- Prescribing oral iron in a patient with malabsorption or intolerance instead of parenteral iron.
- Missing thalassemia trait in a microcytic anemia with normal iron studies.
- Transfusing a stable pregnant woman with mild anemia instead of identifying and treating the cause.
- Continuing hydroxyurea during pregnancy.
- Assuming a high hemoglobin in early pregnancy is reassuring; it may signal poor plasma volume expansion.

## Key Takeaways

1. Check a complete blood count at the first prenatal visit and again at 24 to 28 weeks.
2. Add serum ferritin when hemoglobin is low or when iron deficiency is suspected; a value below 20 µg/L is diagnostic.
3. Treat iron deficiency with ferrous sulfate 325 mg one to three times daily, co-administered with 500 mg ascorbic acid, and continue 6 months after hemoglobin normalizes.
4. Reserve parenteral iron for malabsorption, intolerance, or refusal with hemoglobin below 8.5 g/dL, and give a test dose of iron dextran.
5. Prescribe 400 µg folic acid daily for all women planning pregnancy, and 4 mg daily after a prior neural tube defect.
6. Screen microcytic anemia with hemoglobin electrophoresis and iron studies to separate thalassemia trait from iron deficiency.
7. In sickle cell anemia, give 1.0 mg/day folic acid, vaccinate against pneumococcus, stop hydroxyurea, and monitor growth with serial ultrasound.
