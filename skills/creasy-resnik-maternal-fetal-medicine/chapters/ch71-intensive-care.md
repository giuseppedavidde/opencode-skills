# Chapter 71: Intensive Care Considerations for the Critically Ill Parturient

## Core Idea
Fewer than 1% of pregnant women require intensive care, but when they do, the presentation is deceptive: normal pregnancy expands blood volume 40–50%, so severe hemorrhage can occur with near-normal vital signs, and standard illness-severity scores overestimate mortality in young parturients. The chapter's central claim is that outcome depends less on the specific diagnosis than on recognizing physiologic decompensation early, positioning the patient off the vena cava, and applying general critical care principles with pregnancy-specific modifications. Most obstetric ICU deaths are considered avoidable.

## Frameworks Introduced
**Clinical staging of hemorrhagic shock.** Because of pregnancy hypervolemia, orthostatic hypotension and tachycardia appear only after 25–30% of blood volume is lost; staging is based on estimated loss, vital signs, mental status, and urine output rather than on hemoglobin alone.

**ARDSNet lung-protective ventilation.** Tidal volume 6 mL/kg predicted body weight versus 12 mL/kg, with plateau pressure goal ≤30 cm H₂O versus ≤50 cm H₂O; volume-control mode is the standard for parturients.

**Perimortem cesarean sequence.** Begin CPR and secure the airway; establish IV access; deliver by cesarean if there is no return of spontaneous circulation by 4 minutes; sterile technique is unnecessary and the patient should not be moved to an operating room; continue CPR during and after delivery; continuous fetal monitoring is not feasible.

**Maternal cardiac arrest with viable gestation.** Left uterine displacement, cricoid pressure until the airway is secured, chest compressions, full ACLS drug doses, and delivery within 4–5 minutes.

**AFE diagnostic framework.** Acute hypoxia, right ventricular dysfunction, hypotension or arrest, and coagulopathy during labor, delivery, or within 30 minutes of delivery; exclude hemorrhage, uterine rupture, and sepsis, which produce the same picture.

## Key Concepts
- Maternal mortality in the United States was 14.5 per 100,000 live births (10.2 white, 37.5 African American), up from 11.8 per 100,000 in 1991–1997. Sixty percent of deaths occur after a live birth; 13% antepartum; 3% related to abortion; 3% ectopic.
- ICU admission follows obstetric complications in 47–93% of cases, chiefly hypertensive disorders and hemorrhage. Overall ICU mortality is 0–20%, usually under 5%. In developing countries median ICU mortality is 14% versus 3.4% in developed countries.
- APACHE I, SAPS II, and MPM II all overestimate mortality in obstetric ICU patients (predicted higher despite actual mortality 10.8% versus 10.4% in nonpregnant controls).
- Obstetric hemorrhage: more than 500 mL after vaginal delivery or more than 1,000 mL after cesarean. Massive hemorrhage is more than one blood volume in 24 hours; treat with a 1:1:1 ratio of packed red cells, fresh frozen plasma, and platelets.
- Initial hemorrhagic shock resuscitation: 2 L crystalloid, then packed red cells; permissive hypotension with systolic pressure 80–90 mm Hg until bleeding is controlled.
- One unit of packed red cells raises hemoglobin about 1 g/dL in a 70-kg adult. Transfuse for acute loss greater than 30% of blood volume or hemoglobin 6–10 g/dL with tachycardia and hypotension.
- Dextran 70 (500 mL of 6%) expands intravascular volume by more than 1,000 mL; anaphylaxis occurs in 1 in 3,300 and doses above 20 mL/kg/24 h impair clotting. A 25-g albumin infusion expands volume by about 450 mL over 60 minutes but leaks into the interstitium in sepsis, trauma, and preeclampsia.
- Amniotic fluid embolism occurs in 1.9–6 per 100,000 deliveries. Mortality has fallen from 61% in early reports to 22%. Fetal debris in the pulmonary circulation is not diagnostic — it is found in most normally laboring patients and in only 78% of confirmed AFE cases.
- Risk factors for AFE include age older than 35, medical induction, cesarean delivery, operative vaginal delivery, cervical trauma, uterine rupture, placenta previa, abruption, polyhydramnios, eclampsia, and fetal distress; causality is unproven.
- ARDSNet trial: 861 patients, mortality 31% with 6 mL/kg and plateau ≤30 cm H₂O versus 40% with 12 mL/kg and ≤50 cm H₂O, with shorter intubation in the low-tidal-volume group.
- Airway pressure release ventilation and prone positioning improve oxygenation in ARDS but have not shown mortality benefit; neither has been studied adequately in pregnancy.
- Pulmonary edema complicates about 1 in 1,000 pregnancies; 47% antepartum, 39% postpartum, 14% intrapartum. Cardiac disease and tocolytic use each account for 25.5% of cases.
- Respiratory failure in pregnancy most often results from pulmonary edema, infection, and pulmonary embolism. Of 43 ventilated gravidas, 86% delivered during admission, 65% by cesarean, with 36% mortality after cesarean; overall maternal and perinatal mortality were 14% and 11%. Delivery reduced FiO₂ by only 28% in one series and is not routinely indicated for maternal respiratory failure.
- Trauma complicates 6–7% of pregnancies; about 4 per 1,000 deliveries require hospitalization; 24–38% of admitted patients deliver during that admission; nearly 4,000 fetuses are lost annually. Domestic violence affects an estimated 20% of pregnant patients.
- Placental abruption occurs in about 7% after trauma, often after minor injury and without vaginal bleeding. Ultrasound sensitivity for abruption is only 25%, so a negative scan does not exclude it.
- Minor trauma in the first or second trimester is independently associated with fetal demise (adjusted odds ratio 1.78 and 1.65).
- Uterine compression of the aorta and vena cava begins at about 20 weeks; use manual displacement, lateral positioning, or a hip wedge.
- Diagnostic peritoneal lavage is positive with 10 mL of aspirated blood; otherwise infuse 1 L saline and examine for blood, bile, or bowel contents.
- Burns: maternal mortality 39% and fetal mortality 45% in a 9-year referral series; the strongest predictors are total body surface area above 40% and inhalation injury. Mean burn area was 38% in survivors and 69% in nonsurvivors.
- Sepsis: give broad-spectrum antibiotics within 1 hour; target MAP above 65 mm Hg, CVP 8–12 mm Hg, urine output at least 0.5 mL/kg/h, and central venous oxygen saturation at least 70%.
- Brain death in pregnancy is rare; the usual cause is subarachnoid hemorrhage. Options are immediate delivery, withdrawal of support, or prolonged somatic support to advance gestational age.

## Anti-patterns
- Relying on maternal vital signs or hemoglobin to gauge blood loss; hypervolemia masks 25–30% loss.
- Using APACHE or SAPS to counsel families in obstetric critical illness; they systematically overestimate death.
- Attributing collapse with coagulopathy to AFE without excluding hemorrhage, uterine rupture, and sepsis.
- Excluding abruption because ultrasound is normal; sensitivity is 25%.
- Transporting an arresting mother to the operating room before perimortem cesarean; delivery rates within 5 minutes fall from 57% to 14%.
- Delivering a ventilated mother routinely for respiratory failure alone; benefit is inconsistent and cesarean carries added mortality.
- Leaving a patient supine beyond 20 weeks, causing aortocaval compression.
- Using large-volume crystalloid in hemorrhage instead of early balanced blood products.

## Key Takeaways
1. Position any critically ill parturient beyond 20 weeks off the vena cava, and treat hypotension as hemorrhage until proven otherwise.
2. Activate a massive transfusion protocol at 1:1:1 for blood loss exceeding one blood volume, with permissive hypotension to systolic 80–90 mm Hg.
3. Use 6 mL/kg tidal volume and plateau pressure ≤30 cm H₂O for ARDS in pregnancy.
4. For suspected AFE, resuscitate supportively, correct coagulopathy, and consider perimortem cesarean after 4 minutes without return of circulation.
5. After trauma, monitor for at least 24 hours for abruption even with minor injury and a normal ultrasound.
6. In sepsis, give antibiotics within 1 hour and target MAP above 65 mm Hg and urine output above 0.5 mL/kg/h.
