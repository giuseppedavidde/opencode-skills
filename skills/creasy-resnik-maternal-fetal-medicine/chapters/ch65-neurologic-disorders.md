# Chapter 65: Neurologic Disorders

## Core Idea
Neurologic disease in pregnancy is rarely modified in a predictable way by gestation; the exception is autoimmune disease, which tends to remit during pregnancy and rebound postpartum. The chapter's central claim is that most neurologic disorders can be managed through pregnancy without termination, provided anticonvulsant regimens are optimized before conception and obstetric teams anticipate disease-specific intrapartum hazards. Diagnostic error is the recurring theme: eclampsia is mistaken for epilepsy, preeclampsia for cerebral venous thrombosis, and normal pregnancy symptoms for intracranial tumor.

## Frameworks Introduced
**Epilepsy preconception optimization.** Smallest effective dose, monotherapy over polytherapy, avoid valproate, folate 4 mg daily, check drug levels before conception and during pregnancy, and monitor free rather than total levels.

**Status epilepticus protocol.** Intravenous diazepam 10–30 mg or lorazepam 4–8 mg for temporary control, then fosphenytoin loading 20 mg phenytoin equivalents/kg infused at up to 150 mg phenytoin equivalents/min (cardiac monitoring), with phenobarbital or midazolam as needed; maintain airway, glucose, and electrolytes; continue the pregnancy.

**Seizure-versus-eclampsia distinction.** Eclampsia is identified by hypertension, proteinuria, and edema; their absence points to epilepsy. Treatment of both may be required when the diagnosis is uncertain.

**Myasthenia crisis precautions.** Avoid magnesium sulfate (may precipitate crisis), avoid muscle relaxants where possible, prefer regional analgesia, and reserve cesarean delivery for obstetric indications.

## Key Concepts
- Epilepsy affects 0.3–0.6% of pregnant women. Seizure frequency is unchanged in 54–80%, increased in 14–32%, and decreased in 3–24%. Women seizure-free for at least 9 months before conception remain seizure-free in 84–92%.
- Frequent seizures (more than one per month) predict deterioration; only about 25% of women with fewer than one seizure every 9 months worsen.
- Enzyme-inducing anticonvulsants — phenytoin, phenobarbital, primidone, carbamazepine, oxcarbazepine, topiramate, felbamate — reduce oral contraceptive efficacy; use at least 50 µg ethinyl estradiol or another method. Lamotrigine levels fall with combined oral contraceptives.
- Major congenital malformation rates: carbamazepine 2.2%, lamotrigine 3.2%, phenytoin 3.7%, valproate 6.2%. Valproate carries a 1% neural tube defect rate plus cleft lip/palate, polydactyly, hypospadias, craniosynostosis, and developmental delay. Trimethadione causes malformation and retardation in more than 50%.
- Neonatal coagulopathy from maternal anticonvulsants resembles vitamin K deficiency (factors II, VII, IX, X low; V, VIII, and fibrinogen normal). Maternal vitamin K₁ 10 mg daily in the last month is advocated by some, but routine neonatal vitamin K 1 mg is probably sufficient.
- Status epilepticus in pregnancy is a maternal and fetal emergency; the historical practice of terminating pregnancy is usually unnecessary.
- Migraine affects women two to three times more often than men; 20% have aura. About one third of women have headaches in the first postpartum week.
- Severe migraine is associated with more preeclampsia and severe nausea/vomiting but lower rates of threatened abortion and preterm delivery. Acetaminophen is preferred; avoid triptans, ergots, and propranolol (growth restriction, fetal β-blockade, hypoglycemia, hypothermia).
- Cerebral venous thrombosis: 15% death or dependency in pooled cohorts, though up to 80% recover well; recurrence risk about 1%. Anticoagulate with unfractionated or low-molecular-weight heparin even with intraparenchymal hemorrhage.
- Intracerebral and subarachnoid hemorrhage occur with roughly equal frequency peripartum, mostly after delivery. Aneurysms bleed more often in the latter half of pregnancy. Cerebral arteriovenous malformations are supratentorial in at least 70%; initial hemorrhage mortality about 10%. Known AVM is not a contraindication to pregnancy.
- Multiple sclerosis remits during pregnancy and exacerbates in the first 3–6 months postpartum. Breastfeeding does not affect relapse rate, and pregnancy does not worsen long-term disability. Interferon-β, glatiramer acetate, and natalizumab are not recommended in pregnancy; acute relapse is treated with a brief steroid course.
- Myasthenia gravis: in 47 prospectively followed women, 17% of untreated asymptomatic patients relapsed; among treated patients 39% improved, 42% were unchanged, and 19% worsened; 28% worsened after delivery.
- Transient neonatal myasthenia may occur in infants of myasthenic mothers and does not predict the same outcome in later children; congenital myasthenia is rare, occurs in infants of healthy mothers, and is permanent.
- Myotonic dystrophy type 1 (trinucleotide repeat, 19cen-q13.2) worsens at about 6–7 months; causes abortion, hydramnios, preterm labor, prolonged first stage, retained placenta, postpartum hemorrhage, and congenital myotonic dystrophy with facial diplegia, hypotonia, respiratory distress, feeding difficulty, and talipes in about half. Congenital transmission is almost always maternal.
- Restless legs syndrome affects 10–20% of pregnant women, arises in the latter half of pregnancy, and resolves after delivery; correct iron deficiency first.
- Carpal tunnel syndrome is common in pregnancy, is often bilateral, and usually resolves within about 3 months of delivery with nocturnal wrist splinting.
- Bell palsy incidence is increased in pregnancy and may relate to hypertension or preeclampsia. About 10% have permanent sequelae; steroids must start within 3–5 days to be effective.
- Chorea gravidarum requires exclusion of Sydenham chorea, Huntington disease, systemic lupus erythematosus, thyrotoxicosis, hypocalcemia, Wilson disease, and drug effects.
- Complete spinal cord lesions above T10 abolish labor pain; lesions above T5–T6 risk autonomic hyperreflexia with headache, hypertension, reflex bradycardia, sweating, and piloerection during contractions. Examine the cervix from 24–26 weeks in such patients.
- Wernicke encephalopathy complicates hyperemesis gravidarum; optic neuropathy from hyperemesis can cause rapid bilateral visual loss and may require termination if vomiting is refractory.

## Anti-patterns
- Continuing valproate through pregnancy without a documented attempt at an alternative, given the 6.2% malformation rate and 1% neural tube defect rate.
- Measuring total rather than free anticonvulsant levels; free levels correlate with efficacy.
- Attributing a first seizure in late pregnancy to epilepsy without checking blood pressure and urine protein.
- Treating suspected eclampsia with magnesium in a myasthenic patient.
- Using propranolol for migraine prophylaxis in pregnancy.
- Withholding anticoagulation for cerebral venous thrombosis because of intraparenchymal hemorrhage.
- Assuming a known AVM precludes pregnancy.
- Missing autonomic hyperreflexia in a laboring woman with a high spinal cord lesion.

## Key Takeaways
1. Optimize epilepsy before conception: monotherapy, lowest effective dose, no valproate, folate 4 mg daily, and free drug levels each trimester.
2. Treat status epilepticus with lorazepam 4–8 mg or diazepam 10–30 mg IV, then fosphenytoin 20 mg PE/kg, and continue the pregnancy.
3. Distinguish eclamptic from epileptic seizures by hypertension, proteinuria, and edema; treat empirically for both if uncertain.
4. Anticoagulate cerebral venous thrombosis with heparin despite hemorrhage, and counsel that recurrence risk is about 1%.
5. Expect multiple sclerosis relapse in the first 3–6 months postpartum and myasthenic worsening after delivery in 28%.
6. Avoid magnesium sulfate and muscle relaxants in myasthenia, and plan regional analgesia and assisted second stage.
