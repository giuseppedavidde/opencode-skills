# Chapter 16: Evidence-Based Practice in Perinatal Medicine

## Core Idea
Evidence-based medicine integrates individual clinical expertise with the best available external evidence from systematic research; it is not the mechanical application of trial results. Because clinical research grows by roughly 1,000 MEDLINE articles per day, the practitioner must classify study design, judge systematic and random error, and interpret measures of effect. The cautionary case is electronic fetal monitoring, adopted before evidence of benefit and never shown to improve outcomes beyond intermittent auscultation in low-risk patients, while contributing to rising cesarean rates. The same discipline applies to screening: a test's value depends on prevalence, and a screening program without an effective intervention has no value.

## Frameworks Introduced
**Study-design algorithm (Fig. 16-1).** Ask in order: was the purpose to characterize an individual or to quantify a relationship; did the investigator assign the exposure; what was the conceptual direction of exposure and outcome; was allocation random? This yields case report, case series, cohort, case-control, cross-sectional, nonrandomized controlled trial, or randomized controlled trial.

**Case-control versus cohort.** Case-control studies identify subjects by disease, suit rare conditions, assess multiple exposures, are fast and inexpensive, cannot give incidence, and are prone to bias. Cohort studies identify subjects by exposure, suit rare exposures, can measure incidence directly, and are less prone to bias but slow and expensive when prospective.

**Five maxims for systematic error.** (1) Systematic error matters more in observational studies; randomization prevents confounding in trials. (2) Retrospective studies are more likely biased than prospective ones. (3) Read the methods section for bias. (4) Do not discard a biased study automatically; consider the direction of the bias. (5) Always consider confounding, and prefer adjusted over unadjusted associations.

**Four-step analysis.** Graph and summarize; univariable analysis (chi-square or Fisher for unmatched binary data, McNemar for matched binary data, paired or unpaired t test for means); stratified analysis to screen for confounding and effect modification, with Mantel-Haenszel summary estimates; multivariable analysis for independent effects.

**Screening program criteria.** Significant health burden; effective early treatment; valid and reliable test; sufficient sensitivity and specificity; inexpensive and easy; safe and acceptable; cost-effective.

## Key Concepts
- Confounding occurs when two associated factors distort each other's effect on the outcome. Example: indomethacin tocolysis and neonatal morbidity in a matched retrospective cohort of 57 exposed and 57 unexposed infants below 30 weeks. Because indomethacin is not first-line, exposure may mark failed first-line tocolysis, itself linked to subclinical intra-amniotic infection.
- Bias types: selection bias (comparing preventive induction by family-medicine physicians with standard care by obstetricians, where populations differ) and information bias, of which recall bias is the classic form in teratogenesis studies.
- A probability value is the likelihood of the observed difference if no true difference exists; 0.05 means a 5% chance under the null. The chapter questions any meaningful distinction between 0.049 and 0.051.
- Relative risk = incidence in exposed ÷ incidence in unexposed. In the vitamins C and E trial (935 active, 942 placebo; preeclampsia 56 vs 47), RR = **1.20**. A 95% CI contains the true estimate 95% of the time in an unbiased study; if 1.0 lies inside, P is generally >0.05.
- Risk difference is the arithmetic difference in incidence (0.06 − 0.05 = **0.01**, a 1.0% absolute increase); zero means no difference. It cannot be calculated from case-control data.
- Odds ratio approximates relative risk when disease is rare and is the standard output of logistic regression; prefer relative risk or risk difference when incidence is measurable.
- Meta-analysis example (antibiotics for preterm premature rupture of membranes, 11 trials): pooled RR for neonatal infection **0.67 (95% CI 0.52–0.85)**, a 33% reduction, compatible with 15% to 48% reduction. For perinatal death, pooled RR **0.89 (95% CI 0.67–1.18)**, compatible with a 33% reduction or an 18% increase.
- An antiplatelet meta-analysis found over 50% reduction in preeclampsia when aspirin started before 16 weeks and no significant effect at 16 weeks or later. Of five trials with more than 1,000 women per arm, four showed no reduction — the argument that one well-performed large trial in a generalizable population can outweigh a heterogeneous meta-analysis.
- Sample size requires alpha, beta, and the expected incidence or exposure prevalence; alpha is conventionally 0.05 and beta 0.05–0.20, giving 80–95% power. Type II error produces a false-negative study. Reporting standards are CONSORT for trials and QUOROM for meta-analyses.
- Screening is presumptive identification of unrecognized disease and always requires diagnostic follow-up. Cervical cytology fulfills all criteria; cytomegalovirus screening does not, because no successful intervention exists. Fetal fibronectin can identify up to 60% of women destined for preterm birth before 28 weeks, but no effective intervention follows a positive result.
- Fetal fibronectin for delivery within 7 days: prevalence **3%**; sensitivity **87%**, specificity **82%**, PPV **13%**, NPV **99.5%**, positive likelihood ratio **4.8**. At a hypothetical 15% prevalence with unchanged sensitivity and specificity, PPV rises to **45%** and NPV falls to **97%**.
- Predictive values depend on prevalence; sensitivity and specificity do not. As prevalence rises, PPV rises and NPV falls.
- Post-test odds = pretest odds × likelihood ratio; probability = odds/(1 + odds). For 3% pretest probability (odds 0.031) × 4.8, post-test odds are 0.1488 and post-test probability 13.0%, matching the 2 × 2 calculation.
- Cervical length: 183 women with a prior birth below 32 weeks, transvaginal measurement every 2 weeks. The shortest observed length outperformed the first measurement; the optimal cut point was **25 mm**, with sensitivity only about **70%** and a false-positive rate of about **20%**. Against Bishop score, contraction frequency, and fibronectin, cervical length had the best but still modest discrimination.

## Anti-patterns
- **Adopting technology before evidence of benefit.** Electronic fetal monitoring spread before trials showed benefit.
- **Treating P < 0.05 as binary truth.** The threshold is arbitrary; 0.049 and 0.051 are not meaningfully different.
- **Reading a wide confidence interval as a negative study.** RR 0.89 (0.67–1.18) excludes neither meaningful benefit nor harm.
- **Judging a diagnostic test by its NPV alone.** Fetal fibronectin's 99.5% NPV was obtained at 3% prevalence; at 15% prevalence the NPV is 97% and 3% of test-negative patients still deliver within 7 days.
- **Assuming databases equal prospective cohorts.** Administrative data behave like retrospective cohorts, with misclassification and missing-not-at-random data.
- **Substituting secondary analyses for new trials.** A subgroup finding should generate a randomized trial, not become practice.

## Key Takeaways
- Classify every paper by design first; design determines valid measures of effect and plausible biases.
- Report relative risk or risk difference for trials and cohorts; reserve odds ratios for case-control designs and rare outcomes.
- Judge precision by the confidence interval, not the P value; check whether 1.0 (or 0) lies inside it.
- Confirm the seven screening criteria, especially that an effective intervention exists for those who screen positive.
- Recalculate predictive values for your own population prevalence before acting on published sensitivity and specificity.
- Interpret a 25-mm cervical length as a modest discriminator, not a diagnostic threshold.
