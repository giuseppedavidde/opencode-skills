# Chapter 23: Fetal Cardiac Imaging

## Core Idea
Congenital heart disease (CHD) is the most common major congenital malformation, occurring in about 8 per 1,000 live births, with 3 to 4 per 1,000 requiring medical or surgical intervention, yet prenatal detection remains the weakest part of obstetric sonography. The screen is a moving study, not a still frame: a beating four-chamber view plus both outflow tracts, acquired from good windows with adequate time. Ductal-dependent lesions such as transposition, tetralogy of Fallot, double-outlet right ventricle, and truncus arteriosus often have a normal-appearing four-chamber view, so the outflow tracts carry the diagnostic yield. Arrhythmias are classified by rate and regularity, and their management hinges on whether hydrops or structural disease is present.

## Frameworks Introduced
**Five-part four-chamber assessment — situs, size, symmetry, structure, squeeze.**
- *Situs*: heart in the left thorax, apex about 45° to the left. Dextrocardia, mesocardia, and left-axis deviation are associated with CHD. Pericardial fluid >2 mm, circumferential or otherwise, warrants further evaluation.
- *Size*: heart no more than about one third of the thoracic cross-sectional area, or cardiac circumference no more than about half the thoracic circumference.
- *Symmetry*: right side slightly larger than left; this right dominance may increase in the third trimester. Left larger than right, or marked right dominance, needs explanation.
- *Structure*: foramen ovale flap deviated into the left atrium; atrial septum visible just above the mitral insertion; tricuspid septal leaflet inserting slightly more apically than the mitral; thin delicate valves opening symmetrically; smooth-walled left ventricle reaching the apex; more trabeculated right ventricle also reaching the apex; intact ventricular septum on 2D.
- *Squeeze*: assess motion, not just anatomy. A motionless four-chamber view is inadequate.

**Outflow tract views.** From the four-chamber view, slight cephalic angulation gives the left ventricular outflow tract (aorta arising from the left ventricle, ascending aorta); further angulation gives the right ventricular outflow tract, with the pulmonary valve and main pulmonary artery crossing the aortic root and pointing to the left of the spine. The pulmonary valve and artery should be slightly larger than the aortic valve and root. Adding only the LVOT detects most major anomalies missed by the four-chamber view alone.

**Five sonographic findings of fetal congestive heart failure.** Cardiomegaly, tricuspid regurgitation, pericardial effusion, diminished ventricular systolic function, and increased atrial flow reversal in systemic veins. Severe CHF progresses to hydrops and pulsations in the free-floating umbilical vein.

**Arrhythmia classification (Box 23-7).**
- Irregular rhythm: premature atrial contractions/couplets, second-degree block.
- Sustained bradycardia: sinus, atrial, blocked atrial bigeminy, atrial flutter with high-degree block, complete heart block.
- Sustained tachycardia: sinus, supraventricular, atrial flutter, ventricular.

## Key Concepts
- Screening begins with extracardiac review: three-vessel cord, heart and stomach both on the left, no ascites or pericardial or pleural effusion.
- Risk factors include abnormal situs or cardiac views, arrhythmia, aneuploidy, two-vessel cord, extracardiac malformation, growth restriction, polyhydramnios, effusions, twins, and increased nuchal translucency; maternal lupus, diabetes, phenylketonuria, viral illness, and teratogens; and a first-degree relative with CHD.
- Ductal assessment: an S/D ratio **<1.9** suggests ductal constriction; a pulsatility index **>3** suggests increased right ventricular output and left heart obstruction.
- Bradycardia is a sustained rate **<120 beats/min**. Transient slowing with deep transducer pressure or maternal position is normal and needs no cardiac workup if it resolves.
- Complete heart block presents at **40–70 beats/min**; the atrial rate is normal but does not conduct. About **50%** occur with structural heart disease (L-looped ventricles, atrioventricular septal defect, polysplenia) and carry a poor prognosis; the other **50%** are immune-mediated from maternal anti-Ro or anti-La antibodies. Antibody-positive pregnancies carry about a **1%** risk of heart block, and the recurrence risk for a second affected fetus may be as high as **15%**.
- Supraventricular tachycardia is typically **240–280 beats/min**, usually via an accessory pathway, most often in structurally normal hearts, and may be associated with Wolff-Parkinson-White. It usually conducts 1:1 and is often intermittent.
- Atrial flutter has an atrial rate of **300–500 beats/min**, with ventricular rates from **<100 to >300** depending on 2:1 or 3:1 conduction; flutter is usually sustained rather than intermittent and has a higher association with structural disease.
- Ventricular tachycardia usually presents at **180–300 beats/min** with poor ventricular function and atrioventricular dissociation. Lower rates (**180–220**) are better tolerated. Digoxin is contraindicated.
- Premature atrial contractions typically resolve spontaneously within **2–3 weeks**; they carry no real risk and need no treatment, though a small proportion progress to SVT. Caffeine, decongestants, and tobacco may aggravate them.
- Blocked atrial bigeminy produces a ventricular rate exactly half the atrial rate; no therapy is needed beyond observation and avoidance of stimulants, and prognosis is excellent.
- Treatment thresholds in the authors' practice: treat SVT when it occupies more than **33%** of the time; treat earlier if structural disease or hydrops is present; beyond **32–34 weeks**, delivery may be preferable to maternal drug therapy. Digoxin loading is **0.25–0.5 mg orally or intravenously every 6–8 hours** with a target level of **2.0 µg/mL**, stopping for effect, therapeutic level, maternal toxicity, or fetal toxicity; maintenance is 0.25–0.5 mg two to four times daily. Second-line agents include propranolol, flecainide, and amiodarone; sotalol is favored by some for atrial flutter.
- Immune-mediated heart block: dexamethasone or intravenous immunoglobulin may prevent progression, but this remains controversial; sympathomimetics rarely achieve sustained rate increase, though they may be used below **55 beats/min**.

## Anti-patterns
- **Screening with a still four-chamber image.** Motion is required to judge structure and function; a static frame misses major disease.
- **Relying on the four-chamber view alone.** Transposition, tetralogy of Fallot, truncus arteriosus, and double-outlet right ventricle can have normal four-chamber views.
- **Ignoring the outflow tracts because they are difficult.** They do not lie in one plane, which is precisely why dedicated sweeps and time are needed.
- **Treating every fetal bradycardia as pathologic.** Deep transducer pressure and maternal position cause transient slowing.
- **Giving digoxin for ventricular tachycardia.** It is contraindicated; atrioventricular dissociation distinguishes VT from SVT.
- **Missing the ductal constriction waveform.** An S/D below 1.9 or PI above 3 should prompt a search for constriction or left heart obstruction.
- **Reassuring after a normal heart in a high-risk pregnancy.** Risk factors and extracardiac anomalies change the pretest probability and the indication for formal echocardiography.

## Key Takeaways
- Screen with a beating four-chamber view plus both outflow tracts, and assess situs, size, symmetry, structure, and squeeze explicitly.
- Accept a cardiothoracic area up to about one third and cardiac circumference up to about half the thoracic circumference; anything larger is a strong CHD indicator.
- Measure pericardial fluid; more than 2 mm is abnormal and warrants evaluation for hydrops and CHD.
- Classify arrhythmias by rate: <120 bpm bradycardia, 240–280 bpm SVT, 300–500 atrial flutter, 180–300 ventricular tachycardia, 40–70 complete heart block.
- Treat SVT when it occupies >33% of time, or earlier with hydrops or structural disease; consider delivery after 32–34 weeks.
- Test anti-Ro/La-positive pregnancies with serial PR-interval Doppler and counsel about a 1% risk and up to 15% recurrence of heart block.
- Look for the five signs of CHF — cardiomegaly, tricuspid regurgitation, pericardial effusion, poor systolic function, and increased systemic venous atrial reversal — before hydrops develops.
