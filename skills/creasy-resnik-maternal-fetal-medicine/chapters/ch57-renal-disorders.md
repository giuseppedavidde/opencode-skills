# Chapter 57: Renal Disorders

## Core Idea

Pregnancy alters renal physiology enough that normal values differ from the nonpregnant state, and kidney disease alters pregnancy outcome in proportion to its severity and proteinuria. Urinary tract infection is the commonest renal problem, and untreated asymptomatic bacteriuria progresses to pyelonephritis in up to 30%. Preeclampsia is the dominant pregnancy-specific renal syndrome and shares features with thrombotic microangiopathy. Chronic kidney disease is staged by glomerular filtration rate, and stage predicts both maternal renal decline and fetal growth restriction. Dialysis and transplantation require disease-specific adjustments in prescription, medication, and delivery planning.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Urinary tract infection framework.** Three syndromes: asymptomatic bacteriuria, cystitis, and pyelonephritis. Diagnosis requires urine culture with at least 100,000 colony-forming units per milliliter of a single uropathogen; lower counts can be significant in symptomatic women or with slow-growing organisms.
- **CKD staging (K/DOQI).** Stage 1 is kidney damage with normal or increased GFR (above 90 mL/min/1.73 m²); stages 2 to 5 represent progressive GFR decline. Standard clearance methods and the MDRD equation are inaccurate in pregnancy.
- **Preeclampsia as an angiogenic disorder.** Soluble fms-like tyrosine kinase 1 (sFlt-1) and soluble endoglin are antiangiogenic and rise before clinical disease, while placental growth factor is proangiogenic and falls. Abnormal angiogenic markers antedate clinical signs by weeks.
- **Thrombotic microangiopathy differential (Table 57-1).** HUS/TTP, HELLP, and acute fatty liver of pregnancy are compared by hemolytic anemia, thrombocytopenia, coagulopathy, central nervous system symptoms, renal failure, hypertension, proteinuria, transaminases, bilirubin, ammonia, response to delivery, and management. Severe ADAMTS13 deficiency (below 5%) strongly favors HUS or TTP.
- **Acute fatty liver of pregnancy (AFLP).** Occurs in about 1 in 10,000 pregnancies; associated with HADHA mutations causing long-chain 3-hydroxyacyl-CoA dehydrogenase deficiency; presents with nausea, vomiting, jaundice, and abdominal pain; renal failure occurs in over half.
- **Dialysis prescription in pregnancy.** Increase hemodialysis to at least 20 hours per week, target predialysis blood urea nitrogen below 50 mg/dL, give 1.5 to 1.8 g protein/kg/day, folate 1 to 5 mg daily, target hemoglobin 10 to 11 g/L, and keep postdialysis blood pressure below 140/90 mm Hg. Avoid ACE inhibitors, ARBs, and diuretics.
- **Renal transplant management.** Continue calcineurin inhibitors and minimize other immunosuppressants; monitor drug levels because pregnancy changes volume of distribution; intrauterine devices are less reliable and barrier or hormonal contraception is preferred.

## Key Concepts

- Asymptomatic bacteriuria affects 2% to 10% of pregnant women and progresses to pyelonephritis in up to 30% if untreated. Escherichia coli causes 65% to 80% of cases.
- Antibiotic treatment of asymptomatic bacteriuria clears infection and reduces pyelonephritis (relative risk 0.23). Treated patients have about two thirds the risk of low birth weight (relative risk 0.66) and half the risk of preterm delivery (relative risk 0.50).
- Safe antibiotics include cephalosporins, amoxicillin, nitrofurantoin, and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole; trimethoprim is avoided in the first trimester because of neural tube defects. A 4- to 7-day course is better than single-dose therapy.
- Preeclampsia affects 3% to 5% of pregnancies. The GFR fall is usually less than 25% and the GFR often remains above nonpregnant values.
- Women with preeclampsia have a 2.7-fold higher risk of death after a median 13 years, and cardiovascular risk rises with severity: relative risk 2.99 for moderate and 5.3 for severe preeclampsia.
- CKD stages 1 and 2 generally have successful pregnancies without worsened renal prognosis when blood pressure is controlled and proteinuria is minimal.
- In CKD stages 3 to 5 with serum creatinine of 1.4 mg/dL or higher, GFR remained stable in 51%, returned to prepregnancy levels in 8%, stayed persistently lower in 31%, and declined further in 10%. Accelerated decline occurred in 7 of 21 women with creatinine of 2.0 mg/dL or higher versus 1 of 49 with 1.4 to 1.9 mg/dL.
- A baseline GFR below 40 mL/min/1.73 m² plus proteinuria above 1 g/day predicts accelerated loss of GFR after delivery and shorter time to dialysis.
- Acute glomerulonephritis occurs in about 0.05% of pregnancies. Renal biopsy after 32 weeks is not recommended because the prone position is difficult.
- IgA nephropathy is the commonest glomerular disease in pregnancy. Corticosteroids and azathioprine are preferred; cyclophosphamide and mycophenolate are avoided.
- Dialysis outcomes: polyhydramnios 33% to 62%, maternal hypertension 42% to 80%, cesarean delivery 46% to 53%, preterm delivery 85%, mean gestational age at delivery 32 weeks. Infant survival is 40% to 50% when conception occurs on dialysis and 75% to 80% when conception precedes dialysis.
- AFLP causes renal failure in over half of patients and typically remits after delivery.
- Obstetric acute kidney injury occurs in fewer than 1 in 10,000 pregnancies. Early-pregnancy causes are usually prerenal from hyperemesis gravidarum or pyelonephritis.

## Anti-patterns

- Withholding treatment for asymptomatic bacteriuria because the patient has no symptoms.
- Using trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole in the first trimester without weighing the neural tube defect risk.
- Estimating GFR in pregnancy with the MDRD equation or timed urine collections and treating the result as accurate.
- Attributing postpartum renal failure with microangiopathic hemolysis to preeclampsia when ADAMTS13 is severely deficient and plasma exchange is needed.
- Delaying delivery in AFLP while correcting coagulopathy and hypoglycemia indefinitely.
- Prescribing ACE inhibitors or ARBs to a pregnant patient with proteinuric CKD.
- Relying on an intrauterine device for contraception in an immunosuppressed transplant recipient.

## Key Takeaways

1. Screen all pregnant women for asymptomatic bacteriuria and treat positive cultures to prevent pyelonephritis.
2. Stage chronic kidney disease before conception and counsel that baseline creatinine of 2.0 mg/dL or higher, GFR below 40 mL/min/1.73 m², and proteinuria above 1 g/day predict accelerated renal decline.
3. Measure angiogenic markers only as an adjunct; preeclampsia remains a clinical diagnosis of new hypertension and proteinuria after 20 weeks.
4. Distinguish HUS/TTP from HELLP with ADAMTS13 and the postpartum course, and start plasma exchange empirically when TTP is suspected.
5. For dialysis patients, intensify to at least 20 hours per week, target predialysis blood urea nitrogen below 50 mg/dL, and counsel about an 85% preterm delivery rate and about 32 weeks' mean gestational age.
6. Continue calcineurin inhibitors in transplant recipients, avoid mycophenolate and cyclophosphamide, and monitor drug levels through pregnancy.
7. Plan delivery with neonatology because prematurity and growth restriction are the dominant fetal risks in CKD.
