# Chapter 10: Maternal Nutrition

## Core Idea

Maternal nutritional status before conception and during pregnancy is a modifiable determinant of perinatal outcome. The chapter's argument is that the dominant problem in developed countries has shifted from undernutrition to overnutrition. About 32% of reproductive-age US women are obese and 56% are overweight, and about half gain more than the Institute of Medicine recommends. Both inadequate and excessive gestational weight gain carry risk, and pre-pregnancy body mass index predicts several outcomes more strongly than weight gain does.

## Frameworks Introduced

**Institute of Medicine 2009 weight gain framework.** Target gain is set by pre-pregnancy body mass index class, and the 2009 revision broadened the goal from preventing low birth weight to minimising short- and long-term adverse outcomes for mother and child.

**Obesity class framework.** Class I is BMI 30 to 34.9, class II is 35 to 39.9, and class III is 40 or greater.

**Life-course and developmental origins framework.** Maternal metabolic status at conception influences organogenesis and later disease risk in the offspring, so pre-conception nutrition is treated as part of prenatal care.

**MyPlate dietary framework.** Half the plate is vegetables and fruit, one quarter is lean protein, one quarter is whole grain, plus a dairy product.

## Key Concepts

- Recommended total gain, singleton pregnancy: underweight (BMI below 18.5) 28 to 40 lb; normal (18.5 to 24.9) 25 to 35 lb; overweight (25 to 29.9) 15 to 25 lb; obese (30 or above) 11 to 20 lb.
- Recommended total gain, twin pregnancy: normal weight 37 to 54 lb; overweight 31 to 50 lb; obese 25 to 42 lb.
- Rate of gain is about 1 lb/week in the second and third trimesters for underweight and normal-weight women, 0.6 lb/week for overweight women, and 0.5 lb/week for obese women.
- Folic acid: 0.4 mg/day for all women capable of pregnancy; 4 mg/day starting 1 month before conception and through the first trimester after a prior neural tube defect.
- US mandatory fortification of cereal and grain products began in 1998 and reduced neural tube defect incidence by about 25%.
- About 50% of US women exceed the recommended gestational weight gain.
- Extra energy requirement is about 340 kcal/day in the second trimester and 450 kcal/day in the third.
- Protein requirement is about 60 g/day, 10 g above the non-pregnant requirement.
- Iron: recommended intake 27 mg/day. Do not give 50 to 60 mg/day or more to non-anemic women.
- Calcium: 1000 mg/day for women aged 19 to 39; 1300 mg/day for women under 18.
- Iodine: 220 µg/day in pregnancy.
- Choline: estimated requirement 450 mg/day. Neural tube defect risk doubles when intake is 290 mg/day or less.
- DHA: at least 200 mg/day from fish or marine oil.
- Vitamin D: the Institute of Medicine recommends 600 IU/day. ACOG does not recommend universal screening and considers 1000 to 2000 IU/day safe if deficiency is found. The Endocrine Society recommends universal supplementation with 1500 to 2000 IU/day and defines deficiency as 25-hydroxyvitamin D below 50 nmol/L; the Institute of Medicine uses below 20 nmol/L.
- Vitamin C supplementation may increase preterm birth risk. Vitamin E has no proven benefit.
- Multiple gestation: add 300 kcal/day per fetus. Twin caloric targets are 4000 kcal/day for underweight women, 3000 to 3500 for normal weight, 3250 for overweight, and 2700 to 3000 for obese women.
- Triplets: aim for at least 50 to 60 lb of total gain.
- Nausea and vomiting usually begins before 9 weeks. New onset after 9 weeks requires a workup for another cause.
- Hyperemesis gravidarum affects 0.5% to 2% of pregnancies.
- First-line treatment for nausea is vitamin B6 10 to 25 mg three or four times daily; add doxylamine 12.5 mg three or four times daily if needed.
- Bariatric surgery is associated with reduced gestational diabetes and hypertensive disorders, but also with increased small-for-gestational-age birth, preterm birth, and perinatal mortality.
- Vegan diets require vitamin B12 supplementation and attention to calcium, vitamin D, riboflavin, iron, and total energy.

## Anti-patterns

- Congratulating a patient on large weight gain. Excessive gain is a risk factor, not a sign of health.
- Recommending caloric restriction during pregnancy. Improve diet quality and insulin sensitivity instead.
- Giving high-dose iron to non-anemic women. It causes gastrointestinal effects without benefit.
- Assuming a prenatal multivitamin protects equally in all women. Some benefits appear limited to lean women.
- Treating nausea that begins after 9 weeks as ordinary pregnancy sickness.
- Ignoring pre-pregnancy BMI and counselling only on weight gain.

## Key Takeaways

1. Set a weight-gain target from pre-pregnancy BMI at the first visit.
2. Prescribe 0.4 mg folic acid for all, and 4 mg for a prior neural tube defect.
3. Add 340 kcal/day in the second trimester and 450 kcal/day in the third.
4. Use 27 mg/day of iron and reserve high-dose iron for documented deficiency.
5. Investigate nausea that first appears after 9 weeks.
6. Counsel obese women to reach a normal BMI before conception.
