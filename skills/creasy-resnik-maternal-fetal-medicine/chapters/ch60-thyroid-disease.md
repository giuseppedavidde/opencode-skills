# Chapter 60: Thyroid Disease and Pregnancy

## Core Idea

Thyroid disease is one of the commonest endocrinopathies in women of childbearing age, and pregnancy changes thyroid economy in three phases: a twofold to threefold rise in thyroxine-binding globulin from estrogen, a transient thyrotropin suppression from human chorionic gonadotropin at 8 to 14 weeks, and altered peripheral deiodination later in gestation. Iodine demand rises because renal clearance increases and iodine is diverted to the fetoplacental unit. The fetal thyroid depends on maternal iodine and, early in gestation, on maternal thyroxine. Both hypothyroidism and hyperthyroidism worsen obstetric outcome, and treatment must be adjusted repeatedly through pregnancy.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Physiologic framework.** Thyroxine-binding globulin rises twofold to threefold, and free T4 and free T3 fall only 10% to 15% under iodine sufficiency. Human chorionic gonadotropin peaks late in the first trimester; a 10,000 IU/L increment lowers basal TSH by 0.1 mU/L, and about 20% of women have TSH below the nonpregnant lower limit between 8 and 14 weeks. Placental type II deiodinase maintains local T3 production, and type III deiodinase converts T4 to reverse T3.
- **Iodine requirement.** WHO recommends 150 µg of iodine daily for adults and 250 µg for pregnant and lactating women, endorsed by the Endocrine Society. Between 1 and 1.5 billion people are at risk of deficiency, and 500 million live in areas of overt deficiency.
- **Trimester-specific TSH interpretation.** Use trimester-specific reference ranges from iodine-sufficient populations. TSH above 10 mIU/L defines overt hypothyroidism regardless of free T4.
- **Hypothyroidism treatment protocol.** Start levothyroxine 0.1 to 0.15 mg daily; use up to 2 µg/kg/day when little thyroid tissue remains. Adjust every 6 weeks to a TSH in the lower half of the reference range. Women already on thyroxine should increase by two extra doses per week (about 30%) as early as the fifth week of gestation.
- **Hyperthyroidism treatment protocol.** Propylthiouracil is preferred in the first trimester; methimazole is used thereafter or if PTU is unavailable or not tolerated. PTU 100 to 150 mg every 8 hours (300 to 450 mg/day); methimazole starting dose up to 20 mg; equivalence 10:1 to 15:1. Target free T4 in the high-normal range and TSH below 0.5 mU/L. Discontinue by 32 to 36 weeks in many patients.
- **Thyroid storm treatment.** PTU 600 mg loading then 200 to 300 mg every 6 hours; iodide 2 to 5 drops of saturated potassium iodide solution or 500 to 1000 mg sodium iodide intravenously every 12 hours, given at least 1 hour after PTU; lithium carbonate 300 mg every 6 hours if iodine allergic; dexamethasone 2 mg every 6 hours for four doses; propranolol intravenously at 1 mg/min or esmolol 250 to 500 µg/kg loading then 50 to 100 µg/kg/min.
- **Thyroid nodule framework.** Fine-needle aspiration biopsy is safe at any gestational age. For well-differentiated papillary cancer without lymph node metastases or extracapsular invasion, surgery can be deferred until after delivery.

## Key Concepts

- Hypothyroidism occurs in about 1 in 1600 to 2000 deliveries. In a US screening study at 15 to 18 weeks, 2.5% had TSH of 6 mU/L or higher, and thyroid antibodies were present in 58% of them versus 11% of euthyroid controls.
- Hashimoto thyroiditis is the commonest cause of hypothyroidism and affects 8% to 10% of reproductive-age women; antithyroglobulin titers are elevated in 50% to 70%, and nearly all have antithyroid peroxidase antibodies.
- Hypothyroidism increases miscarriage, preeclampsia, abruption, low birth weight, prematurity, and stillbirth.
- Ten to twenty percent of patients become hypothyroid within 6 months of radioactive iodine therapy, and 2% to 4% each year thereafter.
- Hyperthyroidism in pregnancy: low birth weight odds ratio 2.4 when treated and 9.2 when uncontrolled; prematurity odds ratio 2.8 when treated and 16.5 when uncontrolled.
- Methimazole embryopathy includes cutis aplasia, choanal atresia, tracheoesophageal fistula, and facial anomalies. Propylthiouracil has been linked to cutis aplasia and carries higher hepatotoxicity.
- Agranulocytosis is a contraindication to further thionamide therapy; any sore throat on treatment requires immediate drug cessation and a white blood cell count.
- Antithyroid drug effect takes 6 to 8 weeks to become clinically apparent.
- Propranolol 20 to 40 mg two or three times daily controls adrenergic symptoms; avoid atenolol (category D). Prolonged beta-blockade can cause growth restriction, fetal bradycardia, and hypoglycemia.
- Thyroid storm is diagnosed clinically, for example temperature 102°F with agitation and pulse above 130 beats/min; it may be precipitated by labor, delivery, cesarean section, infection, or cardiomyopathy.
- Neonatal Graves disease occurs in about 1% of offspring of women with Graves disease through transplacental thyrotropin-stimulating immunoglobulins. Maternal levels above 300% of baseline predict fetal hyperthyroidism and should be measured by 22 weeks.
- Radioactive iodine is contraindicated in pregnancy because it concentrates in the fetal thyroid after about 12 weeks.
- Postpartum thyroiditis occurs in the first postpartum year and may cause thyrotoxicosis followed by hypothyroidism.
- Ferrous sulfate, aluminum hydroxide, cholestyramine, and sucralfate impair levothyroxine absorption and must be separated by at least 4 hours.

## Anti-patterns

- Applying nonpregnant TSH reference ranges to pregnant women, especially in the first trimester.
- Withholding levothyroxine because the TSH elevation is "subclinical" when TSH exceeds 10 mIU/L.
- Failing to increase pre-pregnancy levothyroxine early in gestation, which produces maternal and fetal hypothyroidism.
- Using methimazole in the first trimester when propylthiouracil is available.
- Continuing full-dose antithyroid therapy into the third trimester and causing fetal goiter and hypothyroidism.
- Giving iodide before a thionamide in thyroid storm, which fuels hormone synthesis.
- Using radioactive iodine for diagnosis or therapy during pregnancy.
- Ignoring a sore throat in a patient taking a thionamide.

## Key Takeaways

1. Use trimester-specific TSH ranges, and treat overt hypothyroidism with levothyroxine 0.1 to 0.15 mg daily, titrating to a TSH in the lower half of the reference range.
2. Increase levothyroxine by about 30% as soon as pregnancy is confirmed, and check TSH every 4 to 6 weeks through the first half of pregnancy.
3. Use propylthiouracil in the first trimester and switch to methimazole afterward; target free T4 in the high-normal range and TSH below 0.5 mU/L.
4. Measure thyrotropin receptor antibodies by 22 weeks in Graves disease, because levels above 300% of baseline predict fetal hyperthyroidism.
5. Treat thyroid storm with PTU first, iodide at least 1 hour later, dexamethasone, and beta-blockade, plus intensive supportive care.
6. Evaluate thyroid nodules with fine-needle aspiration at any gestational age, and defer surgery for well-differentiated cancer without nodal or extracapsular spread until after delivery.
7. Check TSH 6 to 8 weeks postpartum and reduce levothyroxine to the pre-pregnancy dose, watching for postpartum thyroiditis.
