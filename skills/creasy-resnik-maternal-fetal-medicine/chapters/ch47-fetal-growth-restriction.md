# Chapter 47: Intrauterine Growth Restriction

## Core Idea
Small for gestational age (SGA) and fetal growth restriction (FGR) are not the same thing. SGA is a statistical descriptor (birth weight below the 10th percentile), whereas FGR means failure to achieve inherent growth potential. Many SGA infants are constitutionally small and normal, and some infants between the 10th and 20th percentiles are growth restricted. Management therefore depends on distinguishing the two by growth velocity, amniotic fluid volume, and Doppler, and on timing delivery to balance stillbirth risk against prematurity.

## Frameworks Introduced

**SGA classification.** Below the 10th percentile is SGA, 10th to 90th is appropriate for gestational age, above the 90th is large for gestational age. Customized growth curves adjust for maternal height, weight, ethnicity, and parity and better reflect growth potential than population curves.

**Symmetric versus asymmetric FGR.** Symmetric FGR accounts for 20% to 30% of cases and reflects early insults affecting hyperplasia: aneuploidy, malformation, infection, or drug exposure. Femur length and head circumference are both small. Asymmetric FGR reflects late substrate deprivation, usually placental insufficiency: head circumference and femur length are spared while abdominal circumference falls because of reduced hepatic growth and subcutaneous fat.

**Antenatal surveillance algorithm.** With a reassuring biophysical profile and present umbilical artery end-diastolic flow, weekly or alternate-week testing suffices. With diminished end-diastolic flow, add twice-weekly biophysical profile. Deliver after 32 weeks for reversed end-diastolic flow and after 34 weeks for absent end-diastolic flow, unless growth velocity and other tests remain reassuring.

## Key Concepts
- FGR affects about 4% to 8% of infants in developed countries and 6% to 30% in developing countries. One quarter to one third of infants under 2500 g have FGR.
- Fetal growth rate is 5 g per day at 14 to 15 weeks, 10 g per day at 20 weeks, and 30 to 35 g per day at 32 to 34 weeks. Weekly gain peaks at 230 to 285 g at 32 to 34 weeks and may reach zero or become negative at 41 to 42 weeks.
- Perinatal mortality for SGA infants of 1500 to 2500 g is 5 to 30 times that of appropriate-weight infants; below 1500 g it is 70 to 100 times higher.
- Mortality rises progressively below the 10th percentile and markedly below the 6th percentile.
- Fetal mortality in FGR is about 50% higher than neonatal mortality, and male fetuses have higher mortality than female fetuses.
- Congenital anomalies are 10% to 30% more frequent in FGR and account for 30% to 60% of FGR-related perinatal deaths.
- Severe oligohydramnios is defined as a single deepest vertical pocket under 2 cm or an amniotic fluid index under 5 cm, or both.
- A normal biophysical profile score had a fetal death rate of 0.726 per 1000 births, with a false-negative rate under 1 in 1000 in 19,221 high-risk pregnancies.
- Umbilical artery Doppler is the only modality validated by randomized trials for predicting perinatal outcome in FGR. A meta-analysis of 12 trials showed a 38% reduction in the odds of perinatal death; a Cochrane review of 18 trials showed a 29% reduction (OR 0.71) plus fewer inductions and cesarean deliveries.
- Absent or reversed end-diastolic umbilical artery flow predicts increased perinatal morbidity and mortality and correlates with maldevelopment of the placental terminal villous tree.
- The GRIT trial randomized 548 preterm FGR pregnancies at 24 to 36 weeks and found no mortality difference between immediate and delayed delivery. Among infants under 31 weeks, severe disability at 2 years occurred in 13% of immediate deliveries versus 5% of delayed deliveries.
- About 50% of FGR neonates have umbilical artery buffer base below 37 mEq/L at delivery (normal above 40 mEq/L), indicating metabolic acidosis.
- Intrapartum fetal stress occurs in 25% to 50% of FGR pregnancies.
- In 2560 SGA infants at 25 to 34 weeks, elective cesarean offered no neonatal advantage and slightly increased respiratory distress.

## Anti-patterns
- Equating an estimated fetal weight below the 10th percentile with FGR without assessing growth velocity, fluid, and Doppler.
- Using population-based percentiles alone in a woman whose constitutionally small size is normal for her.
- Reassuring a patient on a normal umbilical artery Doppler while ignoring absent growth over serial scans.
- Delivering a preterm FGR fetus solely because of absent end-diastolic flow before 34 weeks when other tests are reassuring.
- Using a normal biophysical profile as proof of a normal fetus rather than as one component of surveillance.
- Missing the diagnosis because ultrasound estimated weight is inaccurate at the margins of viability.

## Key Takeaways
1. Distinguish SGA from FGR using customized growth potential, growth velocity, amniotic fluid volume, and umbilical artery Doppler.
2. Measure growth serially with the same observer and technique; a falling abdominal circumference is an early sign of asymmetric FGR.
3. Use umbilical artery Doppler in every FGR pregnancy; it is the only Doppler modality proven to improve outcome.
4. Deliver after 32 weeks for reversed end-diastolic flow and after 34 weeks for absent flow unless other parameters are reassuring.
5. Give corticosteroids before 34 weeks when delivery is imminent.
6. Monitor labor closely; half of FGR neonates show metabolic acidosis at birth, and cesarean delivery is indicated for deteriorating fetal status.
