# Chapter 28: Uterus and Adnexae Imaging

## Core Idea
This chapter covers the non-fetal pelvic findings that alter obstetric management: adnexal masses, amniotic fluid volume, uterine anomalies, and fibroids. Most adnexal masses in pregnancy are benign and resolve, but a minority are malignant, torse, rupture, or obstruct labor. Amniotic fluid volume is a screening signal for fetal and placental disease rather than a diagnosis. Müllerian anomalies and fibroids raise the risk of preterm birth, malpresentation, growth restriction, and postpartum hemorrhage, but rarely require intervention during pregnancy.

## Frameworks Introduced
**Adnexal mass morphologic categories.** Cystic (follicular <3 cm; corpus luteum 3–6 cm with a "ring of fire"; theca lutein multiloculated; cystadenoma with excrescences), solid (fibroma), solid-cystic (hemorrhagic cyst with sludge, endometrioma, teratoma).

**Amniotic fluid definitions.** Oligohydramnios: amniotic fluid index (AFI) <5 cm, maximum vertical pocket (MVP) <2 cm, or below the 5th percentile. Polyhydramnios: AFI >24 cm, MVP >8 cm, or above the 95th percentile.

**Müllerian anomaly classification (American Fertility Society).** Hypoplasia/agenesis, unicornuate, didelphys, bicornuate, septate, arcuate, and DES-related. Distribution among uterine anomalies: bicornuate 51%, didelphys 25%, septate 13%, unicornuate 3%, arcuate the remainder.

## Key Concepts
- Adnexal masses are detected in 1–4% of pregnant women; 1–3% are malignant. Resolution occurs in 80–90% overall. Lesions likely to resolve are simple cysts <5–6 cm diagnosed before 16 weeks.
- Persistent masses: torsion 1–22%, rupture 0–9%, obstruction of labor 2–17%; malignancy 1–2% overall.
- Surgery, when needed, has the least fetal morbidity at 17–22 weeks. Reserve cesarean delivery for complex lesions strongly suspected to be malignant or likely to obstruct labor.
- AFI is a four-quadrant sum; measure pockets in the maternal sagittal plane without angulation, with gain set to show cord, avoiding narrow pockets and measurements within 1 cm of fetal parts.
- Maternal ingestion of 2 L of water can raise the AFI by 2–4 cm, so dehydration is a reversible cause of oligohydramnios.
- Oligohydramnios associations: growth restriction, dehydration, urinary tract anomalies, ruptured membranes. Polyhydramnios associations: maternal diabetes, obstructed fetal swallowing (esophageal atresia, thoracic mass or effusion, duodenal atresia, facial anomaly), fetal hydrops (anemia, cardiac failure, arrhythmia).
- In a meta-analysis of four randomized trials (3125 women), the deepest vertical pocket versus the AFI produced similar neonatal intensive care admission; the AFI led to more diagnoses of oligohydramnios (RR 2.33), more inductions, and more deliveries for uncertain fetal well-being, without differences in Apgar score, umbilical artery pH <7.1, or nonreassuring heart rate.
- Uterine anomalies occur in 1–10% of women, 2–8% seeking fertility treatment, and 5–30% with recurrent miscarriage. Associated odds ratios: growth restriction 2.0, abruption 3.1, preterm birth <34 weeks 7.4, malpresentation 8.6. Absent kidney in 30%; septate vagina in 5%.
- A second horn beside the gravid horn can mimic an adnexal mass but shows an echogenic endometrial stripe. Preterm birth is not increased with an arcuate uterus.
- Fibroids are present in 3–4% at mid-trimester ultrasound. Risk factors include African-American descent, nulliparity, obesity, polycystic ovary syndrome, diabetes, and hypertension. Most do not grow during pregnancy.
- Fibroids may infarct or degenerate, causing pain and preterm labor; retroplacental location can reduce placental function. Lower uterine segment or cervical fibroids may obstruct delivery. After myomectomy, the risk of uterine rupture in labor is 1% or less, so a trial of labor is not contraindicated.

## Anti-patterns
- Assuming every adnexal mass needs surgery in pregnancy; most resolve, and surgery is delayed to 17–22 weeks when indicated.
- Diagnosing oligohydramnios from a single narrow pocket or from a pocket containing cord.
- Treating an AFI of 5–8 cm as abnormal without confirmatory testing; repeat after hydration and use biophysical testing.
- Missing a Müllerian anomaly because a second horn was called an adnexal mass.
- Attributing growth restriction to a fibroid without assessing placental implantation and fetal growth serially.
- Recommending cesarean delivery solely because of a prior myomectomy.

## Key Takeaways
1. Characterize adnexal masses by wall, septations, internal echoes, and Doppler; simple cysts <6 cm can be followed.
2. Use the deepest vertical pocket for surveillance; it produces fewer interventions than the AFI with equivalent outcomes.
3. Assess the renal system and vaginal septum when a Müllerian anomaly is found.
4. Measure and locate fibroids relative to the placenta and the lower uterine segment; document them near term for delivery planning.
5. Expect 80–90% resolution of adnexal masses, but counsel about torsion, rupture, and a 1–2% malignancy risk.
