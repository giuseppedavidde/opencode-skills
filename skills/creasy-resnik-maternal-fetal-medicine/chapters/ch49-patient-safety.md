# Chapter 49: Patient Safety in Obstetrics

## Core Idea

Obstetric care is high-volume, high-stakes, and team-dependent, so most harm arises from system and communication failures rather than individual incompetence. The safety movement, adapted from aviation, replaces blame with standardization, checklists, team training, simulation, and structured communication. Obstetrics lags other specialties despite more than 4 million US admissions per year and adverse events in up to 16% of deliveries. A just culture balances no-blame with accountability. Culture change is measurable and must be managed with validated surveys and a multidisciplinary safety committee.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Annenberg Conference (1996) to IOM report (1999).** A 1996 meeting of the AAAS, AMA, and Joint Commission launched the National Patient Safety Foundation, the Institute for Healthcare Improvement (IHI), the VA Patient Safety Initiative, the IHI 5 Million Lives Campaign, and Joint Commission Sentinel Event review. The 1999 IOM report estimated 44,000 to 98,000 deaths per year from medical error and reframed error as correctable system fault.
- **Just culture (Marx; AHRQ).** Behavior is classified as human error (slip, lapse, mistake), at-risk behavior (shortcut), or reckless behavior (ignoring required safety steps). Accountability attaches to at-risk and reckless behavior, not to inadvertent slips.
- **Safety Attitudes Questionnaire (SAQ).** Obstetrics-specific, validated by Sexton. Anonymous 5-point Likert survey of teamwork and safety climate. A difference of 10 percentage points or more over time or between staff groups is clinically significant; 80% agreement defines a favorable climate target. It is the only safety climate survey linked to patient outcomes.
- **AHRQ Hospital Survey on Patient Safety Culture.** Public-domain instrument measuring 7 unit-level aspects, 3 hospital-level aspects, and 4 outcome variables, with national benchmarks.
- **Crew resource management (CRM) team training.** Four- to eight-hour mixed-discipline seminars with video, lecture, and role play. Tools include SBAR, CUS (concerned, uncomfortable, scared), the two-challenge rule, callout, check-back, cross-monitoring, the handoff "four whats," huddle, and chain of command.
- **IHI "Idealized Design of Perinatal Care."** Two perinatal bundles covering induction and augmentation of labor.
- **Simulation.** Center-based high-fidelity or in-house low-fidelity drills targeting low-frequency, high-severity events: shoulder dystocia, hemorrhage, eclampsia.
- **Electronic fetal monitoring certification.** Institutional education in NICHD definitions, tracing review, study guides, then an objective examination (National Certification Corporation).
- **Obstetric patient safety committee and laborist model.** A multidisciplinary committee (obstetricians, anesthesiologists, pediatricians, nurses) sets priorities; the obstetric hospitalist ("laborist") provides immediate in-house coverage.

## Key Concepts

- Obstetrician-gynecologists are about 5% of US physicians but generate 15% of liability claims and 36% of total liability payments.
- Mean payment for one obstetric liability claim is approximately $500,000 to $1,900,000.
- 90.5% of obstetricians face at least one claim; mean 2.69 claims per physician.
- Defensive practice after a claim: 19.5% perform more cesarean deliveries, 19.5% abandon vaginal birth after cesarean, 21.4% reduce high-risk patients, 10.4% reduce deliveries, 6.5% stop practice.
- A near miss is a potentially harmful event that causes no harm; near-miss review is a leading preventive tool.
- Baseline SAQ data in one unit showed a 25-percentage-point physician-nurse gap on teamwork.
- Effective programs rest on three components: evidence-based standardization, improved communication, and a dedicated patient safety nurse (PSN) when finances allow.
- Randomized trials of safety interventions are rare; quality-improvement designs are the most feasible evidence source.
- Caregivers become "second victims"; recovery follows six stages, beginning with chaos and accident response and intrusive reflections.

## Anti-patterns

- A pure blame culture suppresses reporting and leaves the system defect in place.
- Absolute "no-blame" ignores at-risk and reckless behavior that requires accountability.
- Assuming a good outcome proves safe care; near misses then go unreported and uncorrected.
- Using simulation only for technical skills and skipping debriefing and teamwork objectives.
- Relying on unstructured verbal handoffs, which lose information at every transfer.
- Allowing mitigating speech, where a subordinate softens a concern instead of escalating it.
- Waiting for randomized proof before adopting checklists, time-outs, and standardization.

## Key Takeaways

1. Charter a multidisciplinary obstetric patient safety committee and, if resources permit, fund a dedicated PSN.
2. Measure safety culture with the SAQ or the AHRQ survey at baseline and serially, and act on gaps of 10 percentage points or more.
3. Standardize high-risk processes: magnesium and oxytocin labeling, shoulder dystocia documentation, labor progress forms, time-outs, and checklists.
4. Train all disciplines together in CRM tools (SBAR, CUS, two-challenge rule, four whats) in 4- to 8-hour sessions.
5. Run in-situ or center-based simulation for shoulder dystocia, obstetric hemorrhage, and eclampsia.
6. Apply just culture: support the second victim, prefer restorative remedies, and reserve privilege loss for repeated reckless behavior.
