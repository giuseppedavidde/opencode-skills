# Chapter 27: Placenta and Umbilical Cord Imaging

## Core Idea
Placental and cord findings are common, mostly benign, and clinically important only when they alter fetal growth, placental invasion, or the route of delivery. The chapter separates findings that need no action (central and marginal cord insertion, placental lakes) from findings that change management (velamentous insertion with vasa previa, placenta accreta spectrum, placenta previa, single umbilical artery). Diagnosis is made by transvaginal sonography or color Doppler, and management is driven by the risk for hemorrhage, not by the sonographic label itself.

## Frameworks Introduced
**Cord insertion categories.** Central, eccentric (more than 2 cm from the margin), marginal (at the margin), and velamentous (into the membranes beyond the margin). Velamentous insertion is diagnosed when at least 1 cm of umbilical vessels runs in the membranes beyond the placental edge. Vasa previa is excluded by showing vessels more than 1 cm from the endocervical os.

**Placenta previa categories.** Complete (bulk over the internal os); partial (edge covers the os and extends beyond it by up to 20 mm); marginal (edge within 20 mm of the os); low-lying (edge 21–40 mm from the os).

**Accreta spectrum.** Accreta 82% (invasion into myometrium, not full thickness); increta 12% (full myometrial thickness, not beyond serosa); percreta 6% (beyond serosa).

**Placental cystic lesions.** Lakes (enlarged intervillous spaces), cysts (near the fetal surface or cord insertion), and chorangioma (nonmalignant vascular tumor).

## Key Concepts
- Velamentous cord insertion occurs in 1–4% of singletons, 10% or more of twins, and 7.5% of placenta previa. Second-trimester ultrasound sensitivity is 60–70% and specificity >99%; sensitivity falls to 50% with a posterior placenta and 40% with a lateral fundus.
- Marginal insertion occurs in about 20% of multifetal gestations. Central and marginal insertion do not affect fetal outcome. Velamentous insertion raises the risk of cord avulsion at delivery, low birth weight, prematurity, and abnormal intrapartum heart patterns.
- Molar gestation: 1/1000 pregnancies in the United States, up to 1/100 in Asia. Complete mole: 46,XX (90%, single sperm) or 46,XY (10%); avascular villi, no embryo. Partial mole: triploid 69,XXY or tetraploid 92,XXXY; some vascularized villi and a fetus may be present. "Snowstorm" of echolucencies and echogenicities smaller than 5 mm; bilateral theca lutein cysts. Invasive mole risk 10%; choriocarcinoma 2–5%.
- Placenta accreta occurs in 1/300–500 pregnancies. Risk with previa by pregnancy number: first 3.8%, second 11%, third 40%, fourth 61%, fifth 67%, sixth or more 67%. Without previa the risk is 0.03% in the first pregnancy and rises to about 4.7% after six or more.
- Accreta sonographic signs: loss of the hypoechoic retroplacental zone, multiple lacunae ("Swiss cheese"), bridging vessels at the uterus-bladder interface, retroplacental myometrium <1 mm, and placental bulging into adjacent spaces. Diagnosis is unreliable before 20 weeks.
- Accreta management: scheduled cesarean delivery with hysterectomy at 34 weeks after antenatal steroids, unless bleeding dictates earlier delivery; multidisciplinary team; blood products available; do not attempt placental removal. Gadolinium is pregnancy category C and should be avoided or minimized.
- Placenta previa affects 0.5% of third-trimester pregnancies and is seen in 5% of mid-trimester scans. Resolution by delivery when diagnosed at 15–19, 20–23, 24–27, 28–31, and 32–35 weeks is 88%, 64%, 51%, 38%, and 27%.
- Previa management: confirm within 1–2 weeks of delivery; deliver uncomplicated previa at 36–37 weeks; deliver at 34–35 weeks if accreta is suspected; avoid amniocentesis for lung maturity; give corticosteroids; have blood available.
- Placental lakes are found in 20% of second-trimester scans and are usually benign. Cysts larger than 5 cm are associated with growth restriction and hemorrhage. Chorangioma is well defined, hypoechoic, and variable on color Doppler.
- Single umbilical artery (SUA) occurs in 1/200 singletons and up to 5% of twins; the left artery is absent in 60–70%. Structural anomalies occur in about 30%; with coexisting anomalies, 5–50% are aneuploid. Isolated SUA is not clearly linked to adverse neurologic outcome, though one study reported a 60% excess of children not completing compulsory schooling.

## Anti-patterns
- Calling a low-lying placenta a previa without transvaginal measurement of the distance to the internal os.
- Assuming a mid-trimester previa will persist; most resolve, and repeat imaging is needed at 33–35 weeks.
- Excluding accreta because the placenta is not previa. Prior cesarean and prior curettage carry risk without previa.
- Attempting manual placental removal when accreta is suspected; this causes torrential hemorrhage.
- Diagnosing vasa previa without excluding vessels within 1 cm of the os.
- Treating placental lakes as a cause of growth restriction; current data do not support this.
- Diagnosing SUA from a cord cross-section alone; use color Doppler over the fetal bladder.

## Key Takeaways
1. Measure the distance from the placental edge to the internal os by transvaginal scan and classify previa accordingly.
2. In any patient with prior cesarean and an anterior placenta, look specifically for accreta signs and plan delivery at a center with transfusion, ICU, and NICU capability.
3. Deliver uncomplicated previa at 36–37 weeks; deliver suspected accreta at 34–35 weeks after steroids, with hysterectomy planned.
4. Document cord insertion with color Doppler in every twin pregnancy and in every low-lying placenta.
5. When one umbilical artery is absent, perform a detailed anatomic survey, offer karyotype if anomalies coexist, and assess growth serially.
