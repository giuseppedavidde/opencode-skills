# Trades About to Happen
**Author**: David Weis | **Chapters**: 11 | **Generated**: 2026-05-28

## Core Frameworks & Mental Models

### The Story of the Lines
Weis's foundational framework: draw support/resistance lines, trend lines, channels, and reverse trend channels to frame price movement. Lines tell the story of the struggle between buyers and sellers. A confluence of lines (multiple lines converging at one price) often marks important turning points. Lines alone don't signal trades—they focus attention on where price/volume behavior matters.

### Clusters & Displacements
Price bars that close within a narrow price range (clusters) reveal equilibrium between supply and demand. When price displaces outside a cluster with no follow-through, it signals exhaustion. Clusters of closes around support/resistance lines indicate absorption. The tighter the cluster, the more explosive the eventual breakout.

### Springs (False Breakdowns)
A spring is a penetration below a support level or trading range that fails to follow through and reverses upward. The spring is Weis's highest-conviction setup. Key variants: (1) high-volume spring with secondary test, (2) low-volume spring reflecting exhausted supply, (3) spring in an uptrend (highest success rate). Springs in downtrends are "bottom picker's nightmares"—they fail more often than not.

### Upthrusts (False Breakouts)
A move above resistance that fails to follow through and reverses downward. Upthrusts are harder to trade than springs because the public loves buying new highs. Professional selling at tops is obscured under public buying. Upthrusts thrive in downtrends; in uptrends they rarely pan out. Multiple upthrusts can form in a top before markdown begins.

### Absorption
The process through which long liquidation, profit-taking, and new short selling are overcome. Clues: rising supports, volume increasing near resistance, lack of follow-through after threatening bars, prices pressing against resistance without giving ground, minor upthrusts that fail to produce breakdowns. Absorption at lows = "bag-holding" (large operators trapping shorts).

### Tape Reading (Weis Wave)
Weis's modern adaptation of Wyckoff's ticker tape methods. The Weis Wave converts time-based bar charts into wave charts that reflect how price actually moves (in waves, not equal time periods). Key elements: wave length (price distance), wave volume (cumulative force), wave duration (time). When buying waves shorten in length, volume, and duration, the trend is tiring. Selling waves that increase in these same dimensions signal growing institutional pressure.

### Shortening of the Thrust (SOT)
Diminished progress measured from high to high or low to low. Requires minimum three impulses. When SOT occurs on very low volume = momentum exhausted; on very heavy volume = large effort with little reward (institutional resistance). SOT is distinct from springs/upthrusts but often occurs alongside them.

### Effort vs. Reward (E/R)
Compare volume (effort) against price progress (reward). Big effort + little reward = impending reversal. Small effort + big reward = ease of movement confirming trend. This is the core analytical lens for every bar, wave, or tick.

### Point & Figure Projections
Count boxes across congestion zones on P&F charts to project price targets. Weis prefers breaking counts into phases (conservative to maximum). Congestion = preparation = potential energy. Renko bricks with volume and time data come closest to Wyckoff's original tape-reading charts.

### Principle of Contraction/Expansion (Crabel)
Markets alternate between contraction (narrow ranges, low volatility) and expansion (wide ranges, trend). NR7, 2Bar NR, 3Bar NR, ID/NR4 are specific setups. Contraction areas at support/resistance with volume confirmation offer high-probability entries. Weis integrates Crabel's quantified patterns with Wyckoff's contextual reading.
