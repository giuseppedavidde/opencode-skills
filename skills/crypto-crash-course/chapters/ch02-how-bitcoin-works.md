# Chapter 2: How Bitcoin Works

## Core Idea
Bitcoin is a decentralized peer-to-peer electronic cash system secured by proof-of-work mining. New coins are created at a predetermined rate, rewarding miners who validate transactions. The total supply is capped at 21 million, making Bitcoin deflationary by design.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Proof-of-Work Consensus**: Global "elections" every 10 minutes where the network reaches consensus on valid transactions. Miners solve cryptographic puzzles; first to solve adds the block and earns the reward.
- **Halving Economics**: Block reward halves every 4 years (50 → 25 → 12.5 → 6.25 BTC). This creates artificial scarcity and mimics gold mining becoming harder over time.
- **Miner Incentive Model**: Miners compete for block rewards + transaction fees. More computational power = higher chance of reward. This incentivizes network security.

## Key Concepts
- **Mining**: Computers running specialized software to verify and record transactions on the Bitcoin database
- **Block reward**: 50 BTC per block initially, halving every 4 years; approximately 21M total coins will ever exist
- **Limited supply**: Hard-coded into the protocol; no government or individual can change it without community consensus
- **Verification**: Every transaction must be verified by multiple nodes before being added to the chain
- **Nakamoto's withdrawal**: In 2011, Satoshi left Bitcoin to prove no single person controls the network

## Anti-patterns
- Believing "Bitcoin is dead" every time price drops — transaction volume has steadily increased
- Thinking mining is easy money — requires significant hardware investment and electricity
- Assuming the 21M cap is truly inviolable — community could theoretically vote to change it

## Key Takeaways
- Bitcoin is governed by code and mathematical principles, not human bankers
- Mining secures the network and creates new coins at a predictable, decreasing rate
- The fixed supply makes Bitcoin a deflationary asset unlike fiat currencies
