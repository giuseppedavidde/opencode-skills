# Chapter 2: The Illusory Validity of Subjective Technical Analysis

## Core Idea

Subjective technical analysis (TA) — methods too vague to be reduced to back-testable algorithms — is **not legitimate knowledge**. Its apparent validity is an illusion produced by systematic cognitive biases that cause human beings to perceive patterns, confirm existing beliefs, and overestimate their predictive abilities in uncertain domains like financial markets.

## Frameworks Introduced

### The Illusion of Validity Model
A network of mutually reinforcing cognitive errors that create false confidence in subjective TA:
- **Bounded Rationality** (Herbert Simon): Human information processing is limited to ~7 chunks of conscious memory and a maximum of 3 variables for configural thinking. Financial markets demand far more.
- **Linear vs. Configural Thinking**: Humans default to linear combining of variables, but market prediction requires configural thinking (interactions between variables). 5 binary indicators produce 32 distinct configurations — beyond unaided human capacity.
- **Pattern-Finding Predisposition**: Evolution rewarded pattern detection more than pattern validation. Humans developed a "voracious but indiscriminate appetite for predictive patterns" with no built-in ability to distinguish valid from invalid patterns.

### The Null Hypothesis Baseline
Before accepting any method's validity, begin with the assumption that it has **no predictive power**. This is the starting point for any rigorous evaluation.

## Key Concepts

### Cognitive Biases That Destroy Objective Judgment

| Bias | Mechanism | TA Consequence |
|------|-----------|----------------|
| **Overconfidence Bias** | Self-assessments err high; most extreme for difficult tasks like predicting stock prices | Analysts believe their chart reading is much more accurate than it is |
| **Confirmation Bias** | Seeking and overweighting evidence that confirms existing beliefs; ignoring disconfirming evidence | Seeing only confirming patterns and rationalizing away false signals |
| **Self-Attribution Bias** | Attributing successes to skill, failures to external factors | "I was right because I'm skilled; I was wrong because the market manipulated" |
| **Hindsight Bias** | After outcomes are known, believing they were predictable | "I knew it would break out" — after the breakout occurred |
| **Illusory Correlation** | Perceiving relationships between variables where none exist, especially with binary asymmetric data | Seeing a reliable pattern in a random head-and-shoulders formation |
| **Illusion of Control** | Overestimating one's ability to control outcomes | Believing that diligent chart analysis confers control over unpredictable markets |
| **Representativeness Heuristic** | Judging probability by similarity to stereotypes; ignoring base rates and sample sizes | Treating a five-bar pattern as a reliable signal because it "looks like" a textbook example |
| **Texas Sharpshooter Fallacy** | Drawing the target after shooting — defining success criteria after seeing results | Redefining support/resistance levels after price action invalidates the originals |

### Why Subjective TA Is "Worse Than Wrong"
A wrong claim can be falsified by evidence. Subjective TA's propositions lack sufficient specificity to be empirically challenged — they are **untestable**. Without falsifiability, falsehoods accumulate indefinitely because there is no mechanism to excise them.

### The Expert vs. Algorithm Paradox
Meta-analysis of over 100 peer-reviewed studies comparing expert judgment with statistical rules: **statistical rules were more accurate in 96% of cases**. Average correlation between prediction and outcome: expert judgment = 0.33, objective model = 0.64. Experts underperform because they fail to combine information with the consistency of a formal mathematical model.

### Arditti's Random Chart Study
Expert chart readers **cannot reliably distinguish** actual market price histories from randomly generated pseudo-price data — charts that are, by design, impossible to predict. The random charts looked just like authentic ones to experienced technicians.

## Anti-patterns

- **Eyeball Backtesting**: Scrolling through historical charts and noting "this pattern worked here" — confirmation bias guarantees you'll find supportive instances and miss contradictory ones.
- **Post-Hoc Rationalization**: Explaining every failed signal after the fact by finding something you "missed" on the chart. This preserves the illusion that the method works.
- **Vague Signal Definitions**: Using terms like "strong breakout," "significant support," or "clear trend" without operational definitions. Vagueness is the immune system of false knowledge.
- **Appeal to "Obvious Validity"**: Claiming patterns are "too evident" to be illusory. Random data contains equally compelling patterns — "obviousness" is not a valid standard.
- **Anchoring on Anecdotal Success**: Citing specific winning trades as proof a method works while dismissing losses as exceptions. Selective memory of wins combined with self-attribution bias.
- **Indicator Overload without Formal Combination Rule**: Using 5+ indicators but combining them in a subjective, inconsistent manner. Without a formal rule, you're just adding noise.

## Key Takeaways

1. **Subjective TA cannot be separated from the cognitive machinery that produces it.** The biases are not bugs in an otherwise rational system — they are features of how human cognition evolved, and they operate automatically beneath conscious awareness.

2. **The only valid knowledge is testable knowledge.** If a method cannot be reduced to an algorithm and back-tested, its claims carry no cognitive content and cannot legitimately be called knowledge.

3. **Statistical models beat human experts.** In virtually every domain studied (medical diagnosis, academic prediction, stock price forecasting, bankruptcy prediction), formal linear models outperform subjective expert judgment.

4. **The first step toward valid TA is radical skepticism.** Assume every pattern, every rule, every method is worthless until proven otherwise with objective statistical evidence that accounts for randomness and data-mining bias.

5. **Objectification is the path forward.** Even Elliott Wave, the quintessential subjective method, can in principle be objectified (e.g., EWAVES algorithm). The test: can it distinguish real from random price data, and do its signals generate statistically significant profits?
