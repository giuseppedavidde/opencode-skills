# Chapter 7: Theories of Nonrandom Price Motion

## Core Idea
TA is justifiable only if price movements exhibit **nonrandom behavior** at least some of the time. This chapter presents theoretical foundations — from behavioral finance to risk transfer premiums — explaining why markets are not perfectly efficient and why predictability can exist without violating rational economic principles.

## Frameworks Introduced

### 1. The Efficient Markets Hypothesis (EMH) and Its Limitations
EMH contends that prices fully reflect all available information, implying price changes are random and unpredictable. Three forms:
- **Weak form**: Past price/volume data cannot predict future returns
- **Semi-strong form**: All public information is instantly incorporated
- **Strong form**: Even inside information cannot beat the market

**Critical flaws**: EMH assumes (1) rational investors, (2) uncorrelated errors, (3) unlimited arbitrage. All three fail in reality. The Grossman-Stiglitz paradox: if markets were perfectly efficient, there would be no incentive to gather information — yet information gathering is costly, so some inefficiency must exist to compensate for it.

### 2. Behavioral Finance Theories
Psychological and social factors create systematic mispricing:

**Psychological factors**:
- **Conservatism bias**: Investors underreact to new information, causing **momentum** — trends persist longer than EMH predicts
- **Overconfidence / self-attribution bias**: Investors overreact to confirming evidence, causing eventual **reversals**
- **Anchoring**: Fixation on arbitrary reference points (52-week highs, round numbers)
- **Representativeness heuristic**: Seeing patterns in random sequences, extrapolating short trends too far

**Social factors**:
- **Herding and information cascades**: Investors imitate others' actions, amplifying trends beyond fundamentals
- **Positive feedback trading**: Price increases attract more buyers, creating self-reinforcing cycles (Ponzi-like dynamics)

### 3. Major Behavioral Hypotheses
- **BSV Hypothesis** (Barberis-Shleifer-Vishny): Alternating underreaction (momentum) and overreaction (reversals) driven by conservatism and representativeness
- **DHS Hypothesis** (Daniel-Hirshleifer-Subrahmanyam): Overconfidence about private information causes overreaction; self-attribution bias sustains it
- **HS Hypothesis** (Hong-Stein): Interaction between "news watchers" (fundamental information) and "momentum traders" (price trends) creates momentum followed by reversal

### 4. Risk Transfer Premiums
In futures markets, trend-following profits can be explained as **compensation for risk transference**: commercial hedgers pay a premium to speculators who accept price risk. This provides a rational, non-behavioral theory for why trends exist — trend followers earn returns not from market inefficiency but from providing an insurance service.

## Key Concepts
- **Noise traders**: Investors trading on non-fundamental information create mispricings that rational arbitrageurs cannot fully correct due to limits of arbitrage
- **Stale information**: Market data (price, volume, etc.) is "stale" relative to forward-looking fundamentals — yet behavioral biases can cause it to retain predictive power
- **Empirical anomalies**: Momentum (Jegadeesh-Titman), post-earnings-announcement drift, value premium (Fama-French), 52-week high effect — all challenge EMH
- **Limits of arbitrage**: Even when mispricing is identified, arbitrageurs face fundamental risk, noise trader risk, and implementation costs

## Anti-patterns
- **EMH nihilism**: Concluding that because EMH has flaws, all TA methods must work — each must still be empirically validated
- **Theory-free testing**: Accepting a proftable back-test without theoretical grounding — an isolated result may simply be a statistical fluke
- **Over-extrapolating anomalies**: Assuming an anomaly documented in academic literature will persist after discovery and widespread implementation
- **Confusing necessary with sufficient**: Nonrandom price motion justifies the TA enterprise but does NOT validate any specific rule
- **Ignoring risk-based explanations**: Attributing to mispricing what may simply be compensation for bearing systematic risk

## Key Takeaways
1. EMH is a useful null hypothesis, but behavioral finance provides compelling theories for **why markets are not always efficient**.
2. The interaction of psychological biases (underreaction, overreaction) and social dynamics (herding, feedback) can produce **momentum and reversal patterns**.
3. Risk transfer premiums offer a rational explanation for trend-following profits in futures markets without invoking irrationality.
4. A successful back-test is **more credible** when supported by sound theory — theory and evidence reinforce each other.
5. Behavioral finance does not validate any specific TA method — each method must still pass rigorous statistical tests.
