# Chapter 7: Hyperthyroidism

## Core Idea
Hyperthyroidism complicates 0.2–0.7% of pregnancies and Graves' disease causes 95%. Diagnosis requires suppressed TSH with elevated free T4; free T3 is measured when TSH is suppressed but free T4 is normal, which occurs in 5%. Treatment aims to keep free T4 in the high-normal range with the lowest effective thionamide dose, regardless of TSH, because over-treatment causes fetal hypothyroidism and goiter. Propylthiouracil is used through 16 weeks and then switched to methimazole to balance methimazole embryopathy against PTU hepatotoxicity. Radioiodine is absolutely contraindicated. Gestational transient biochemical thyrotoxicosis, common with hyperemesis, needs no testing, follow-up, or treatment because it resolves spontaneously.

## Recommendations and Grades
- Screen women with signs or symptoms of hyperthyroidism with TSH and free T4. *(Guideline)*
- Measure thyroid-stimulating immunoglobulin at the first prenatal visit in clinical hyperthyroidism; a positive result supports Graves' disease. Repeat at 18–22 weeks if elevated, and in the third trimester if still elevated, to identify fetuses at risk.
- Treat overt hyperthyroidism with PTU or methimazole, keeping free T4 slightly above or in the high-normal range. *(No RCTs available)*
- Use PTU through 16 weeks' gestation, then switch to methimazole for the rest of pregnancy; the equivalent dose is about 1:20 (5 mg methimazole = 100 mg PTU).
- Measure TSH and free T4 every 4 weeks until free T4 is consistently high-normal, then every trimester. *(Guideline)*
- Do not test for or treat thyroid function in hyperemesis gravidarum unless other overt signs of hyperthyroidism are present.
- Do not identify or treat subclinical hyperthyroidism in pregnancy; it is not associated with adverse outcomes and treatment exposes the fetus unnecessarily.
- Use propranolol 10–40 mg orally every 6–8 hours for rapid symptom control, targeting a heart rate of 80–90 bpm without palpitations, and stop it within 2–6 weeks.
- Never give radioiodine in pregnancy; advise against conception for 6–12 months after treatment and against breastfeeding for 120 days.
- Choose the second trimester for thyroidectomy when surgery is required.
- Treat thyroid storm with PTU, saturated solution of potassium iodide, dexamethasone, and propranolol.

## Key Concepts
- Clinical hyperthyroidism: suppressed TSH and elevated free T4. Subclinical: TSH <0.1 mU/L with normal free T4 and free T3.
- Graves' disease: thyroid-stimulating immunoglobulins; thyroid-stimulating immunoglobulin coexists with TSH-binding inhibitory immunoglobulin in 30%.
- Signs: tachycardia >100 bpm that does not slow with Valsalva, wide pulse pressure, goiter, lid lag, lid retraction, pretibial myxedema.
- Gestational transient thyrotoxicosis affects 1–3% of pregnancies, follows the human chorionic gonadotropin peak at 10–12 weeks, and resolves by 14–18 weeks.
- PTU: start 100–600 mg/day in three doses; typical 200–400 mg/day, or 50–150 mg every 8 hours; onset 2–3 weeks, full effect 6–8 weeks; doses fall as pregnancy advances.
- Methimazole: 5–30 mg/day, typical 10–20 mg/day, once daily or twice daily if severe.
- PTU >200 mg/day may cause fetal goiter.
- About 30% of women can stop antithyroid therapy and remain euthyroid.
- Agranulocytosis occurs in 0.1–0.4%; stop the drug and check the white count for fever, sore throat, malaise, or gingivitis.
- Fetal risks: thionamide-induced hypothyroidism and goiter (rarely needing therapy), or TSI-mediated hyperthyroidism. IQ of exposed children is normal.
- Fetal surveillance: heart rate >180 bpm suggests tachycardia; weekly non-stress tests from 32–34 weeks if uncontrolled or TSI elevated; growth ultrasound every 4 weeks if clinically hyperthyroid; fetal thyroid circumference at 32 weeks has 92% sensitivity and 100% specificity for clinically relevant dysfunction.
- Fetal blood sampling is rarely indicated; consider it with maternal TSI 200–500% plus hydrops, goiter, tachycardia, cardiomegaly, FGR, or a previously affected fetus. Fetal hyperthyroidism is not expected when TSI <130%.
- Neonatal Graves' disease affects 2–5% of neonates of mothers with Graves', independent of maternal thyroid function; risk is high when the TSI index is ≥5. Signs include tachycardia >160 bpm, goiter, FGR, advanced bone age, craniosynostosis, and hydrops.
- Thyroid storm occurs in 1% of hyperthyroid women, precipitated by labor, infection, pre-eclampsia, severe anemia, or surgery.
- Thyroid storm regimen: PTU 1000 mg orally immediately, then 200 mg every 6 hours; SSKI 5 drops every 8 hours starting 1–2 hours after PTU, or sodium iodide 500–1000 mg IV every 8 hours, or Lugol's 10 drops every 8 hours, or lithium carbonate 300 mg every 6 hours; dexamethasone 2 mg IV every 6 hours for four doses or hydrocortisone 100 mg IV every 8 hours for three doses; propranolol 20–80 mg every 4–6 hours or 1–2 mg IV every 5 minutes to 6 mg, then 1–10 mg IV every 4 hours.
- Postpartum: both PTU and methimazole are safe with breastfeeding; 75% of women in remission relapse or develop postpartum thyroiditis.
- Iodine: 150 µg/day before pregnancy, 250 µg/day during pregnancy and breastfeeding.

## Anti-patterns
- Testing or treating thyroid function in hyperemesis gravidarum: the biochemical picture is transient and self-limited, and antithyroid drugs add first-trimester teratogenic risk without benefit.
- Radioiodine at any stage of pregnancy: absolutely contraindicated; after 10 weeks it ablates the fetal thyroid and termination should be offered.
- Methimazole as the first-trimester default: twofold increase in fetal malformation compared with PTU.
- Treating subclinical hyperthyroidism: no outcome benefit and unnecessary fetal drug exposure.
- Prolonged beta-blockade: risk of FGR, fetal bradycardia, hypoglycemia, and blunted hypoxemic stress response.
- Iodine for longer than 2 weeks: can cause fetal goiter.
- Assuming thyroid storm can wait for laboratory confirmation: diagnosis is clinical and treatment starts immediately.
- Missing the pediatrician handover: neonatal Graves' disease can appear despite normal maternal thyroid function.

## Key Takeaways
1. Confirm hyperthyroidism with TSH and free T4, and measure TSI to separate Graves' disease from gestational thyrotoxicosis.
2. Use PTU through 16 weeks, then methimazole, titrated to high-normal free T4 — not to TSH.
3. Recheck every 4 weeks until stable, then each trimester, and reduce the dose as pregnancy advances.
4. Monitor the fetus for tachycardia >180 bpm, growth restriction, and goiter; check TSI again at 18–22 weeks and in the third trimester.
5. Treat thyroid storm immediately with PTU, iodide, dexamethasone, and propranolol, and never use radioiodine in pregnancy.
