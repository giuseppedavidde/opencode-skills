# Chapter 54: Fetal and Neonatal Alloimmune Thrombocytopenia

## Core Idea
Fetal and neonatal alloimmune thrombocytopenia (FNAIT) is fetal platelet destruction by maternal antibodies against fetal human platelet antigens (HPA) inherited from the father. Unlike Rh disease, it can affect a first pregnancy, maternal antibody titers do not predict outcome, and intracranial hemorrhage is the feared complication, occurring in 10–30% and mostly before 28 weeks. Because fetal thrombocytopenia cannot be seen on ultrasound, management is risk-based: intravenous immunoglobulin, with or without steroids, is given empirically according to the prior sibling's history, and fetal blood sampling is reserved for deciding mode of delivery.

## Recommendations and Grades
- Do not introduce routine universal maternal HPA typing. Nearly 15,000 pregnancies would need to be screened to identify one case of intracranial hemorrhage for possible prevention, and no major organization considers HPA-1a typing an appropriate routine prenatal test. Grade: not stated.
- Diagnose FNAIT by fetal or neonatal thrombocytopenia (<150,000/μL) plus identification of a paternal, fetal, or neonatal platelet antigen with maternal antibodies to that antigen. Test parents in a reference laboratory. Grade: not stated.
- Determine paternal antigen zygosity: recurrence risk is 100% if the father is homozygous and 50% if heterozygous. Offer fetal genotyping by amniocentesis, which is preferred over chorionic villus sampling because of theoretical concern that CVS exacerbates the maternal immune response. Grade: not stated.
- Treat by risk category, escalating without fetal blood sampling. A 2016 randomized trial of 99 women showed that omitting FBS with empiric escalation produced similar birth platelet counts to FBS-directed escalation, and that escalated therapy was effective; "current recommendation is escalate therapy without FBS." Grade as stated: 2016 RCT, n = 99; 2017 systematic review concluded a non-invasive approach with weekly IVIG with or without steroids is preferred.
- Standard risk (prior infant with thrombocytopenia but no intracranial hemorrhage): at 20 weeks, IVIG 1 g/kg/week plus prednisone 0.5 mg/kg/day (alternative IVIG 2 g/kg/week); at 32 weeks, IVIG 2 g/kg/week plus prednisone 0.5 mg/kg/day; deliver at 37–38 weeks by cesarean. Grade: not stated.
- High risk (prior sibling with intracranial hemorrhage in the third trimester or neonatal period): at 12 weeks, IVIG 1–2 g/kg/week; at 20 weeks, add prednisone 0.5 mg/kg/day or increase IVIG to 2 g/kg/week; at 28 weeks, IVIG 2 g/kg/week plus prednisone 0.5 mg/kg/day; deliver at 35–36 weeks by cesarean. Grade: not stated.
- Very high risk (prior sibling with intracranial hemorrhage before 28 weeks): at 12 weeks, IVIG 2 g/kg/week; at 20 weeks, add prednisone 1 mg/kg/day; deliver at 35–36 weeks by cesarean. Grade: not stated.
- Permit vaginal delivery only when fetal blood sampling at 35–36 weeks shows more than 100,000 platelets/μL. Grade: not stated.

## Key Concepts
- Incidence: 1/1000 to 1/1500 births; the most common cause of severe neonatal thrombocytopenia. HPA-1a sensitization is the commonest and most severe cause; in Asian populations HPA-4 incompatibility predominates. About 1 in 42 pregnancies is HPA-incompatible, but FNAIT develops in only 1 in 10 of these.
- Natural history: 90% of affected neonates have diffuse petechiae; 10–30% have intracranial hemorrhage; about 75% of hemorrhages occur antenatally, as early as 20 weeks, and approximately 54% before 28 weeks. Neonatal mortality from intracranial hemorrhage is 5–13%.
- Only HPA-1a antigen and a past history of intracranial hemorrhage predict more severe thrombocytopenia. Maternal antibody titers do not predict outcome.
- Diagnostic testing: maternal platelet antibody first; if positive, perform maternal and paternal HPA testing simultaneously. Reference laboratories typically test HPA-1a and b, 3a and b, 4a and b, and 5a and b, but cannot test every platelet antigen.
- Screening arithmetic: HPA-1a alloimmunization is detected in about 2 per 1000 pregnancies; severe FNAIT occurs in about 31% of immunized pregnancies; intracranial hemorrhage in about 10% of severe cases. Twenty-five percent of FNAIT is not caused by the commonest antigen.
- IVIG: given as a weekly infusion over 6–12 hours. Response rate 75%, with a 98.7% success rate for preventing intracranial hemorrhage. Half of non-responders improve with high-dose prednisone 1 mg/kg/day (60 mg/day). Dexamethasone has not been shown to be beneficial and is associated with oligohydramnios and FGR.
- Fetal blood sampling: 2.8% risk of perinatal loss per procedure; 11% overall complication rate, most commonly emergency cesarean for non-reassuring fetal status. Procedure-related loss is higher when the first intrauterine transfusion is before 20 weeks (5%).
- Delivery precautions: avoid maternal abdominal trauma, external cephalic version, fetal scalp lead, vacuum, and forceps; NSAIDs are contraindicated. There is no evidence that cesarean delivery itself prevents intracranial hemorrhage.
- Neonatal management: IVIG, IV steroids, and antigen-compatible platelets until recovery, usually by 7–10 days of age. Maternal antigen-negative, plasma-depleted, washed, irradiated, packed platelets should always be available. One milliliter of platelets raises the count by about 5000/μL (10 mL = 50,000; 20 mL = 100,000); neonatologists often transfuse 10 mL/kg.
- Recurrence: close to 100% in antigen-positive fetuses, and typically more severe than the previous affected pregnancy. Options include sperm donation from an HPA-1b/1b donor or IVF with preimplantation genetic diagnosis when the partner is HPA-1a/1b.

## Anti-patterns
- Universal HPA-1a screening: the number needed to screen is prohibitive and no body recommends it.
- Using ultrasound to detect fetal thrombocytopenia: it cannot be done.
- Relying on maternal antibody titers to grade severity: they do not predict outcome.
- Serial fetal platelet transfusions as primary therapy: abandoned because of procedure-related risk.
- Dexamethasone for fetal therapy: no benefit and it causes oligohydramnios and growth restriction.
- Routine fetal blood sampling to monitor therapy: a randomized trial supports empiric escalation instead.

## Key Takeaways
- Classify risk from the prior sibling: no intracranial hemorrhage, hemorrhage in the third trimester or neonatal period, or hemorrhage before 28 weeks.
- Start IVIG at 12 weeks in high-risk and very high-risk pregnancies, and at 20 weeks in standard risk.
- Escalate by schedule without fetal blood sampling; deliver at 37–38 weeks (standard risk) or 35–36 weeks (high and very high risk) by cesarean.
- Reserve fetal blood sampling at 35–36 weeks for women who want a trial of labor, and allow it only above 100,000/μL.
- Have compatible maternal platelets ready at delivery and expect neonatal recovery by 7–10 days.
