# Chapter 12: Alimentary Tract Diseases

## Core Idea
This chapter covers biliary disease and appendicitis in pregnancy. Gallstones are common — biliary sludge appears in up to 30% of pregnant women and stones in 20% of multiparous women — but acute cholecystitis complicates only 0.1% of pregnancies. Pregnancy and the postpartum period increase gallstone and cholecystitis risk through gallbladder hypomotility and more lithogenic bile. Ultrasound is the first-line image for both cholelithiasis and cholecystitis, with MRCP or ERCP used for suspected choledocholithiasis. Laparoscopic cholecystectomy is recommended for acute cholecystitis, and laparoscopic appendectomy for acute appendicitis in any trimester. Pregnancy does not change the mode of delivery for any of these conditions.

## Recommendations and Grades
- Diagnose acute cholecystitis with the 2018 Tokyo Guidelines: local signs plus systemic signs for a suspected diagnosis, adding imaging for a definite diagnosis. *(III)*
- Hospitalize women with suspected acute cholecystitis, obtain a surgical consultation, and start nothing by mouth, intravenous fluids, opioid analgesia, and broad-spectrum antibiotics for 12–24 hours.
- Perform laparoscopic cholecystectomy for acute cholecystitis; it is unequivocally recommended with sepsis, ileus, or perforation.
- Position the patient in the left lateral position during surgery, use an open technique for umbilical port insertion, avoid high intraperitoneal pressure, and keep electrocautery away from the uterus.
- Give antenatal corticosteroids before surgery to women with a viable premature fetus, and obtain a neonatology consultation.
- Consider percutaneous cholecystostomy when surgery or anesthesia is contraindicated.
- Investigate suspected choledocholithiasis with MRCP or ERCP when the bile duct is dilated, liver tests are abnormal, or pancreatitis is present; a common bile duct >6 mm is suspicious.
- Perform ERCP with sphincterotomy and stone extraction for symptomatic choledocholithiasis, ideally in the second trimester, with fetal monitoring before and after. *(II-2; systematic review and meta-analysis)*
- For biliary pancreatitis, perform early ERCP with sphincterotomy in the first and third trimester, with laparoscopic cholecystectomy in the second trimester or after delivery in the third.
- Use MRI rather than computed tomography for suspected appendicitis in pregnancy; MRI reduces the need for CT by 50% and diagnoses acute appendicitis in 90%.
- Perform laparoscopic appendectomy for acute uncomplicated appendicitis in any trimester; it shortens operative time and hospital stay and reduces postoperative complications compared with open surgery. *(I; meta-analysis of observational studies)*
- Reserve cesarean delivery for obstetric indications in cholelithiasis, cholecystitis, choledocholithiasis, biliary pancreatitis, and appendicitis.

## Key Concepts
- Up to 50% of pregnant women with cholelithiasis are asymptomatic; biliary colic is sudden right upper quadrant or epigastric pain radiating to the interscapular area or right scapula.
- Gallstones occur in 7% of nulliparous and 20% of multiparous women; 80% are cholesterol or mixed stones. Risk remains elevated for up to 5 years postpartum.
- Gallbladder disease is the second most common indication for non-obstetric surgery in pregnancy.
- Ultrasound findings of cholecystitis: wall thickness >3–5 mm, pericholecystic fluid, gallstones, and sonographic Murphy's sign.
- Tokyo severity grades: grade III has organ dysfunction (dopamine ≥5 µg/kg/min or any norepinephrine, altered consciousness, PaO2/FiO2 <300, oliguria or creatinine >2.0 mg/dL, PT-INR >1.5, platelets <100,000/mm³); grade II has white count >18,000/mm³, palpable tender mass, symptoms >72 hours, or marked local inflammation; grade I is everything else.
- Conservative management of cholecystitis fails in 40–70%, and about 27% of failures need cholecystectomy; up to 60% of pregnant patients now undergo laparoscopic cholecystectomy.
- Choledocholithiasis occurs in about 1 in 1000 pregnant women. Post-ERCP pancreatitis is more frequent in pregnancy (12% versus 5%).
- Fetal radiation dose during ERCP stays below 200 mrad when fluoroscopy is limited to under 1 minute; shield the pelvis and lower abdomen.
- Biliary pancreatitis is the most common cause of pancreatitis in pregnancy; incidence of all pancreatitis is 1 in 1000 to 10,000, and cholelithiasis causes 96%. About 85% of cases are mild or moderate with 0.8% mortality; the 15% that are severe reach 25% mortality.
- Ranson's criteria: age >55, white count >16,000/mm³, glucose >11 mmol/L, AST >250 IU/L, LDH >350 IU/L at admission; at 48 hours, calcium <2.0 mmol/L, hematocrit fall >10%, PaO2 <60 mmHg, BUN rise >1.98 mmol/L, base deficit >4 mmol/L, fluid sequestration >6 L. Score ≥3 predicts severe disease; mortality is 2% at 0–2, 15% at 3–4, 40% at 5–6, and 100% at 7–8.
- Acute appendicitis occurs in up to 41 per 10,000 pregnancies and is the most common non-obstetric surgical problem; it is most frequent in the second trimester.
- Appendicitis complications: preterm delivery up to 30% and fetal loss up to 20%, mostly with perforation.
- Indomethacin is not recommended for cholecystitis; avoid it after 32 weeks because of ductus arteriosus closure and oligohydramnios.

## Anti-patterns
- Treating acute cholecystitis with conservative therapy as the definitive plan: relapse is common and complications are higher than with surgery.
- Delaying necessary surgery because of pregnancy: indications are the same as outside pregnancy.
- Using computed tomography as the first test for appendicitis: MRI is preferred and ultrasound is less sensitive.
- Repeating imaging after an equivocal study within the same admission is acceptable, but clinical deterioration should prompt surgery rather than further delay.
- Indomethacin to reverse gallbladder inflammation: not recommended.
- Assuming a normal ultrasound excludes choledocholithiasis: ultrasound is insensitive; use MRCP or ERCP when suspicion persists.
- Routine cesarean for biliary or appendiceal disease: mode of delivery is unaffected.
- Operating without fetal assessment: document fetal heart tones before and after in previable pregnancies, and monitor continuously before and after in viable pregnancies.

## Key Takeaways
1. Use ultrasound first for biliary disease; add MRCP or ERCP for a dilated duct, abnormal liver tests, or pancreatitis.
2. Treat acute cholecystitis with antibiotics and laparoscopic cholecystectomy; operate urgently for sepsis, ileus, or perforation.
3. Perform ERCP in the second trimester when possible, limit fluoroscopy to under 1 minute, and monitor the fetus.
4. For biliary pancreatitis, do early ERCP with sphincterotomy and interval cholecystectomy.
5. Use MRI and laparoscopic appendectomy for suspected appendicitis at any gestational age, and keep cesarean for obstetric indications.
