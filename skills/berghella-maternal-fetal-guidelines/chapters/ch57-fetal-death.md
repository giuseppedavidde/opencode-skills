# Chapter 57: Fetal Death

## Core Idea
Fetal death is death before complete expulsion or extraction, irrespective of gestational duration, shown by absence of breathing, heartbeat, cord pulsation, or voluntary movement. There is no uniformly accepted definition, but stillbirth is the preferred term for deaths at 20 weeks or more or above 350 g. Diagnosis is confirmed by ultrasound. The clinical priorities are to deliver safely, to obtain the three most informative tests (autopsy, placental and cord examination, and genetic evaluation), and to plan the next pregnancy.

## Recommendations and Grades
- Confirm fetal death by ultrasound, documenting absence of fetal cardiac activity. Grade: not stated.
- Recommend induction of labor in women with fetal death unless they are already in labor. Grade: not stated.
- For fetal death at about 14–28 weeks, use misoprostol: 200–400 µg vaginally every 4 hours, 400 µg orally every 4 hours, 200 µg buccal, or 600 µg vaginally every 12 hours. This is "the most cost-effective method of delivery, with acceptable side effects." Grade: not stated.
- After 28 weeks, use standard obstetric protocols: balloon catheter, misoprostol, or oxytocin for induction. Grade: not stated.
- Screen for coagulopathy only if the fetal death is more than 4 weeks from delivery; check fibrinogen, platelet count, prothrombin time, and activated partial thromboplastin time before neuraxial anaesthesia or other invasive procedures. Grade: not stated.
- Send the fetus for autopsy and the placenta, membranes, and cord for gross and microscopic examination; obtain fetal and placental tissue for cytogenetics, and amniotic fluid for cytogenetics before delivery if possible. Grade: not stated.
- Offer dilation and evacuation as an alternative to induction at 14–24 weeks: complication rates are 4% for D&E versus 29% for labor induction in this window. Counsel that autopsy is of very limited value after D&E. Grade: not stated.
- Plan the next pregnancy as in Table 57.3: growth scans about every 4 weeks from 28 weeks, fetal movement counting from 28 weeks, antepartum surveillance from 32 weeks or 1–2 weeks before the gestational age of the previous stillbirth, and planned induction at 39 weeks. Early term delivery at 37 0/7–38 6/7 weeks may be undertaken for severe anxiety after discussing the increased neonatal risks. Grade: not stated.

## Key Concepts
- Definitions: fetal death at 20–27 weeks is intermediate; late fetal death is after 28 weeks. Unexplained fetal death is death with no identifiable cause after complete evaluation.
- Epidemiology: about 2.6 million stillbirths annually worldwide, 98% in low- and middle-income countries and two thirds in Southeast Asia and Sub-Saharan Africa. In the United States there are about 23,000 stillbirths annually; the rate fell from 5.7 to 5.6 per 1000 between 2007 and 2018.
- Fetal risk factors and frequencies: congenital malformations 15–20%; chromosomal or genetic abnormalities 8–13% (monosomy X, trisomies 21, 18, and 13). Maternal risk factors include chronic hypertension, pre-eclampsia, diabetes, renal disease, lupus, antiphospholipid syndrome, cholestasis, alloimmunization, obesity, smoking, and cocaine or alcohol use.
- Pre-delivery investigations (Table 57.2): amniotic fluid for cytogenetics; coagulopathy screen only if death is more than 4 weeks from delivery; CBC and antibody screen; Kleihauer–Betke or flow cytometry; lupus anticoagulant, anticardiolipin IgM and IgG, and anti-β2-glycoprotein IgM and IgG; syphilis testing (RPR or VDRL); glucose screening (oral glucose tolerance test or hemoglobin A1c) if not done in pregnancy; urine drug screen if drug use is suspected or in abruption. Post-delivery: autopsy and placental examination; fetal and placental tissue for cytogenetics.
- Autopsy value: it reduces unexplained fetal deaths by at least 10%, altered counseling and recurrence risk in 26% of cases at one institution, and combined with clinical, laboratory, and placental data improved identification of probable cause to 74%. If autopsy is declined, consider head-sparing autopsy, MRI, or minimally invasive autopsy. Cord entanglement occurs in about 25% of normal pregnancies, so it must be interpreted cautiously; cord abnormalities caused 19% of stillbirths in one study.
- Expectant management hazards: retention of a dead fetus can cause chronic consumptive coagulopathy from gradual release of thromboplastin, usually after 4 weeks but sometimes earlier; coagulation abnormalities occur in about 3–4% of uncomplicated fetal deaths over the next 4–8 weeks and rise with abruption or uterine perforation. Expectant management also lengthens the interval to delivery, degrading autopsy information, and is psychologically difficult.
- Misoprostol: successful expulsion, mostly within 24 hours, in 80–100% of cases. Vaginal misoprostol is as effective as other prostaglandin preparations and more effective than oral. 600 µg vaginally at 12-hour intervals has fewer adverse effects than 6-hour intervals.
- D&E versus induction at 14–24 weeks: overall complication rate 4% versus 29%; similar rates of transfusion, infection, cervical laceration, organ damage, and readmission. Laminaria lowers D&E complications; misoprostol lowers complications of medical termination. D&E is less expensive and more effective than misoprostol induction.
- Prior cesarean: risk of uterine rupture about 0.4% with one prior low transverse incision, up to 9% with two or more, and up to 50% with a prior vertical incision. After 28 weeks with a prior low transverse incision, oxytocin and a Foley bulb for ripening may be used, and trial of labor remains preferable; cesarean should generally be avoided because there is no fetal benefit.
- Recurrence and future risk: stillbirth recurs 2.5- to 10-fold more often; in subsequent pregnancies preterm birth OR 2.8 (95% CI 1.9–4.2), pre-eclampsia OR 3.1 (95% CI 1.7–5.7), and abruption OR 9.4 (95% CI 4.5–19.7). Conception within 12 months of stillbirth was not associated with worse outcomes than 24–59 months.
- Postpartum care: warn the family that results may take 2–3 months and that a cause may never be found; offer to see and hold the infant, keepsake items, and grief counseling before discharge, with referral to bereavement support or mental health services. Staff also need counseling support.

## Anti-patterns
- Expectant management as a default: coagulopathy, worse autopsy yield, and psychological burden.
- Omitting autopsy or placental pathology: these tests most often establish the cause.
- Routine infection or thrombophilia testing without clinical suspicion: the text states routine testing is not supported where evidence is lacking.
- Cesarean delivery for fetal death: there is no fetal benefit, and rupture risk rises steeply with prior scars.
- Reassuring a family that no cause will be found before the workup is complete, or promising that all results will be normal.

## Key Takeaways
- Confirm death by ultrasound, then offer induction; do not wait for spontaneous labor.
- Use misoprostol for deaths at 14–28 weeks and standard induction agents after 28 weeks.
- Order the three highest-yield tests: autopsy, placental and cord histology, and cytogenetics.
- Screen for coagulopathy only when death is more than 4 weeks from delivery or before invasive procedures.
- Plan the next pregnancy with growth scans and movement counting from 28 weeks, surveillance from 32 weeks, and delivery at 39 weeks.
