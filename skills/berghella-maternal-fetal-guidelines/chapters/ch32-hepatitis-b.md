# Chapter 32: Hepatitis B

## Core Idea
The single goal of hepatitis B management in pregnancy is prevention of vertical transmission. Every pregnant woman is screened with hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg). Newborns of HBsAg-positive mothers, or mothers of unknown status, receive immune globulin and vaccine within 12 hours of birth regardless of maternal antiviral therapy. Women with a viral load above 200,000 IU/mL start tenofovir disoproxil fumarate in the third trimester. Mode of delivery and breastfeeding follow obstetric and infant criteria, not virologic ones.

## Recommendations and Grades
- Vaccinate every non-immune reproductive-age woman before conception or early in pregnancy — Grade [III] (WHO position paper; ACOG Practice Bulletin No. 86).
- Screen all pregnant women for HBsAg at the first prenatal or preconception visit — Grade [III].
- Report hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection to the local health department — reportable disease, no grade stated.
- Repeat HBsAg in the early third trimester for high-risk groups — may be considered, no grade stated.
- Measure HBV DNA by quantitative polymerase chain reaction in the late second or early third trimester in women with chronic infection — no grade stated.
- Start tenofovir disoproxil fumarate (TDF) in the third trimester when viral load exceeds 200,000 IU/mL or 10⁶–10⁸ copies/mL — Grade [I] (randomized trial of maternal tenofovir, ref 40); supporting cohort data Grade [II-2].
- Offer TDF in the third trimester when viral load exceeds 10⁷ IU/mL, per NICE — guideline-based.
- Give hepatitis B immune globulin (HBIg) and HBV vaccine within 12 hours of birth to every newborn of an HBsAg-positive mother or a mother of unknown or undocumented status, regardless of maternal antiviral therapy — Grade [III] (ACOG, SMFM).
- Base mode of delivery on obstetric indications; cesarean delivery does not reduce vertical transmission — Grade [III].
- Allow breastfeeding when the newborn receives immunoprophylaxis and the nipples are not cracked or bleeding — Grade [III].
- Do not use tenofovir alafenamide (TAF) in pregnancy — insufficient data.

## Key Concepts
- About 2 billion people worldwide have been infected; the World Health Organization estimated 257 million with chronic infection in 2015. About 90% of exposed adults clear the virus and 10% become chronic.
- Chronic infection risk is age-dependent: about 90% of newborns infected before 1 year, 30–50% of children infected at 1–5 years, and under 5% of healthy adults.
- The vaccine is about 95% effective. Endemicity is high above 8% population prevalence; China has over 80 million infected people. Low-prevalence regions such as North America and Western Europe are below 0.7%.
- United States acute cases fell from about 8,000 to 3,500 per year between 2000 and 2017; the rate is now 1.1 per 100,000, down from 9.6 per 100,000 in 1982. Neonatal vaccination at birth remains only 73.6%.
- Serology: acute infection is HBsAg positive, HBcAb positive, HBcIgM positive, HBsAb negative. Chronic infection is HBsAg positive for more than 6 months, HBcAb positive, HBcIgM negative, HBsAb negative.
- Vertical transmission without immunoprophylaxis: up to 95% with HBeAg positivity, about 10% with HBsAg positive and HBeAg negative. Acute infection in the first trimester transmits 10%, in the third trimester 80–90% (HBeAg negative 10–20%, HBeAg positive 90%). Chronic infection transmits 2–10% when HBeAg negative and 80–90% when HBeAg positive.
- About 90% of infected newborns develop chronic HBV; 25% of chronic carriers eventually die of cirrhosis or hepatocellular carcinoma.
- Five-year progression to cirrhosis is 8–20%; liver failure develops within 5 years in 20% of those with cirrhosis; hepatocellular carcinoma develops in 2–5% annually.
- Workup after a positive HBsAg: HBsAb, HBcAb, HBcIgM, HBeAg, HBeAb, and HBV DNA.
- TDF trial 1 (viral load above 200,000 IU/mL): 68% of treated women versus 2% of controls had viral load below 200,000 IU/mL at delivery; child infection 5% versus 18% (p = 0.007).
- TDF trial 2 (HBeAg positive): viral load above 200,000 IU/mL at delivery fell to 12% versus 90%; child infection 0 versus 2% (p = 0.12, not significant). Both trials gave vaccine and HBIg to all newborns.
- Lamivudine from 24–32 weeks to 4 weeks postpartum lowered intrauterine infection by 80% (odds ratio 0.2; 95% CI 0.10–0.39; p < 0.001) but carries resistance risk; TDF is preferred.
- A 2016 meta-analysis of 26 studies with over 3,600 women showed lower viral load at delivery (risk ratio 0.3; 95% CI 0.2–0.5) and lower vertical transmission (risk ratio 0.3; 95% CI 0.2–0.4) with third-trimester lamivudine or telbivudine.
- HBIg dosing: 0.06 mL/kg intramuscular for adults, 0.5 mL for neonates. It is safe in pregnancy and for the neonate.
- Vaccine dosing: Recombivax HB 10 micrograms (1 mL) three doses above age 20, 5 micrograms (0.5 mL) three doses under age 20, 10 micrograms (1.0 mL) two doses at 11–15 years. Engerix-B 20 micrograms (1 mL) three doses above age 20, 10 micrograms (0.5 mL) three doses under age 20. Heplisav 20 micrograms (0.5 mL) from age 18, with limited pregnancy safety data.
- Food and Drug Administration pregnancy categories: tenofovir and telbivudine category B; lamivudine, adefovir, entecavir, and interferons category C.
- The full immunoprophylaxis algorithm reduced HBV infection in United States children by 96% between 1990 and 2005.
- Acute HBV in pregnancy: supportive outpatient care; hospitalize for severe anemia, diabetes mellitus, severe dehydration, coagulopathy, significantly elevated bilirubin, or mental status change. Vitamin K 10 mg intramuscularly or orally every 8 hours for three doses treats coagulopathy. Mortality about 1%.
- Exposure in a non-immune pregnant woman: give HBIg and start the vaccine series, preferably within 24 hours.
- Hepatitis D virus superinfects 20–25% of chronic HBV patients; 25% may die of disease. It has no independent effect on pregnancy or the fetus.

## Anti-patterns
- Performing cesarean delivery to prevent vertical transmission — it has not been shown to reduce transmission.
- Withholding breastfeeding — not contraindicated with immunoprophylaxis and intact nipples.
- Delaying neonatal HBIg and vaccine beyond 12 hours — the benefit depends on timely birth dosing.
- Withholding neonatal immunoprophylaxis because the mother took antiviral therapy — it is still required.
- Using lamivudine as first-line pregnancy therapy — resistance is frequent; TDF is preferred.
- Using tenofovir alafenamide in pregnancy — data are insufficient.
- Treating HBeAg-negative infection as non-infectious — transmission still occurs in 2–10% of chronic cases.
- Omitting hepatitis A serology and vaccination in chronically infected women — co-infection adds morbidity.
- Restricting fetal scalp electrode or episiotomy solely for HBV — use obstetric indications; immunoprophylaxis mitigates risk.

## Key Takeaways
1. Screen every pregnant woman with HBsAg; if positive, order HBsAb, HBcAb, HBcIgM, HBeAg, HBeAb, and HBV DNA.
2. Measure viral load in the late second or early third trimester.
3. Start TDF in the third trimester if viral load exceeds 200,000 IU/mL ([II-2]); NICE threshold is 10⁷ IU/mL.
4. Give HBIg 0.06 mL/kg (neonate 0.5 mL) plus vaccine within 12 hours of birth to every exposed newborn.
5. Deliver by obstetric indications and support breastfeeding once immunoprophylaxis is given.
6. Vaccinate non-immune women and screen for hepatitis A before or during pregnancy.
