# Chapter 41: Trauma

## Core Idea
Trauma complicates a substantial fraction of pregnancies and is a leading cause of maternal death: about 27% of maternal deaths in the United States are injury related. There is no level I evidence to dictate initial care, the type or duration of monitoring, the testing required, or follow-up of the ongoing pregnancy; published guidance from ACOG, the Eastern Association for the Surgery of Trauma, and the Society of Obstetricians and Gynaecologists of Canada is level II–III. Maternal stabilization always takes precedence over fetal assessment, and indicated radiologic studies must not be withheld because of pregnancy. Placental abruption is the most common adverse pregnancy event after blunt trauma, is frequently delayed 2–6 hours and up to 24 hours, and correlates poorly with injury severity. Intimate partner violence is the leading cause of pregnancy-related trauma and must be asked about.

## Recommendations and Grades
- Follow ATLS: airway, breathing, circulation, disability, exposure — then the fetus. Maternal stabilization takes priority over fetal assessment. Source: ACOG, American College of Surgeons, SOGC, unanimous; evidence level II–III given the absence of randomized trials.
- Transfer to a trauma center for severe cases; the decision is usually made at the scene. CDC field triage (2011 update) adds pregnancy >20 weeks as a criterion for immediate transport to a center capable of definitive initial management. Air transport must not be precluded by pregnancy.
- Do not withhold radiologic or other diagnostic studies because of pregnancy. CT is recommended for the hemodynamically stable patient with neurologic injury, multiple non-abdominal injuries, or equivocal examination; a negative CT still warrants admission for observation.
- Left uterine displacement after mid-pregnancy (manual displacement preferred by the 2015 AHA guidelines) to relieve aortocaval compression.
- FAST ultrasound for blunt abdominal trauma: 80% sensitivity, 100% specificity for intra-abdominal injury in pregnancy. Diagnostic peritoneal lavage, if used, by open technique. Exploratory laparotomy for a positive DPL, most positive FAST scans, and suspected uterine rupture.
- Single preoperative dose of broad-spectrum antibiotic for penetrating abdominal wounds; gram-positive prophylaxis as soon as possible for open fractures; broad-spectrum prophylaxis for penetrating brain injury.
- Tetanus prophylaxis is unchanged by pregnancy; consider human tetanus immune globulin 250 units IM in all cases of traumatic wound.
- Rh-negative patients receive Rh immune globulin after trauma of any degree; ACOG recommends Kleihauer–Betke testing in all Rh-negative pregnant trauma patients to determine the RhIG dose.
- Fetal monitoring: ACOG suggests a minimum of 6 hours; EAST recommends at least 6 hours of continuous monitoring; SOGC recommends 4 hours if fetal heart tones and examination are normal, extended to 24 hours for uterine or abdominal tenderness, vaginal bleeding, contractions more often than every 10 minutes, nonreassuring fetal heart tones, fibrinogen <200 g/L, or other high-risk features.
- Consider admission ≥24 hours for uterine tenderness, continued abdominal pain, high-risk mechanism (auto versus pedestrian, high-speed crash), persistent contractions >4/hour, ruptured membranes, positive Kleihauer–Betke, bleeding, or abnormal fetal heart tracing.
- Screen for intimate partner violence at the initial prenatal visit, at least once per trimester, and postpartum (ACOG). Counsel gun safety.
- Perform perimortem cesarean delivery (resuscitative hysterotomy) when resuscitation after major trauma fails; do not move the patient or assemble extensive equipment — "the only equipment needed to start a PMCD is a scalpel."

## Key Concepts
- Prevalence per 100,000 pregnancies (Table 41.1): domestic violence 8307, blunt trauma (MVC, slips, falls) 260, penetrating 3.3, homicide 3.3, suicide 2.
- Mechanism distribution in United States data: MVC 74% of trauma, assault 12%, fall 9%, bicycle 2%, suicide <1%, other 3%. MVC involves a pregnant patient in 3–4% of all crashes.
- Maternal mortality from trauma 0.1–1.4%, a 10–100-fold increase over the background maternal mortality ratio; case fatality among hospitalized pregnant trauma patients 2–4%. Of injury deaths, MVC 44%, homicide 31%, unintentional 13%, suicide 10%. 77% of pregnancy-associated homicides occur during pregnancy.
- Risk factors for worse pregnancy outcome (Box 41.1): ISS >9, lactate >2 mmol/L, GCS <8, no seat belt, severe head injury, injury to thorax, abdomen, lower extremities, or spine. Fetal demise risk factors: penetrating injury, injury severity, maternal hypotension, need for laparotomy.
- In minor trauma (ISS = 0), Kleihauer–Betke, fibrinogen <200, contraction pattern, direct abdominal trauma, placental location, and abdominal pain are not reliable predictors of adverse outcome.
- Placental abruption occurs in 1–13% of traumas; third-trimester fetal mortality may reach 54%; onset averages 2–6 hours and up to 24 hours. Histology after 20 weeks shows pathology in 85% of placentas versus 40% of controls, retroplacental hemorrhage in 38%.
- Uterine rupture <1% of pregnant trauma patients, most likely with prior cesarean; maternal mortality 10%, fetal mortality 100%.
- Pelvic fracture occurs in <1% of pregnancy fractures (1–20 per pregnancy-related fractures) and is not a contraindication to vaginal delivery.
- Fetal death rate 0.4–1.5%; about 11% among patients hospitalized for trauma; about 5/1000 fetal deaths are attributable to trauma. Fetal death odds ratio 3.55 for any MVC, 2.49 for third-trimester crashes. Risk 93% with fatal maternal injury, 5% with major injury, 1% with minor injury.
- Correct seat belt use (three-point; shoulder over collar bone and between the breasts, lap low under the abdomen): adverse fetal outcome 29% correctly belted, 50% improperly restrained, 80% unrestrained. ISS 7 versus 13 in restrained versus unrestrained; emergency surgery 14% versus 10%. Stillbirth tripled in unbelted pregnant drivers. Crashes ≥30 mph: 85% had fetal death, direct fetal injury, uterine rupture, or preterm delivery.
- Airbag deployment showed no statistically significant difference in maternal or fetal outcomes, but abruption was 58% higher without airbags. Airbags save maternal lives and therefore fetal lives.
- Contraction data: at >20 weeks, >90% of trauma patients contract within the first 4 hours; 64% contract at least every 5 minutes in the first hour, falling to 29% by hour 4. Patients with no contractions or never more than every 10 minutes were discharged at 4 hours and none had abruption; total abruption rate 8%. Nearly 5% of fetal compromise or abruption becomes evident only after 6–48 hours.
- Kleihauer–Betke: positive (>0.01 mL fetal blood) in 20% of patients tested; 96% of positive patients had preterm contractions; no negative patient had contractions. One study reported a likelihood ratio >20 for preterm labor, suggesting monitoring could be limited to 1–2 hours if negative. The test is subjective and varies more than 10-fold on repeat testing; flow cytometry or anti-HbF monoclonal antibodies are more reliable.
- Fetal radiation doses: skull and chest <0.01 mGy, abdomen 1.4 mean/4.2 max, lumbar spine 1.7/10, pelvis 1.1/4, IVP 1.7/10. CT: head <0.005, chest 0.06/0.96, abdomen 8.0/49, lumbar spine 2.4/8.6, pelvis 25/79, pelvimetry 0.2/0.4. Teratogenic effects are not a concern below 5–10 mGy; thresholds for death and malformation rise from 200–500 mGy at 4–9 weeks to >1000 mGy after 23 weeks. Excess fatal childhood cancer about 1 per 33,000 per mGy; a single chest radiograph gives an excess risk of about 10 per million. Concern about cancer is not a reason to offer termination.
- Iodinated contrast is not teratogenic and does not suppress fetal thyroid function; do not withhold it when needed.
- Burns: aggressive fluid resuscitation; consider delivery if burn area >50%.
- Massive transfusion: 1:1:1 platelets:plasma:packed red cells is the most common protocol and improves outcomes.
- PMCD: best fetal outcomes with delivery within 4–5 minutes, higher fetal mortality beyond 10 minutes. Return of spontaneous circulation 93% without PMCD versus 54% with PMCD, but maternal survival odds 5-fold higher when PMCD occurs within 10 minutes; PMCD contributed to maternal survival in 32% and was never deleterious. Overall neonatal survival 64% even beyond 10 minutes. Survival after CPR in the emergency department after trauma is 17%, worse than age-matched non-pregnant controls.

## Anti-patterns
- Delaying maternal resuscitation to assess the fetus: fetal assessment belongs to the secondary survey.
- Avoiding CT or plain radiography because of "radiation fear": this leads to missed injury, and diagnostic doses are far below teratogenic thresholds.
- Treating a negative fetal ultrasound as exclusion of abruption: ultrasound detects abruption only when more than 50% of the placenta is involved, and abruption can develop days later.
- Reassuring on the absence of uterine tenderness: it does not exclude uterine or placental injury.
- Relying on injury severity to predict abruption: severity correlates poorly.
- Routine coagulation studies without hemorrhage: no evidence of benefit.
- Omitting Rh immune globulin because the trauma was minor: even minor trauma can cause enough fetomaternal hemorrhage to sensitize.
- Withholding air or ground transport, or any indicated study, because the patient is pregnant.
- Offering termination routinely after diagnostic radiation exposure.
- Tilted positioning instead of manual uterine displacement during CPR: manual displacement allows better compressions, defibrillation, and airway access.
- Extensive preparation before perimortem cesarean: it must start where the arrest occurs.

## Key Takeaways
1. Stabilize the mother first; the fetus is assessed only after the primary survey.
2. Never withhold indicated imaging; use the lowest-dose study that answers the question.
3. Monitor the viable fetus for at least 4–6 hours; extend to 24 hours with contractions, tenderness, bleeding, abnormal fetal heart tones, or fibrinogen <200 g/L.
4. Give Rh immune globulin to every Rh-negative trauma patient and check a Kleihauer–Betke to size the dose.
5. Counsel correct three-point seat belt use — it is the single most effective fetal protection.
6. Ask about intimate partner violence at every trimester and postpartum.
7. In maternal cardiac arrest after trauma, start perimortem cesarean within 4–5 minutes, at the scene, with a scalpel.
