# Chapter 29: Inherited Thrombophilia

## Core Idea
Inherited thrombophilias raise venous thromboembolism (VTE) risk, but their link to adverse pregnancy outcome is weak and inconsistent between retrospective and prospective studies. Universal screening is not recommended. Screening is justified when the result changes anticoagulation decisions: a prior personal VTE, or a VTE in the current pregnancy. Anticoagulation is chosen by thrombophilia type and VTE history, not by obstetric history. Thromboprophylaxis given solely to improve obstetric outcome is not advised.

## Recommendations and Grades
- Do not screen universally for inherited thrombophilias — no grade stated (expert guidance).
- Screen any pregnant woman with a prior personal VTE for factor V Leiden (FVL), prothrombin G20210A mutation (PGM), antithrombin III (ATIII), protein S (PS), and protein C (PC) — no grade stated.
- In VTE during the current pregnancy, screen for FVL, PGM, and ATIII only; PC and PS assessment is unreliable in pregnancy — no grade stated.
- Do not screen an otherwise healthy woman whose first-degree relative carries a thrombophilia or had VTE — insufficient evidence, no proven benefit.
- Do not screen for thrombophilia after an adverse pregnancy outcome in a woman without major VTE risk factors — insufficient evidence.
- Give prophylactic anticoagulation for prior VTE when PC, PS, heterozygous FVL, or PGM is found — no grade stated.
- Give full therapeutic anticoagulation for prior VTE with homozygous FVL or PGM, compound heterozygosity, or ATIII deficiency; prophylactic dosing may be adequate — no grade stated.
- Give prophylactic anticoagulation when a prior VTE had a recurring etiology such as estrogen-containing contraceptives or pregnancy — no grade stated.
- Omit routine antepartum prophylaxis when the prior VTE had a non-recurring cause and no thrombophilia is present; still give postpartum prophylaxis, especially after cesarean — no grade stated.
- Treat a VTE in the current pregnancy with full anticoagulation for 3–6 months, then prophylactic dosing through 6 weeks postpartum for low-risk thrombophilias — no grade stated.
- Do not give thromboprophylaxis to improve obstetric outcomes — the chapter concludes it does not appear efficacious; association studies are Grade [II-2].
- Replace folate, vitamin B6, or vitamin B12 when homocysteine is elevated — no grade stated (vitamin therapy does not prevent recurrent VTE).

## Key Concepts
- VTE complicates 0.2–2 per 1,000 births. Pregnancy raises risk fivefold; cesarean delivery raises it a further fivefold over vaginal delivery. Risk persists up to 12 weeks postpartum.
- Antepartum risk is divided equally across trimesters; pulmonary embolism is more common postpartum. Up to 50% of pregnancy-related thromboses occur in women with an inherited or acquired thrombophilia.
- Prevalence in general population versus patients with thrombosis: FVL heterozygous 1–15% versus 10–50%; PGM heterozygous 2–5% versus 6–18%; ATIII deficiency 0.02% versus 1–3%; PS deficiency 0.1–1.3% versus 1–5%; PC deficiency 0.2–0.4% versus 3–5%; hyperhomocysteinemia 5% versus 10%; MTHFR C677T heterozygous 5–14%.
- FVL carrier rate: 6.1% in Caucasians, 0.8% in African Americans, 1.7% in Hispanics, 1.9% in others. FVL is the most common cause of activated protein C resistance.
- Relative VTE risk and absolute risk per pregnancy: FVL heterozygous 5–7 and 0.5–3.1%; FVL homozygous 25 and 2.2–14%; PGM heterozygous 3–9 and 0.4–2.6%; PGM homozygous 25 and 2–4%; FVL/PGM compound heterozygote 84 and 4–8.2%; ATIII below 60% 50–100 and 0.2–11.6%; PC below 50% 10–13 and 0.1–1.7%; PS free antigen below 55% 2–10 and 0.3–6.6%; hyperhomocysteinemia above 16 micromol/L 3–6 and 0.2%.
- Share of all VTE: FVL heterozygous 40%, PGM heterozygous 17%, PC deficiency 14%, PS deficiency 3%, FVL homozygous 2%, ATIII deficiency 1%, compound heterozygote 1–3%.
- A 2017 Bayesian meta-analysis of 36 studies found absolute VTE risk above 3% only for ATIII, PC, or PS deficiency and FVL homozygosity; all such women also had a family history of VTE.
- Individual gestational VTE probability in women aged 35 or older with prior VTE: heterozygous FVL 0.7%, homozygous FVL 3.4%, heterozygous PGM 0.6%, FVL/PGM compound 8.2%, ATIII deficiency 9.0%, PC deficiency 1.1%, PS deficiency 1.0%.
- ATIII deficiency is the most thrombogenic: 70–90% lifetime risk, prevalence 1 in 1,000 to 1 in 5,000, present in only 1% of VTE patients. Diagnostic activity threshold <60%.
- PC deficiency is diagnosed by functional activity <50%, present in 0.3% of the population. PS is 40% free and functional; free PS is the measured fraction.
- Isolated MTHFR C677T homozygosity with normal homocysteine is not a thrombophilia. Homocysteine reaches a stable baseline once folate intake exceeds 400 micrograms per day.
- Reported associations with adverse outcome include placental abruption with PGM (OR 12.15, 95% CI 2.45–60.39) and stillbirth with FVL (OR 8.85, 95% CI 1.60–48.92), but prospective cohorts show no or minimal association.
- The only randomized evidence for obstetric benefit: LMWH 40 mg from 8 weeks plus folic acid versus aspirin 100 mg in women with one loss at or after 10 weeks and heterozygous FVL, PGM, or PS deficiency — healthy live birth 86% versus 29%, low birthweight 10% versus 30%. The trial was unblinded and the control outcomes were worse than expected.
- ACOG-based prophylaxis ladder: low-risk thrombophilia without prior VTE — surveillance antepartum; postpartum prophylaxis only with additional risk factors. Low-risk with family history — surveillance antepartum, postpartum prophylaxis. High-risk without prior VTE — prophylactic or intermediate-dose heparin antepartum and postpartum. Two or more VTE episodes — intermediate or adjusted-dose, with resumption of long-term anticoagulation.

## Anti-patterns
- Universal thrombophilia screening — prevalence is high, positive predictive value is low, and management rarely changes.
- Screening asymptomatic women who only have an affected relative — no proven benefit.
- Treating MTHFR genotype as a thrombophilia — isolated MTHFR with normal homocysteine does not raise VTE risk.
- Anticoagulating to prevent pre-eclampsia, growth restriction, or stillbirth — no consistent benefit and scant high-quality data.
- Ordering PC and PS levels during pregnancy or the acute thrombotic event — results are unreliable.
- Giving B vitamins to prevent recurrent VTE — the VITRO study showed homocysteine normalization without reduction in recurrent thrombosis.
- Assuming a negative thrombophilia screen excludes recurrence risk — VTE in pregnancy is multifactorial.

## Key Takeaways
1. Screen only when anticoagulation would change: prior personal VTE, or VTE in the current pregnancy.
2. Use the risk table: absolute risk exceeds 3% only with ATIII, PC, or PS deficiency or homozygous FVL.
3. For VTE in the current pregnancy, give full anticoagulation for 3–6 months, then prophylactic dosing through 6 weeks postpartum; high-risk types continue full doses and often lifelong therapy.
4. For a non-recurring prior VTE with no thrombophilia, skip antepartum prophylaxis but give postpartum prophylaxis, especially after cesarean.
5. Do not prescribe heparin to improve obstetric outcome.
6. Replace folate, B6, and B12 when homocysteine is elevated, but do not expect fewer thromboses.
