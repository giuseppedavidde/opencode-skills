# Chapter 45: Dermatoses of Pregnancy

## Core Idea
Only polymorphic eruption of pregnancy (PEP) is truly pregnancy-specific; the others are ordinary dermatoses grouped by their occurrence in pregnancy. The accepted classification has four categories — PEP, atopic eruption of pregnancy (AEP), pemphigoid gestationis (PG), and intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy (ICP) — with impetigo herpetiformis (IH) often treated as a fifth. Pruritus is the dominant symptom of all of them, and early differentiation is genuinely difficult, so the diagnosis is made methodically by exclusion and, where equivocal, by biopsy with direct immunofluorescence. AEP is the most common dermatosis of pregnancy, and PEP and AEP carry no fetal risk, whereas PG and IH are associated with prematurity, growth restriction, placental insufficiency, and fetal loss. Most evidence is case reports and case series; only striae gravidarum has randomized trials of treatment.

## Recommendations and Grades
- Use low- to mid-potency topical corticosteroids as first-line therapy for PEP and AEP. Source: 2012 Cochrane Review, which found a probable association between potent or very potent topical steroids and low birthweight, but no association for low- to mid-potency agents [meta-analysis].
- When potent or very potent topical steroids are required, use them for the shortest possible duration. The risk of potent topical steroids is still lower than that of systemic corticosteroids.
- Add liberal emollients, with or without antihistamines, for AEP. First-generation antihistamines diphenhydramine and chlorpheniramine are pregnancy class B and are first-line.
- Consider a tapered course of systemic corticosteroids plus UVB phototherapy for severe AEP; consider cyclosporine for recalcitrant cases. Avoid methotrexate and mycophenolate mofetil.
- For PG, biopsy lesional and perilesional skin for histology and direct immunofluorescence; ELISA for BP180 antibodies may confirm or replace immunofluorescence.
- Treat PG with topical steroids in mild cases and systemic steroids in most: prednisolone 20–40 mg daily or 1–2 mg/kg/day. For recalcitrant disease consider rituximab, intravenous immunoglobulin, or plasmapheresis.
- Treat IH with prednisone 15–30 mg daily, with doses up to 60–80 mg/day if needed; taper very slowly because rebound is common.
- Add cyclosporine A 3–10 mg/kg/day when IH is uncontrolled by steroids; monitor renal function and blood pressure, and avoid breastfeeding during therapy.
- Consider TNF-α inhibitors such as infliximab — pregnancy category B — for IH, even as first-line therapy where appropriate.
- Monitor patients with IH closely with fetal testing because of the risk of placental insufficiency, and check serum calcium, vitamin D, and parathyroid hormone.
- Do not use topical tretinoin during pregnancy (category C) or breastfeeding; it is effective only for early striae rubra.
- Counsel that PEP rarely recurs in later pregnancies, whereas AEP recurs because of the atopic background and PG and IH recur, often earlier and more severely.

## Key Concepts
- Striae gravidarum: prevalence 50–90%, mean onset 25 weeks. Strongest associations are pre-existing breast or thigh striae, a mother or other family member with striae, and non-white race. A study of 800 primiparas found higher pre-pregnancy weight and BMI, weight at delivery, and gestational weight gain significantly associated with postpartum striae (p < 0.001). Vitamin D >30 ng/mL was associated with less striae than <20 ng/mL.
- Striae prevention: a 2012 Cochrane Review found no high-quality evidence for any topical preparation. Cocoa butter failed in two RCTs; olive oil failed in three RCTs; aloe vera and sweet almond oil reduced itching and erythema but not striae number or diameter.
- Striae treatment: topical tretinoin 0.1% improves early striae rubra but has no benefit on white atrophic striae; 585-nm pulsed dye laser improves appearance, and XeCl excimer UVB, fractional CO2, non-ablative fractionated laser, and tretinoin plus fractional radiofrequency also show benefit. Newer, more erythematous striae respond better.
- PEP: about 0.5% of pregnancies, mostly primigravidas, third trimester. Urticarial papules and plaques beginning on the abdomen within striae, with periumbilical sparing; spread to thighs and buttocks, occasionally arms; face, palms, and soles spared. Severe pruritus with a notable lack of excoriation. No consistent maternal or fetal complications, and newborns are unaffected. No HLA association and no autoimmune mechanism. Direct immunofluorescence is negative or non-diagnostic — this is the key discriminator from PG.
- AEP: the most common dermatosis of pregnancy; onset before the third trimester in 75%. Lichenified, excoriated, hyperpigmented patches and plaques on flexural surfaces in 80%, prurigo-like papules or nodules in 20%. IgE elevated in 20–70% but not diagnostic. Total serum bile acids must be normal. Recurrence in subsequent pregnancies is expected.
- PG: about 1 in 50,000 births. Autoantibodies against collagen XVII (BP180) in the basement membrane zone; complement activation produces sub-epidermal blisters with numerous eosinophils. HLA-DR3 or HLA-DR4 in most patients. Onset at a mean of 21–28 weeks, often with periumbilical and umbilical involvement (unlike PEP), spreading to palms and soles; face and mucosae spared. Linear C3 deposition in all cases and linear IgG in about half. ELISA for circulating autoantibodies is positive in over 92%. Associated with prematurity in about 20% and small for gestational age. Passive antibody transfer causes transient neonatal skin lesions in 5–10% with no long-term sequelae. Tends to flare at delivery and recur in later pregnancies, with menses, and with oral contraceptives.
- IH: about 100 reported cases; third trimester, sometimes first trimester; most patients have no prior psoriasis. Symmetric erythematous patches with peripheral superficial sterile pustules, beginning in intertriginous and flexural skin. Spongiform pustule of Kogoj on histology, indistinguishable from pustular psoriasis. Associated with HLA-B17 and Cw6. Complications are placental insufficiency, fetal death, hypocalcemia, low vitamin D, and hypoparathyroidism, which can cause tetany or seizures. There is no infectious etiology despite the name.
- Systemic corticosteroids in the first trimester are associated with an increased risk of orofacial clefts in a recent meta-analysis; associations with low birthweight and preterm birth are confounded by maternal disease severity.
- Melanoma is covered in Chapter 44; the dermatology chapter emphasizes that changing or darkening nevi in pregnancy must be biopsied, because the belief that nevi normally darken can delay diagnosis. Nevi on the breast and abdomen enlarge from skin stretching; nevi elsewhere generally do not.
- ICP is discussed in Chapter 10 of the Obstetric volume; here it is only classified as a dermatosis of pregnancy without primary skin lesions.

## Anti-patterns
- Treating presumed PEP without excluding PG: PG can present with urticarial lesions before blisters, is widespread, has no striae predilection, and does not spare the periumbilical area.
- Using potent or very potent topical steroids as first-line therapy: associated with low birthweight.
- Using topical tretinoin during pregnancy or breastfeeding.
- Expecting tretinoin to improve white atrophic striae: no benefit.
- Prescribing methotrexate or mycophenolate mofetil for recalcitrant AEP.
- Tapering IH steroids rapidly: rebound is common.
- Assuming impetigo herpetiformis is infectious and treating with antibiotics: the pustules are sterile.
- Reassuring a patient that a darkening nevus is a normal pregnancy change.
- Missing hypocalcemia in IH.

## Key Takeaways
1. Pruritus in pregnancy with abdominal striae and periumbilical sparing suggests PEP; low- to mid-potency topical steroids are first-line and fetal risk is nil.
2. Periumbilical and umbilical involvement, blisters, and a positive ELISA or linear C3 on immunofluorescence indicate PG; treat with systemic steroids and expect prematurity in 20%.
3. AEP is the most common dermatosis; treat with topical steroids, emollients, and first-generation antihistamines, and expect recurrence.
4. IH carries the highest fetal risk — placental insufficiency and fetal death — and needs systemic steroids plus fetal surveillance.
5. Do not promise prevention of stretch marks: no topical agent has proven efficacy.
6. Biopsy any changing nevus; do not attribute it to pregnancy.
