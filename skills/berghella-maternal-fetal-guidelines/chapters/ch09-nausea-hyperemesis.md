# Chapter 9: Nausea/Vomiting of Pregnancy and Hyperemesis Gravidarum

## Core Idea
Nausea affects 50–80% of pregnancies and vomiting about 50%, but hyperemesis gravidarum (HG) affects only 0.3–1%. HG is defined as intractable nausea and vomiting ≥3 times a day with dehydration (large ketonuria, high urine specific gravity, or electrolyte imbalance) and weight loss >3 kg or >5% of prepregnancy weight, after other diagnoses are excluded. Symptoms begin at 4–6 weeks, peak at 8–12 weeks, and resolve by 20 weeks in 91%; onset after 9 weeks should prompt a search for another cause. Treatment is stepwise and additive, starting with prevention and non-pharmacologic measures, then vitamin B6 with doxylamine, then dopamine antagonists or ondansetron, then intravenous rehydration, then enteral and finally parenteral nutrition. Early treatment prevents progression, and nausea and vomiting are commonly undertreated.

## Recommendations and Grades
- Start prenatal multivitamins 3 months before conception to reduce the incidence of nausea and vomiting; for women with prior HG, give prophylactic vitamin B6 and doxylamine.
- Do not measure TSH in women with nausea and vomiting or HG unless they have a history or symptoms of hyperthyroidism. *(Guideline)*
- Treat early and stepwise; for HG, start at step 3 while still applying steps 1 and 2.
- Use vitamin B6 10–25 mg every 8 hours (maximum 100 mg/day) and doxylamine 25 mg at bedtime plus 12.5 mg twice daily as needed as first-line pharmacotherapy.
- Add metoclopramide 10–20 mg every 6–8 hours and/or ondansetron 4–8 mg three or four times daily, and/or promethazine 12.5–25 mg every 4–6 hours when symptoms persist or dehydration or weight loss appears.
- Admit for intravenous rehydration with D5 or normal saline when the woman cannot tolerate oral intake; add thiamine 100 mg daily for 2–3 days before dextrose, then multivitamins.
- Give glucocorticoids only after 10 weeks' gestation for refractory HG; use an initial 3-day course and taper over 1–2 weeks, for a maximum of 6 weeks. *(FDA category C; cleft lip risk before 10 weeks)*
- Prefer enteral nutrition over parenteral nutrition when weight loss or dehydration persists beyond 5–7 days despite aggressive inpatient therapy.
- Offer psychological care and consider psychiatry consultation for refractory cases; mindfulness-based cognitive therapy added to pyridoxine improved symptoms, anxiety, depression, and pregnancy distress in one RCT.
- Escalate to gastroenterology and nutrition consultation for persistent weight loss or dehydration over 5–7 days.

## Key Concepts
- Mild symptoms: intermittent nausea, odor and food aversion, retching, vomiting. About 10% of women with nausea and vomiting need medication.
- HG is the most common indication for hospital admission in the first trimester and second only to preterm labor across pregnancy.
- Recurrence in a subsequent pregnancy is about 67%; daughters of affected mothers have a threefold risk.
- Risk factors: young age, nulliparity, prior HG, prior molar pregnancy, obesity, African-American race, female fetus, motion sickness, migraine, psychiatric illness, preexisting hyperthyroidism, diabetes, gastrointestinal disease, asthma, multiple gestation, and high human chorionic gonadotropin states.
- Helicobacter pylori IgG is present in 90.5% of HG patients versus 47.5% of controls; no RCTs of eradication exist.
- Differential diagnosis includes gastroparesis, gastroenteritis, biliary disease, hepatitis, obstruction, peptic ulcer, pancreatitis, appendicitis, inflammatory bowel disease, urinary tract infection, pyelonephritis, renal stones, and metabolic or neurologic causes.
- Laboratory workup: electrolytes, BUN, creatinine, glucose, liver function tests, amylase, lipase, acetone, urine ketones and specific gravity. Quantitative human chorionic gonadotropin is not useful; ultrasound excludes molar pregnancy and multiple gestation.
- Transient biochemical hyperthyroidism occurs in 60–70% of HG and resolves spontaneously in 1–10 weeks.
- Ginger 125–250 mg three or four times daily; acupressure; small meals with increased protein and avoidance of noxious stimuli.
- Other antihistamines: diphenhydramine 25–50 mg every 4–6 hours (maximum 400 mg/day); meclizine 25–50 mg every 6 hours (maximum 100 mg/24 h); hydroxyzine 25–100 mg every 6–8 hours (maximum 600 mg/24 h); dimenhydrinate 50–100 mg every 4–6 hours.
- Prochlorperazine 5–10 mg every 4–6 hours (maximum 40 mg/day); droperidol carries a black-box warning for torsades de pointes.
- Metoclopramide causes tardive dyskinesia with use beyond 12 weeks or high cumulative dose.
- Reflux: add an H2 blocker (famotidine 20–40 mg twice daily) or a proton pump inhibitor (omeprazole 20–40 mg daily, maximum 80 mg/day).
- Nutrition: Harris-Benedict equation with an activity factor of 1.2–1.9 plus 300 kcal for pregnancy; start tube feeding at 25 mL/hour and increase by 25 mL/hour to goal, then consolidate over 8–12 hours overnight.
- Parenteral nutrition: about 25% catheter infection, plus thrombosis, occlusion, pneumothorax, cholestasis, preterm birth, and fetal death.
- Maternal complications: Wernicke's encephalopathy (vitamin B1 deficiency), peripheral neuropathy (B6 and B12), central pontine myelinolysis, splenic avulsion, esophageal rupture, pneumothorax, acute tubular necrosis, and rarely death; 2.9% of Swedish HG cases ended in termination.
- Fetal complications: HG is associated with growth restriction, low birth weight, SGA, gestational hypertension, and preterm delivery, but not congenital malformation; nausea and vomiting are associated with lower pregnancy loss.

## Anti-patterns
- Testing TSH routinely in HG: the biochemical hyperthyroidism is transient and self-limited.
- Giving dextrose before thiamine in prolonged vomiting: precipitates Wernicke's encephalopathy.
- Corticosteroids before 10 weeks: increased risk of cleft lip.
- Benzodiazepines and clonidine: adverse fetal effects or insufficient safety data.
- Starting with parenteral nutrition: try enteral feeding first; PN has high catheter morbidity.
- Prolonged metoclopramide beyond 12 weeks: tardive dyskinesia risk.
- Rapid correction of hyponatremia with hypertonic solutions: central pontine myelinolysis.
- Enforcing 12-hour fasting after admission: immediate oral feeding gives similar nausea, vomiting, and satisfaction.
- Assuming HG protects against growth restriction: severe HG is associated with FGR and low birth weight.
- Dismissing psychological morbidity: anxiety and depression increase and may persist postnatally.

## Key Takeaways
1. Diagnose HG only after excluding other causes, using ketonuria, weight loss >5%, and dehydration criteria.
2. Prevent with preconception multivitamins and, after prior HG, prophylactic B6 plus doxylamine.
3. Escalate additively through B6/doxylamine, then metoclopramide or ondansetron or promethazine, then IV fluids with thiamine.
4. Use steroids only after 10 weeks, and prefer enteral over parenteral nutrition.
5. Screen for and treat psychosocial morbidity, and arrange growth surveillance if symptoms persist into the second half of pregnancy.
