# Chapter 33: Hepatitis C

## Core Idea
Chronic hepatitis C virus (HCV) infection is defined by a reactive antibody with detectable HCV RNA for more than 6 months. Universal screening is recommended for every pregnant woman at the start of each pregnancy. Pregnancy does not change the course of HCV, but HCV worsens obstetric outcome, mainly through preterm delivery, growth restriction, and intrahepatic cholestasis. Direct-acting antivirals cure most cases but are not yet approved in pregnancy, so treatment is planned before conception or after delivery. Cesarean delivery is reserved for obstetric indications in HCV-monoinfected women.

## Recommendations and Grades
- Screen all pregnant women for HCV at the start of each pregnancy — Grade [Guideline] (CDC sexually transmitted infection treatment guidelines); ACOG review-level guidance Grade [III].
- Counsel all women about HCV risk factors (Table 33.1) — Grade [III].
- In women of reproductive age with chronic HCV, strongly consider direct-acting antiviral treatment before conception, particularly with advanced fibrosis, compensated cirrhosis, severe extrahepatic complications, or a prior child infected by mother-to-child transmission — no grade stated.
- Do not treat HCV in pregnancy; refer for postpartum treatment once viral load is detectable — no grade stated.
- Reserve cesarean delivery for obstetric indications in HCV-positive, HIV-negative women — Grade [III].
- Avoid prolonged rupture of membranes and fetal scalp electrodes when possible — Grade [III].
- Encourage breastfeeding in HCV-positive, HIV-negative women when nipples are not traumatized, cracked, or bleeding — Grade [III].
- Vaccinate non-immune women against hepatitis A and B during pregnancy — Grade [III].
- Do not breastfeed with HCV/HIV co-infection — Grade [III].
- Screen for intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy when pruritus appears — serum bile acids and transaminases.

## Key Concepts
- Worldwide prevalence of chronic HCV is about 71 million. In the United States, 2.4 million people (1%) have chronic infection, 4.1 million (1.7%) are antibody positive, and there were about 50,300 new infections in 2018. HCV caused or contributed to over 15,000 deaths in 2018.
- Prevalence among reproductive-age women is 1–1.6%. About 52% of people who inject drugs are infected. The largest rise in new infections is in the 20–39-year age group.
- HCV RNA appears 1–2 weeks after exposure; anti-HCV antibody appears by 12 weeks. Acute infection is positive HCV RNA with negative antibody. Chronic infection is detectable RNA for more than 6 months.
- Mother-to-child transmission: 5.8% when the mother is HCV-RNA positive and HIV negative; about 11% with HIV co-infection. Table rates: antibody positive but RNA negative 0–3%; viremic 4–6%; HIV positive 19–25%; HIV negative 3–6%; anti-HCV with injection drug use 7–9%; anti-HCV without injection drug use 3–7%.
- Infant testing: anti-HCV antibody at or after 18 months; HCV RNA polymerase chain reaction at 2 months if needed; HCV RNA after age 3 years to diagnose chronic infection. Only 42% of children in one cohort were tested, and only 21% of those at 1–2 years.
- About 75% of infected adults are asymptomatic or have mild nonspecific symptoms; fatigue is the most common complaint.
- Natural history: spontaneous clearance 20–50% (recent data up to 68.7%), mostly within 6 months; chronic infection 50–80%; cirrhosis in 5–25% of chronically infected; hepatocellular carcinoma 1–4% per year once cirrhosis or bridging fibrosis is present; decompensation in 4–8%.
- Risk factors with odds ratios: injection drug use 49.6, blood transfusion 10.9, sex with a partner who injects drugs 6.3, incarceration over 3 days 2.9, religious scarification 2.8, cut with a bloody object 2.1, body piercings 2.0, immunoglobulin injection 1.6.
- HCV in pregnancy: aminotransferases improve during pregnancy and rise postpartum; HCV RNA rises through pregnancy and returns to baseline after delivery. Spontaneous clearance after delivery is reported in up to 25%, so measure viral load postpartum before starting therapy.
- Obstetric risks: preterm delivery, fetal growth restriction, small for gestational age, neonatal assisted ventilation, and neonatal intensive care admission. Cirrhosis and portal hypertension add pre-eclampsia, cesarean delivery, hemorrhage, low birthweight, and maternal and neonatal death.
- Intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy is strongly associated with HCV (odds ratio 20.40; 95% CI 9.39–44.33; I² = 55%).
- Preferred antiviral regimens for any genotype in treatment-naive patients without cirrhosis: 8 weeks of glecaprevir/pibrentasvir, or 12 weeks of sofosbuvir/velpatasvir. Side effects are few and mild (headache, nausea, fatigue, insomnia).
- Ribavirin is contraindicated in pregnancy because of teratogenicity.
- Workup: quantitative HCV RNA and genotype; HIV, hepatitis A total antibody, HBsAg and HBsAb, gonorrhea, chlamydia, syphilis; AST, ALT, bilirubin, albumin, platelet count, prothrombin time/INR; liver ultrasound in pregnancy or elastography before conception or postpartum.
- Cirrhosis warrants pneumococcal vaccination. Avoid alcohol, hepatotoxic medicines such as acetaminophen and some herbal remedies, and obesity-related fibrosis progression.
- Invasive prenatal diagnosis: amniocentesis is preferred over chorionic villus sampling when testing is desired.
- Cesarean delivery may be considered for HCV/HIV co-infection — one retrospective study found 60% lower transmission, but this is an exception to the general rule.

## Anti-patterns
- Risk-based screening only — it misses cases; universal screening is recommended.
- Treating HCV during pregnancy with interferon, ribavirin, or direct-acting antivirals outside a trial — ribavirin is teratogenic and direct-acting antivirals are not yet approved in pregnancy.
- Elective cesarean to prevent HCV transmission in HIV-negative women — no proven benefit.
- Withholding breastfeeding in HCV-monoinfected women — not a transmission risk.
- Testing the infant for antibody before 18 months — maternal antibody persists.
- Assuming normal aminotransferases exclude infection — most patients are asymptomatic.
- Missing cholestasis — pruritus in an HCV-positive pregnancy needs bile acid testing.
- Forgetting to test the child — most exposed children are never tested.

## Key Takeaways
1. Screen every pregnancy for HCV with antibody and reflex RNA.
2. Counsel and plan direct-acting antiviral cure before conception or in the postpartum interval.
3. Expect preterm delivery, growth restriction, and cholestasis; check bile acids if pruritus occurs.
4. Deliver vaginally unless obstetric indications exist; avoid prolonged membrane rupture and fetal scalp electrodes.
5. Support breastfeeding in HCV-monoinfected women; avoid it with HIV co-infection.
6. Arrange pediatric testing at 18 months and hepatology follow-up for the mother.
