# Chapter 35: Gonorrhea

## Core Idea
Gonorrhea in pregnancy is usually asymptomatic and is diagnosed by culture or nucleic acid amplification testing. Screening targets high-risk women only, because there is no evidence that screening low-risk pregnant women helps. First-line treatment is dual therapy with ceftriaxone 250 mg intramuscularly plus azithromycin 1 g orally as single doses; single-agent therapy is no longer adequate because of resistance. Vertical transmission occurs in 30–47% of women with cervical infection at delivery and causes neonatal conjunctivitis, which can blind.

## Recommendations and Grades
- Screen pregnant women at high risk for gonorrhea: age under 25, prior sexually transmitted infection, multiple partners, partner with a sexually transmitted infection history, sex work, drug use, or inconsistent condom use — Grade: epidemiologic data, no numeric grade stated (CDC surveillance).
- Do not screen low-risk pregnant women — no evidence of benefit.
- Diagnose by culture or nucleic acid amplification test (NAAT) — Grade [Review] (CDC laboratory recommendations).
- Treat with ceftriaxone 250 mg intramuscularly as a single dose plus azithromycin 1 g orally as a single dose — Grade [Review] (CDC treatment guidelines; 99.1% efficacy for cervix, urethra, and rectum; 99% for pharynx).
- Use spectinomycin 2 g intramuscularly as a single dose only for cephalosporin allergy, after infectious disease consultation — 98.2% efficacy for urogenital and anorectal infection, but only 50% for pharyngeal infection.
- Test for Chlamydia trachomatis, syphilis, HIV, and hepatitis B — coinfection is common, and 35–50% of women with endocervical gonorrhea have chlamydia.
- Manage preterm premature rupture of membranes expectantly when active gonorrhea is promptly treated — no grade stated.
- Do not perform routine test of cure after the recommended regimen; test at 2 weeks only if an alternate regimen was used.
- Report suspected treatment failure to the health department and obtain culture for susceptibility testing — no grade stated.

## Key Concepts
- The World Health Organization estimated 86.9 million new cases worldwide in 2016, with incidence about 26 per 1,000 in men and 20 per 1,000 in women aged 15–49.
- United States 2018: 583,405 reported cases, an 82.6% increase since 2009 and a 5% increase from 2017; about 58,000 occurred in pregnant women. Fewer than half of infections are reported, and the true annual burden exceeds 1 million.
- Prevalence in pregnancy in low- and middle-income countries: 4.6% in southern Africa, 2.3% in east Africa, 2.8% in Asia, 0.3% in Latin America.
- Transmission from male to female during vaginal intercourse is about 50% per contact, rising to 90% after three exposures. Incubation averages 2–7 days (range 1–14).
- Vertical transmission occurs in 30–47% of cases when cervical infection is present at delivery. The eye is the most common neonatal site; disseminated gonococcal infection and gonococcal arthritis also occur. Transmission is mostly during vaginal delivery but has been reported after cesarean with ruptured membranes.
- Asymptomatic cervical infection occurs in up to 80% of women. Urethral infection accompanies 70–90% of cervical cases, mostly with dysuria. Pharyngeal infection occurs in 10–20% and rectal infection in about 40% of women with cervical gonorrhea.
- Disseminated gonococcal infection occurs in 0.5–3% overall and in 0.04–0.09% of pregnancies; it causes septic arthritis with hemorrhagic papules and pustules.
- Pelvic inflammatory disease and perihepatitis are uncommon after the first trimester.
- Complications include spontaneous abortion, preterm birth, early rupture of membranes, chorioamnionitis, and perinatal mortality; causality is uncertain because infection may be a marker of other risk. Active cervical infection at delivery raises postpartum infection risk.
- Before routine neonatal ocular prophylaxis, about 25% of congenital blindness was caused by gonococcal conjunctivitis.
- Gonococcal infection facilitates HIV transmission.
- NAAT performance: 96.7% sensitivity and 98% specificity compared with culture. Culture: 80–90% sensitivity and 100% specificity. Gram stain: 40–60% sensitivity and 70–90% specificity, with a 99–100% negative predictive value. Non-NAAT DNA probe testing: 92.1% sensitivity and 99% specificity but not recommended.
- NAAT positive predictive value falls below 60% when prevalence is under 1%, because a single organism can cause a positive result.
- Preferred specimen for NAAT is a self-collected or clinician-collected vaginal swab; first-catch urine or endocervical swab is acceptable. Conjunctival specimens should be cultured. NAAT is the recommended method for rectal and oropharyngeal specimens when the laboratory is CLIA compliant.
- Treatment efficacy: ceftriaxone 250 mg plus azithromycin 1 g is 99.1% effective for cervix, urethra, and rectum, and 99% for pharynx. Spectinomycin 2 g is 98.2% for urogenital and anorectal infection but only 50% for pharynx. Gonococcal conjunctivitis is treated with ceftriaxone 1 g intramuscularly single dose plus azithromycin 1 g orally.
- Expedited partner therapy: cefixime 400 mg orally single dose plus azithromycin 1 g, appropriate only when the partner cannot present for the first-line intramuscular regimen. Verify state law first.
- Advise abstinence for 7 days after treatment and until all partners are treated and have completed 7 days of abstinence.
- Allergy: first-generation cephalosporin reactions occur in under 2.5% of people with penicillin allergy. Ceftriaxone and cefixime are contraindicated with IgE-mediated penicillin allergy (anaphylaxis, Stevens-Johnson syndrome, toxic epidermal necrolysis).
- Resistance: the Gonococcal Isolate Surveillance Project and Euro-GASP document resistance to penicillin, tetracyclines, and fluoroquinolones; cefixime minimum inhibitory concentrations rose from 2006 to 2012, and three ceftriaxone-resistant isolates were reported in the European Union in 2018.
- Test of cure is unnecessary after the recommended regimen. With an alternate regimen, test at 2 weeks by NAAT or culture; a positive NAAT requires culture for susceptibility before retreatment. Pharyngeal infection treated with an alternate regimen requires test of cure at 14 days.

## Anti-patterns
- Single-agent therapy — no longer adequate because of resistance; dual therapy is required.
- Non-NAAT DNA probe testing — significantly lower sensitivity; not recommended.
- Screening low-risk pregnant women — no evidence of benefit and a high false-positive rate when prevalence is low.
- Routine test of cure after first-line therapy — unnecessary.
- Retreatment based on a positive NAAT alone after suspected failure — culture is required so susceptibility can be tested.
- Using oral cefixime as first-line therapy or as the default for partner treatment — reserve for expedited partner therapy when the partner cannot attend.
- Giving ceftriaxone with a history of IgE-mediated penicillin allergy.
- Omitting chlamydia coverage — 35–50% of women with endocervical gonorrhea are coinfected.
- Treating gonorrhea without testing for HIV, syphilis, and hepatitis B.

## Key Takeaways
1. Screen only high-risk pregnant women; ask about age under 25, prior STI, multiple partners, sex work, drug use, and condom use.
2. Diagnose with NAAT on a vaginal swab, or culture where susceptibility matters.
3. Treat with ceftriaxone 250 mg intramuscularly plus azithromycin 1 g orally, both as single doses.
4. Treat partners, offer expedited partner therapy only if they cannot attend, and advise 7 days of abstinence.
5. Do not perform routine test of cure; culture any suspected failure and report it to the health department.
6. Expect neonatal conjunctivitis risk with untreated cervical infection at delivery and arrange neonatal evaluation and prophylaxis per local protocol.
