# Chapter 31: Hepatitis A

## Core Idea
Hepatitis A virus (HAV) infection is almost always self-limited, is not transmitted perinatally in any meaningful way, and has no antiviral therapy. Management in pregnancy is supportive, with hospitalization reserved for severe dehydration, encephalopathy, or coagulopathy. Prevention is the whole intervention: inactivated vaccine for at-risk women, and vaccine or immune globulin within 2 weeks of exposure. Obstetric outcome is worse with acute infection, with reported preterm labor, premature rupture of membranes, and placental abruption.

## Recommendations and Grades
- Vaccinate against HAV in pregnancy when the woman is at risk of HAV exposure or at risk of severe illness — Grade [III] (ACIP recommendations, 2020).
- Give post-exposure prophylaxis to unvaccinated pregnant women within 2 weeks of close personal or sexual contact — Grade [III].
- Use the first dose of HAV vaccine for post-exposure prophylaxis, with the second dose 6 months later for long-term immunity — Grade [III].
- Consider immune globulin (GamaSTAN 0.1 mL/kg intramuscular, single dose) during pregnancy; it is safe — Grade [III]; efficacy Grade [III].
- Do not use the combined hepatitis A and B vaccine (Twinrix) for post-exposure prophylaxis — Grade [III] (ACIP).
- Offer supportive outpatient therapy for acute infection; hospitalize only for severe dehydration, encephalopathy, or coagulopathy — Grade [III] (ACOG Practice Bulletin No. 86).
- Do not order antepartum fetal testing for HAV — not indicated.
- Allow breastfeeding with good hand hygiene — not contraindicated.

## Key Concepts
- Case definition (Council of State and Territorial Epidemiologists, 2019): acute viral hepatitis symptoms plus jaundice or bilirubin ≥3.0 mg/dL, or alanine aminotransferase (ALT) >200 IU/L, when no more likely diagnosis exists. Laboratory confirmation is anti-HAV immunoglobulin M (IgM) positive or a nucleic acid amplification test positive for HAV RNA.
- Confirmed acute HAV requires the clinical criteria plus anti-HAV IgM positivity, or contact with a laboratory-confirmed case in the preceding 15–50 days, or HAV RNA detection.
- Incubation averages 28 days (range 15–50). Peak infectivity is 2 weeks before the abrupt onset of symptoms.
- Anti-HAV IgM appears 5–10 days before symptoms and persists 3–12 months.
- Illness is self-limited over 1–2 weeks; relapse occurs in up to 20%, with an average duration of 4.7 weeks and reports up to 4 months.
- Children under 6 years are usually asymptomatic and are a major source of infection. Having a child under 3 years in the home raises transmission risk (odds ratio 8.8; 95% confidence limits 2.1–36; P = .002).
- United States burden: about 24,900 cases in 2018. Incidence fell more than 95% from 11.7 per 100,000 in 1996 to 0.4 per 100,000 in 2011, then rose to just under 4 per 100,000 by 2018, driven by person-to-person outbreaks among people experiencing homelessness or reporting drug use.
- Hospitalization occurs in 11–22%. Overall mortality is 0.3%, rising to at least 1.8% above age 50 and with chronic liver disease. There is no chronic carrier state.
- Rare complications: liver failure, nephrotic syndrome, glomerulonephritis, vasculitis, aplastic anemia.
- Pregnancy associations: preterm labor, premature rupture of membranes, placental abruption. Perinatal transmission is not expected and has been reported only rarely.
- Risk groups: poor hygiene and sanitation, homelessness, men who have sex with men, drug use, unvaccinated travel to high- or intermediate-endemicity countries, occupational exposure to non-human primates, group living or incarceration, chronic liver disease, HIV, age over 40 years.
- Vaccine schedules above age 18: Havrix 1 mL (1,440 ELISA units of inactivated HAV) intramuscular at 0 and 6–12 months; Vaqta 1 mL (50 units HAV) intramuscular at 0 and 6–18 months; Twinrix 1 mL (720 ELISA units inactivated HAV plus 20 micrograms hepatitis B surface antigen) at 0, 1, and 6 months, or accelerated at 0, 7 days, and 21–30 days with a booster at 12 months.
- Immune globulin prevents HAV infection in 80–90% of cases when given within 2 weeks of exposure; the chapter's key points state greater than 85% efficacy.
- HAV in food is inactivated by heating above 185 degrees Fahrenheit for 1 minute.

## Anti-patterns
- Using Twinrix for post-exposure prophylaxis — explicitly not recommended.
- Giving post-exposure prophylaxis later than 2 weeks after exposure — efficacy depends on that window.
- Searching for antiviral therapy — none exists.
- Ordering antepartum fetal surveillance for isolated HAV — not indicated.
- Assuming a chronic carrier state or long-term infectivity — no chronic carrier state exists.
- Withholding breastfeeding — not contraindicated; maintain hygiene.
- Relying on low pregnancy incidence to skip testing when viral hepatitis symptoms appear — test for HAV, HBV, and HCV together.

## Key Takeaways
1. Test for HAV with anti-HAV IgM or HAV RNA when acute hepatitis symptoms, bilirubin ≥3.0 mg/dL, or ALT >200 IU/L appear.
2. Treat supportively; hospitalize only for severe dehydration, encephalopathy, or coagulopathy.
3. Vaccinate at-risk pregnant women; use the two-dose inactivated vaccine.
4. Give post-exposure prophylaxis within 2 weeks — vaccine first dose, or immune globulin 0.1 mL/kg intramuscular.
5. Counsel hand washing, food hygiene, and heating food above 185 degrees Fahrenheit for 1 minute.
6. Expect obstetric risk of preterm labor, premature rupture of membranes, and abruption, and manage by obstetric indications.
