# Chapter 28: Antiphospholipid Syndrome

## Core Idea
Antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) requires at least one clinical and one laboratory criterion, with abnormal tests on more than two occasions at least 12 weeks apart and within a 5-year window. The syndrome links venous and arterial thrombosis, early and late pregnancy loss, early pre-eclampsia, and growth restriction. Treatment is risk-stratified by obstetric history and thrombosis history: low-dose aspirin plus prophylactic heparin for recurrent loss or fetal loss, therapeutic heparin for thrombosis in the current pregnancy, and lifelong warfarin after prior thrombosis. Steroids and intravenous immune globulin add harm without benefit.

## Recommendations and Grades
- Treat APS with ≥3 unexplained consecutive losses before 10 weeks, or ≥1 unexplained fetal loss after 10 weeks, with low-dose aspirin plus prophylactic heparin (unfractionated or low-molecular-weight) — Grade [I] (individual randomized trials); meta-analysis of 19 RCTs, n = 1,251, showed live birth RR 1.23 (95% CI 1.12–1.36) for heparin plus aspirin versus aspirin alone.
- Treat APS with thrombosis during the current pregnancy with therapeutic anticoagulation — review-level support, no numeric grade stated.
- Treat APS with thrombosis before pregnancy with prophylactic heparin antepartum and for 6 weeks postpartum — review-level support.
- Give low-dose aspirin 75–100 mg daily, started once viability is established (some experts start preconception in severe cases) — no grade stated.
- Continue anticoagulation for at least 6 months after a thrombotic event, plus 6 weeks postpartum — no grade stated.
- Give no recommendation for APS with late fetal death — the two relevant trials were weak.
- Base management of APS with medically indicated preterm birth before 34 weeks on expert opinion; clinical surveillance alone is reasonable — no trial evidence.
- Deliver at 39 0/7 to 39 6/7 weeks and stop anticoagulation 4–24 hours before planned delivery — no grade stated.
- Do not use prednisone or intravenous immune globulin as adjunct therapy — no benefit and added harm (see anti-patterns).

## Key Concepts
- Clinical criteria: (1) one or more objectively confirmed arterial, venous, or small-vessel thromboses; and/or (2) pregnancy morbidity — one or more unexplained deaths of a morphologically normal fetus at or beyond 10 weeks, or one or more preterm births before 34 weeks due to eclampsia, severe pre-eclampsia, or placental insufficiency, or three or more unexplained consecutive spontaneous abortions before 10 weeks after excluding anatomic, hormonal, paternal, and maternal chromosomal causes.
- Laboratory criteria: lupus anticoagulant (LA); anticardiolipin antibody (ACA) IgG or IgM above 40 GPL or MPL; anti-beta-2 glycoprotein-I antibody above the 99th percentile.
- Up to 11% of healthy women with uncomplicated pregnancies have antiphospholipid antibodies, median prevalence about 2%. Positive predictive value for obstetric complications is poor.
- 25–35% of patients with systemic lupus erythematosus have APS. ACA occurs in 15% and LA in 8% of recurrent miscarriage; LA occurs in up to 30% of mid-trimester loss. Of definite APS patients, 70% have both ACA and LA.
- Asymptomatic non-pregnant people with antibodies have 0.5–2% thrombosis per year; 65–70% of events are venous.
- Pre-eclampsia occurs in 18–48% of APS pregnancies and is significantly associated with ACA. Autoimmune thrombocytopenia occurs in 40–50%. Preterm birth occurs in 33%.
- Stillbirth antibody prevalence versus live births: ACA IgM 6% versus 3%, ACA IgG 5% versus 1%, anti-beta-2-microglobulin-I 2% versus 0.6% (3- to 5-fold increased risk).
- Prophylactic unfractionated heparin: 5,000–7,500 U first trimester, 7,500–10,000 U second trimester, 10,000 U third trimester subcutaneously every 12 hours.
- Prophylactic low-molecular-weight heparin: enoxaparin 30–40 mg subcutaneously every 12 hours, or dalteparin 5,000 U every 12 hours. Adjust to anti-Xa 0.2–0.4 in high-risk cases; draw 4 hours after injection.
- Therapeutic dosing: intravenous unfractionated heparin to keep aPTT 2–3 times normal; enoxaparin 1 mg/kg every 12 hours or dalteparin 200 U/kg every 12 hours, adjusted to anti-Xa 0.5–1.2.
- Neuraxial anesthesia timing: intravenous unfractionated heparin 4–6 hours after dose or when aPTT is normal; subcutaneous unfractionated heparin 4–6 hours after 5,000 U, 12 hours after 7,500–10,000 U, 24 hours after more than 10,000 U; low-molecular-weight heparin at least 12–24 hours after the last prophylactic or therapeutic dose.
- Switch from low-molecular-weight heparin to unfractionated heparin at about 36 weeks if on full anticoagulation, to permit regional anesthesia.
- Heparin lowers bone mineral density by 5%. Give calcium 1,500 mg daily (gluconate or carbonate), vitamin D, and resistance exercise.
- Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia occurs in under 5%, is usually mild, and starts 3–15 days after initiation; it is less common with low-molecular-weight heparin. Check platelets at baseline and weekly for 3 weeks.
- Postpartum: 6 weeks of anticoagulation after obstetric APS in the United States (3–5 days in the United Kingdom); lifelong warfarin with INR 2.0–3.0 after prior thrombosis. Warfarin is safe in breastfeeding.
- Postpartum risk in obstetric APS without prior thrombosis: deep vein thrombosis adjusted hazard ratio 1.85 (95% CI 1.50–2.28; annualized 1.46%) and stroke adjusted hazard ratio 2.10 (95% CI 1.08–4.08; annualized 0.17%).
- About 10% of APS patients later develop systemic lupus erythematosus.
- Surveillance: early dating ultrasound, anatomy survey at 18–20 weeks, growth and fluid assessment every 4–6 weeks, and fetal testing from 32 weeks.

## Anti-patterns
- Adding prednisone to aspirin or to aspirin plus heparin — a small study found no benefit and more hypertension and diabetes in the prednisone group.
- Adding intravenous immune globulin to heparin plus aspirin — does not affect pregnancy outcome (Grade [I], RCT, n = 16).
- Testing antiphospholipid antibodies in patients who do not meet clinical criteria — isolated positive tests are common and have poor predictive value.
- Diagnosing APS from a single positive test — two tests at least 12 weeks apart are required.
- Testing for antibodies other than ACA, LA, and anti-beta-2 glycoprotein-I — not recommended.
- Expecting benefit from anticoagulation for late fetal death or for pre-eclampsia/growth restriction before 34 weeks — no trial evidence; expert opinion only.
- Using combined estrogen-containing contraceptives — relatively contraindicated because of further venous thromboembolism risk.
- Shortening or omitting postpartum prophylaxis after prior thrombosis — lifelong warfarin is indicated.

## Key Takeaways
1. Confirm APS with clinical plus laboratory criteria on two occasions ≥12 weeks apart; do not treat isolated antibodies.
2. Recurrent early loss or fetal loss after 10 weeks: low-dose aspirin plus prophylactic heparin, then 6 weeks of postpartum heparin plus aspirin ([I]).
3. Thrombosis in the current pregnancy: therapeutic heparin for at least 6 months total plus 6 weeks postpartum; prior thrombosis: lifelong warfarin, INR 2.0–3.0.
4. Stop heparin 4–24 hours before delivery and observe the neuraxial anesthesia intervals strictly.
5. Add calcium 1,500 mg daily, vitamin D, and resistance exercise for heparin-associated bone loss.
6. Plan delivery at 39 0/7 to 39 6/7 weeks to control the timing of anticoagulation.
