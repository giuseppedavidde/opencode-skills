# Chapter 34: HIV

## Core Idea
Identification of HIV in pregnancy is the prerequisite for prevention. Universal opt-out screening is recommended at entry to prenatal care, with repeat testing before 36 weeks in high-risk women and rapid testing for untested women in labor. All pregnant women with HIV receive combination antiretroviral therapy regardless of CD4 count, aiming for a viral load below 1,000 copies/mL or below the assay limit of detection. Viral load governs mode of delivery: planned cesarean at 38 weeks with zidovudine infusion for a load above 1,000 copies/mL. Effective therapy, appropriate delivery, and formula feeding reduce perinatal transmission below 2%.

## Recommendations and Grades
- Screen all pregnant women with an antigen/antibody immunoassay at entry to prenatal care using an opt-out approach — Grade [III] (national panel guidance).
- Repeat screening before 36 weeks in women with high-risk behavior, high-prevalence area, incarceration, symptoms of acute HIV, or prior declined testing — Grade [III].
- Use rapid testing for previously untested women in labor or before urgent delivery; start antiretroviral prophylaxis on a positive rapid result without waiting for confirmation — Grade [III].
- Offer combination antiretroviral therapy to all women with HIV regardless of clinical or immunological status — Grade [III].
- Monitor plasma HIV-1 RNA serially, at least initially and every trimester — Grade [III].
- Counsel women with viral load above 1,000 copies/mL about planned cesarean delivery at 38 weeks with zidovudine infusion — Grade [III].
- Allow trial of labor when the recent viral load is below 1,000 copies/mL — the added risk is under 2% — Grade [III].
- Do not breastfeed when a reliable supply of formula is available — Grade [III].
- Give zidovudine prophylaxis to the newborn and perform virologic testing — Grade [III].
- Do not give BCG vaccine to HIV-infected women or their newborns — Grade [III].

## Key Concepts
- Worldwide, 36.2 million adolescents and adults live with HIV; about 50% are women. The United States has about 1.2 million people living with HIV; 19% of new cases are in women, and Black and Hispanic women account for 75% of female diagnoses in 2018.
- Over 90% of pediatric HIV cases come from perinatal transmission or breastfeeding. Perinatal diagnoses in the United States fell 41% from 2012 to 2016, but 1,814 children under 13 still live with perinatally acquired HIV.
- Timing of transmission: antepartum 25–40%, intrapartum 60–75%, postpartum through breastfeeding 14%.
- Transmission by regimen: about 1.2% with highly active antiretroviral therapy, 10.4% with zidovudine monotherapy, and 25% with no antiretrovirals. The PACTG-076 regimen reduced transmission from 25.5% to 8%.
- Transmission can occur at any viral load, including undetectable levels, but risk correlates strongly with the load at delivery.
- Other transmission risk factors: low CD4 count, no antiretroviral therapy, viral phenotype, substance use, prolonged membrane rupture, hepatitis C co-infection, sexually transmitted infections, preterm birth, and chorioamnionitis.
- Preferred nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitor backbones (all once daily): tenofovir disoproxil fumarate/emtricitabine, tenofovir disoproxil fumarate/lamivudine, or abacavir/lamivudine. Abacavir is contraindicated with HLA-B*5701.
- Preferred integrase regimens: abacavir/lamivudine/dolutegravir once daily, or a dual backbone plus dolutegravir 50 mg daily or raltegravir 400 mg twice daily. Raltegravir should not be taken within 2 hours of a prenatal vitamin.
- Preferred protease inhibitor regimens: a dual backbone plus darunavir 600 mg/ritonavir 100 mg twice daily or atazanavir 300 mg/ritonavir 100 mg daily. Atazanavir causes maternal hyperbilirubinemia, so monitor neonatal bilirubin, and it cannot be given with proton pump inhibitors.
- Rapid tests have 95–100% sensitivity and specificity; positive predictive value falls with prevalence, reaching about 60% at 1% prevalence.
- Diagnosis algorithm: positive antigen/antibody immunoassay, then a supplemental antibody differentiation assay. Reactive immunoassay with non-reactive or indeterminate differentiation requires HIV-1 nucleic acid testing; a positive result indicates acute infection.
- Cesarean technique: continue antiretrovirals, start zidovudine infusion 3 hours before incision and continue until the cord is clamped, and give prophylactic antibiotics about 30 minutes before incision.
- Women with a recent viral load below 1,000 copies/mL may labor; prolonged membrane rupture is not a transmission risk factor. Intravenous zidovudine is indicated when the load exceeds 1,000 copies/mL; between 50 and 999 copies/mL it may be considered case by case, and it is not required when the load is below 50 copies/mL on adherent therapy.
- Intrapartum practice: induce only for obstetric indications, admit in early labor, augment to expedite delivery, delay amniotomy (not contraindicated), and avoid fetal scalp electrode, intrauterine pressure catheter, fetal blood sampling, episiotomy, forceps, and vacuum when possible.
- Amniocentesis carries a small unquantified risk; perform it only after effective combination therapy with an undetectable viral load, and avoid traversing the placenta.
- Preterm premature rupture of membranes before 34 weeks: expectant management with corticosteroids and latency antibiotics. At 34 weeks or later, deliver, with mode chosen by viral load.
- Maternal complications: chorioamnionitis, postpartum endometritis, and wound infection, with risk inversely proportional to CD4 count. Pre-eclampsia risk is increased in HIV-infected women.
- Possible increased preterm delivery with protease inhibitor regimens, but no increase in growth restriction, stillbirth, or low Apgar scores.
- Opportunistic infection prophylaxis: Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia when CD4 is below 200, or 200–250 when monitoring is not feasible, or CD4 percentage below 14 — trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole one double-strength tablet daily. Toxoplasma prophylaxis when CD4 is below 100 with positive IgG. Mycobacterium avium complex primary prophylaxis is not recommended on antiretroviral therapy. Tuberculin reaction ≥5 mm: isoniazid 300 mg plus pyridoxine 50 mg daily for 9 months.
- Vaccines: influenza annually, tetanus-diphtheria every pregnancy (preferably third trimester), hepatitis B, inactivated polio, and meningococcal. MMR or varicella postpartum if CD4 exceeds 200. Yellow fever may be given if CD4 exceeds 200 and exposure risk is high, but serologic response may be as low as 35%.
- Infant care: bathe soon after delivery, obtain a baseline CBC, give zidovudine prophylaxis, and refer to an HIV specialist. Virologic testing at 14–21 days, 1–2 months, and 4–6 months. HIV is presumptively excluded with two or more negative tests, one at 14 days or later and one at 1 month or later; antibody testing at 12–18 months confirms negative status. Maternal antibodies clear at a mean of 10.3 months and can persist to 18 months. Start Pneumocystis prophylaxis at 4–6 weeks until two consecutive negative results.
- Breastfeeding: in resource-limited settings where formula is not feasible, exclusive breastfeeding is preferred over mixed feeding, and mothers on therapy should breastfeed for 12–24 months.

## Anti-patterns
- Risk-based or opt-in screening — opt-out universal screening raises acceptance.
- Waiting for confirmatory results before starting prophylaxis after a positive rapid test in labor.
- Withholding antiretroviral therapy because CD4 count or clinical stage looks adequate — therapy is indicated for all.
- Using zidovudine monotherapy where combination therapy is available — transmission is 10.4% versus 1.2%.
- Offering a trial of labor when viral load exceeds 1,000 copies/mL without counseling on cesarean benefit.
- Elective cesarean when viral load is below 1,000 copies/mL — no meaningful added benefit, and CD4 depletion increases postoperative complications.
- Using fetal scalp electrodes, fetal blood sampling, or vacuum delivery routinely.
- Mixed breast and formula feeding where formula is unavailable — exclusive breastfeeding is safer.
- Giving BCG to an HIV-exposed newborn — disseminated BCG has been reported.
- Reassuring an infant with a positive antibody test before 18 months — maternal antibody persists.

## Key Takeaways
1. Screen universally with opt-out at first prenatal visit; retest before 36 weeks if high risk; rapid-test in labor if undocumented.
2. Start combination therapy in every pregnancy and target a viral load below 1,000 copies/mL or undetectable.
3. Check viral load every trimester; use the latest value to choose mode of delivery.
4. For viral load above 1,000 copies/mL, plan cesarean at 38 weeks with zidovudine infusion from 3 hours before incision.
5. Do not breastfeed when formula is reliably available; give newborn zidovudine and test at 14–21 days, 1–2 months, and 4–6 months.
6. Arrange long-acting reversible contraception before discharge and continue HIV primary care.
