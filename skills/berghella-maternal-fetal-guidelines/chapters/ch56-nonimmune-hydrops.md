# Chapter 56: Nonimmune Hydrops Fetalis

## Core Idea
Hydrops fetalis is fluid accumulation in two or more fetal extravascular compartments. Nonimmune hydrops fetalis (NIH) is hydrops with a negative indirect Coombs test, and now accounts for about 90% of cases because of anti-Rh prophylaxis. NIH is a final common pathway of many disorders, so management means finding a treatable cause: detailed ultrasound and echocardiography, maternal serology, and amniocentesis, with MCA-PSV to identify fetal anemia. Perinatal mortality is 50–100% and is driven by etiology, gestational age at presentation, pleural effusions, and whether the cause is treatable.

## Recommendations and Grades
- Confirm that antibody screening (indirect Coombs) is negative, including in Rh-positive patients, before labeling hydrops as nonimmune. Grade: not stated.
- Perform a structured evaluation: detailed history; ultrasound of anatomy, amniotic fluid, placenta, cord, fetal echocardiogram, and MCA-PSV; maternal blood type and antibody screen, CBC with red cell indices and hemoglobin electrophoresis, serology for parvovirus, CMV, and rubella, non-treponemal test for syphilis, and Kleihauer–Betke test. Add other tests only when history suggests them. Grade: not stated.
- Offer amniocentesis for karyotype with microarray and PCR for parvovirus B19, toxoplasmosis, and CMV. Freeze and store amniotic fluid so that RASopathy or lysosomal storage disorder testing can follow if phenotype later suggests it. Grade: not stated.
- In otherwise unexplained NIH, test for genetic disorders such as RASopathies or lysosomal storage disorders, because approximately 29% of unexplained cases may be due to a genetic disorder. Grade: not stated.
- Do not use cordocentesis routinely in the workup, but strongly recommend it when fetal anemia is suspected (MCA-PSV >1.5 MoM). Grade: not stated.
- Perform weekly NST, BPP (at ≥28 weeks), and Doppler studies of the umbilical artery and MCA when the etiology is non-lethal, the fetus is potentially viable, and test findings would guide delivery timing. Grade: not stated.
- Give antenatal steroids to reduce neonatal complications of preterm delivery. Grade: not stated.
- Deliver for fetal or maternal deterioration, such as mirror syndrome; consider aspiration of excessive pericardial, pleural, or peritoneal fluid before delivery to improve neonatal status and reduce dystocia and fetal trauma. Grade: not stated.

## Key Concepts
- Definition: at least two of hydrothorax, ascites, pericardial effusion, and skin edema (>5 mm at the skull or chest wall). Associated findings: polyhydramnios in 40–75% and placentomegaly (placental thickness ≥4 cm in the second trimester, ≥6 cm in the third).
- Incidence 1/1700 to 1/3000 pregnancies, reported as 1/4000 live births. Etiology is suspected or confirmed prenatally in 50–80% of cases; 5% remain idiopathic after delivery, and 10–20% of causes remain undetermined after workup.
- Etiology: cardiovascular 20%; non-cardiovascular anomalies 15–25%; chromosomal 15–20%; infection 10–15%; hematological 5–15%; monochorionic twin complications 5%; genetic syndromes 1%; metabolic 1–5%; no identifiable cause 20%.
- Cardiovascular detail: tachyarrhythmias 40% of cardiac causes, structural malformations 20%. Complete heart block causes hydrops when the fetal heart rate is below 60 beats per minute.
- Chromosomal abnormalities are inversely proportional to gestational age at diagnosis: 50–75% when NIH is diagnosed before 20 weeks. Turner and Down syndromes account for 90% of aneuploidies associated with hydrops.
- MCA-PSV: >90% sensitive and specific for fetal anemia at a cutoff of >1.5 MoM. The rise reflects reduced blood viscosity, increased venous return, and increased cardiac output.
- Parvovirus: vertical transmission 30%, but most fetuses are unaffected; overall NIH rate 4.2%, rising to 10.6% when infection occurs at 9–20 weeks. Hydrops increases the risk of intrauterine death (OR 3.60, 95% CI 1.3–10.5) and perinatal death (OR 4.16, 95% CI 1.6–11.0). Fetuses with hydrops are far more likely to need transfusion (OR 159, 95% CI 41.5–611). Intrauterine death was 6% with transfusion versus 30% without.
- Tachyarrhythmia treatment: digoxin has historically been first line, with flecainide, sotalol, amiodarone, verapamil, and adenosine as alternatives. In hydropic fetuses digoxin bioavailability falls and flecainide bioavailability rises: digoxin had lower rates of tachycardia resolution than flecainide (OR 0.412, 95% CI 0.268–0.632), and flecainide was superior for conversion to sinus rhythm (OR 5.0, 95% CI 2.5–10).
- Thoracic lesions: intrapleural OK-432 has shown promising results in three studies. Serial thoracocentesis (for example every 48 hours) is discouraged.
- Mirror syndrome (Ballantyne syndrome): maternal edema that mirrors the hydropic fetus, with hypertension, proteinuria, and oliguria; the reported intrauterine fetal death rate was 57.9% and no maternal deaths occurred. Delivery is recommended in most cases, and should not be delayed if maternal status worsens.
- Obstetric complications: polyhydramnios in 33.6%, with preterm delivery, placental abruption, and postpartum hemorrhage; large edematous placentas occur in 62.8%.
- Prognosis: perinatal mortality 50–100%. Infants with chromosomal abnormalities have the highest mortality, followed by genetic syndromes and inborn errors of metabolism; infections and placental causes have the lowest. Of live births, 64% survive, and 40% of survivors have associated morbidity. The strongest predictors of neonatal death are the underlying cause, gestational age at delivery, and lower serum albumin at birth.
- Neonatal care: deliver at a center with a level III neonatal intensive care unit when neonatal intervention may help. Prompt intubation is often needed, and the team should be ready for thoracentesis, paracentesis, and pericardiocentesis. Autopsy should be performed after fetal or neonatal death.

## Anti-patterns
- Accepting a diagnosis of immune hydrops without a negative indirect Coombs test, including in Rh-positive women.
- Routine cordocentesis in every hydropic fetus: the hemodynamic state is fragile.
- Serial thoracocentesis every 48 hours for pleural effusion.
- Expectant management of mirror syndrome with worsening maternal status.
- Assuming a normal anatomy scan excludes a genetic cause: about 29% of unexplained NIH may be genetic.
- Omitting autopsy: it is the main route to a cause and to recurrence risk.

## Key Takeaways
- Diagnose hydrops by two or more fluid compartments and exclude alloimmunization first.
- Measure MCA-PSV in every hydropic fetus; above 1.5 MoM, proceed to cordocentesis and consider transfusion.
- Screen for parvovirus, CMV, syphilis, and toxoplasmosis, and offer karyotype with microarray plus PCR on amniotic fluid.
- Freeze amniotic fluid and plan RASopathy and lysosomal storage testing when the workup is negative.
- Treat what is treatable (parvovirus anemia, tachyarrhythmia), give steroids, and deliver for deterioration or mirror syndrome.
