# Chapter 20: Other Neurologic Diseases in Pregnancy

## Core Idea
Neurologic disease in pregnancy needs a multidisciplinary team that includes obstetrics, maternal-fetal medicine, neurology, anesthesia, and rehabilitation, with the patient's goals at the center. Pregnancy alters maternal tolerance of neurologic disease, so symptoms need close follow-up for deterioration. Four conditions are covered here: spinal cord injury with autonomic dysreflexia, Guillain-Barre syndrome, intracranial aneurysm, and myasthenia gravis. Each requires a planned delivery setting, an anesthesia strategy, and postpartum planning.

## Recommendations and Grades
The chapter states no formal evidence grades; recommendations are narrative.
- Provide preconception counseling to every woman with neurologic disease; most such disease does not affect fertility, so offer contraception to those avoiding pregnancy.
- Give at least 400 micrograms of folic acid preconception, and 4 mg daily to women with congenital spinal lesions such as meningomyelocele because of the increased risk of spinal cord lesions in offspring.
- Screen frequently for depression, because perinatal depression risk is markedly increased in neurologic disease.
- Hold a pre-delivery anesthesia consultation in the second trimester when regional anesthesia may be unsafe or technically difficult.
- For spinal cord injury, screen for asymptomatic bacteriuria, treat urinary infection aggressively, use stool softeners and a high-fiber diet, examine skin at every visit, reposition frequently, and minimize falls. Universal pharmacologic thromboprophylaxis is not supported by sufficient data.
- Use intermittent self-catheterization every 4-6 hours rather than an indwelling catheter, and apply lidocaine before Foley placement.
- For Guillain-Barre syndrome, give plasmapheresis or intravenous immunoglobulin for severe or worsening weakness; steroids are not indicated.
- For intracranial aneurysm, consider intervention before pregnancy when the aneurysm measures 3-6 mm.
- For myasthenia gravis, continue pyridostigmine up to 600 mg/day and avoid magnesium sulfate and fluoroquinolones.

## Key Concepts
- Spinal cord injury: 17,730 new injuries occur yearly in the United States, about 20% in women, and about 2,000 affected women become pregnant each year.
- Antenatal complications of spinal cord injury: urinary tract complications 59%, autonomic dysreflexia 27%, worsened spasticity 22%, thrombosis 8%, postpartum depression 35%, preterm birth 8-13%, anemia 12%, pressure sores 6-15%, and venous thromboembolism up to 8%.
- Autonomic dysreflexia affects up to 85% of lesions at or above T6 and can be fatal. Systemic hypertension is the most common sign and is synchronous with uterine contractions, unlike the hypertension of pre-eclampsia, which is independent of contractions. Other features are hyperthermia, piloerection, diaphoresis, spasticity, pupillary dilation, bradycardia or tachycardia, anxiety, headache, and loss of consciousness.
- Dysreflexia treatment: remove the stimulus, sit the patient upright and loosen clothing, give rapid-onset short-acting nifedipine or labetalol, and prepare for rebound hypotension by lowering the head and raising the legs. Neuraxial anesthesia at the onset of labor with a T10 block is the treatment of choice and may be life-saving. Expedite delivery in the second stage, and proceed to cesarean birth if dysreflexia cannot be controlled.
- Acute spinal cord injury: neurogenic shock causes hypotension, bradycardia, and hypothermia. Treat as outside pregnancy with volume resuscitation and pressors; methylprednisolone within 8 hours may improve neurologic recovery. Deep vein thrombosis risk peaks within 8 weeks, so consider prophylaxis.
- Cord transection above T10 can produce painless labor and unattended delivery; abdominal or leg spasms, shortness of breath, or increased spasticity may be the only signals. Consider induction or admission when the cervix is dilated, especially with lesions above T6.
- Guillain-Barre syndrome: acute inflammatory polyneuropathy with ascending symmetric weakness, hypo- or areflexia, mild sensory loss, and albuminocytologic dissociation in cerebrospinal fluid, typically triggered 2-4 weeks after an infection such as Campylobacter jejuni.
- Guillain-Barre outcomes: maternal mortality 10%, intensive care admission up to 35%, overall mortality 2-10%, and up to 20% non-ambulatory at 6 months.
- Guillain-Barre treatment: intravenous immunoglobulin 1 g/kg daily for 2 days or 0.4 g/kg daily for 5 days (2 g/kg total); a second course is unsupported and delivery does not change the course.
- Guillain-Barre anesthesia: avoid succinylcholine because of hyperkalemia, autonomic instability, and difficult extubation. Regional anesthesia and passive or assisted second stage are preferred; cesarean delivery is only for obstetric indications.
- Aneurysm: 1-6% of the population has an intracranial aneurysm and 1.8% of those aged 16-22 do. Rupture affects 1-10 per 100,000 pregnancies, 92% antepartum and 80% in the third trimester, with 3-7% mortality; overall rupture morbidity and mortality is 20-30%.
- Aneurysm size: five-year rupture risk below 7 mm is negligible in the anterior circulation but 2.5% posterior; 7-12 mm carries 2.6% and 14.5%, 13-24 mm 14.5% and 18.4%, and above 25 mm 40% and 50%. Over 90% of pregnancy ruptures occurred above 6 mm, so 6 mm may be the better cutoff.
- Aneurysm delivery: vaginal delivery does not appear to increase rupture risk, though a shortened second stage is preferable; cesarean delivery is reasonable for large or unstable aneurysms.
- Myasthenia gravis: autoimmune fatigable ocular, bulbar, and proximal weakness from antibodies to the acetylcholine receptor, MuSK, LRP4, or agrin, worsened by pregnancy, infection, medication, and anesthesia.
- Myasthenia gravis management: pregnancy does not alter the course or increase pregnancy loss, but early pregnancy and postpartum are flare periods. Intravenous cholinesterase inhibitors can cause uterine contractions, and steroids for lung maturity can worsen a flare unless given after immunoglobulin or plasmapheresis.
- Neonatal myasthenia occurs in 10-20% of infants, with feeding difficulty, swallowing difficulty, respiratory difficulty, or hypotonia appearing 12-48 hours after birth; keep infants in hospital at least 48 hours.

## Anti-patterns
- Do not use magnesium sulfate in myasthenia gravis; it causes neuromuscular blockade.
- Do not prescribe fluoroquinolones in myasthenia gravis; they provoke flares.
- Do not use succinylcholine in Guillain-Barre syndrome because of hyperkalemia risk.
- Do not mistake dysreflexia for pre-eclampsia; contraction-synchronous hypertension points to dysreflexia.
- Do not perform episiotomy in spinal cord injury; it can trigger prolonged dysreflexia.

## Key Takeaways
- Plan delivery with anesthesia in the second trimester for any neuromuscular disease.
- Treat dysreflexia by removing the trigger, sitting the patient upright, giving nifedipine or labetalol, and starting epidural anesthesia early.
- Give intravenous immunoglobulin or plasmapheresis promptly in Guillain-Barre syndrome; steroids do not help.
- Offer aneurysm intervention before pregnancy at 3-6 mm, and consider cesarean delivery for large or unstable aneurysms.
- Keep infants of myasthenic mothers at least 48 hours for observation of neonatal myasthenia.
