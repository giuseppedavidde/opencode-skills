# Chapter 21: Mood Disorders

## Core Idea
Depression is twice as common in women as in men and peaks between ages 25 and 44. Perinatal depression is one of the most common complications of pregnancy, with global postpartum prevalence near 17.7%, and untreated disease raises preterm birth, low birth weight, and impaired child development. Psychotherapy is first-line, and antidepressants are added for severe or relapsing illness at the lowest effective dose. Postpartum psychosis affects 0.1-0.2% of women, is a psychiatric emergency, and is a strong predictor of bipolar disorder. Bipolar disorder carries a very high postpartum relapse risk, especially when mood stabilizers are stopped.

## Recommendations and Grades
The chapter states no formal evidence grades; recommendations are narrative.
- Screen all pregnant and postpartum women at least once with a validated tool such as the PHQ-9 or the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS), and follow a positive screen with assessment, referral, and treatment.
- Repeat screening at the comprehensive postpartum visit, and screen at the 1-, 2-, and 4-month well-child visits per the American Academy of Pediatrics.
- Offer psychotherapy to every pregnant woman with depression; cognitive behavioral therapy and interpersonal psychotherapy are effective.
- Use one medication rather than several, at the lowest effective dose, and avoid suboptimal dosing that leaves both depression and drug exposure.
- Prefer alternatives to paroxetine as first-line because of a small excess of cardiac malformations.
- Continue lithium in women with severe bipolar illness who have responded well, and obtain high-resolution ultrasound and fetal echocardiography after first-trimester exposure.
- Avoid valproic acid in women of childbearing potential; the risk of major congenital anomaly is about 10.9%.
- Consider electroconvulsive therapy for severe or refractory perinatal depression, high suicide risk, psychotic features, catatonia, or significant physical decline.
- Continue the antidepressant that worked during pregnancy into lactation; for a treatment-naive nursing mother, prefer paroxetine or sertraline, and nortriptyline among tricyclics.

## Key Concepts
- Definitions: DSM-5 applies the "peripartum onset" specifier to the first 4 weeks after birth, ICD-10 allows up to 6 weeks, and clinicians commonly use up to 1 year. Postpartum blues affects up to 75% of mothers, begins on days 2-4, and resolves in about 2 weeks without impairing function.
- Epidemiology: major depression affects 8.7% of women versus 5.3% of men; perinatal prevalence is 6-12.9% and higher in low-income countries; postpartum depression affects 6-17%; suicide accounts for up to 20% of postpartum deaths.
- Postpartum psychosis: incidence 0.89-2.6 per 1,000 births, with mood lability, agitation, confusion, thought disorganization, hallucinations, and disturbed sleep. Onset peaks on postpartum days 1-3, and 94% of manic or psychotic episodes in bipolar I disease begin within 4 weeks. It carries risk of suicide, infant neglect, and infanticide.
- Bipolar disorder: lifetime prevalence 1% for type I and 1.1% for type II, with a 2:1 female excess in type II. Postpartum relapse is 35%, rising to 66% when mood stabilizers are stopped versus 23% when maintained, and psychiatric admission within 30 days of delivery is 23-fold more likely than in non-postpartum women. Of women with a single postpartum psychosis, 56.7% later have non-postpartum episodes consistent with bipolar disorder.
- Antidepressant discontinuation: relapse risk doubles for severe or recurrent depression but not for mild or moderate disease.
- SSRI and SNRI safety: not major teratogens in most large reviews, though some show small increases in cardiac and other defects that are partly explained by confounding by indication. Associations are reported with preterm birth, low birth weight, small for gestational age, spontaneous abortion, pre-eclampsia, postpartum hemorrhage, and persistent pulmonary hypertension of the newborn.
- Poor neonatal adaptation syndrome: jitteriness, tremor, tachypnea, hypoglycemia, temperature instability, weak cry, poor tone, and mild respiratory distress in up to 30% of serotonergic-exposed infants, usually resolving within 2 weeks.
- Lithium: baseline Ebstein anomaly risk is 1:20,000; the odds ratio for any cardiac anomaly is 1.86 and rises with doses above 900 mg, while absolute Ebstein risk is about 1:1,500. The number needed to treat for relapse prevention is 3 and the number needed to harm for any congenital anomaly is 33. Renal excretion rises 30-50% in the third trimester, so doses often increase; monitor thyroid function and maintain hydration in labor.
- Lithium neonates: arrhythmia, nephrogenic diabetes insipidus, thyroid dysfunction, hypotonia, hypoglycemia, hyperbilirubinemia, poor respiratory effort, and cyanosis, usually transient. If lithium is stopped, taper over more than 15 days; rapid withdrawal shortens time to relapse.
- Valproate: major malformations in about 10.9%, including neural tube defects, cardiovascular anomalies, limb defects, and hypospadias; neural tube defects are 10-20 times more frequent than in the general population. Cognitive deficits, learning difficulty, and autism are repeatedly reported, with risk at doses above 800-1,000 mg and higher with polytherapy.
- Carbamazepine: malformation rates 2.2-5.4%, relative risk 2 versus unexposed women, and neural tube defects in 0.5-1%; doses above 1,000 mg carry more risk than doses below 400 mg.
- Second-generation antipsychotics: first-trimester quetiapine, olanzapine, and aripiprazole data are reassuring with no clinically meaningful increase in teratogenicity; risperidone showed a relative risk of 1.26 for cardiac and overall malformations.
- Electroconvulsive therapy: risks resemble non-pregnant patients, with headache, confusion, and memory complaints, plus uterine contraction risk of 0.6-24%; the most common fetal adverse event is cardiac arrhythmia.
- Breastfeeding: most SSRIs produce low infant levels. Fluoxetine has higher infant plasma levels because of its long half-life; venlafaxine has a higher relative infant dose, still below 10%. Lamotrigine reaches relatively high milk levels and infant levels, so monitor the infant.
- Newer options: brexanolone is a 60-hour infusion for postpartum depression requiring continuous pulse oximetry and sedation checks under a risk mitigation strategy; ketamine prevention data are limited and conflicting.

## Anti-patterns
- Do not use subtherapeutic antidepressant doses; partial treatment exposes the fetus to both drug and untreated depression.
- Do not prescribe valproate for migraine in pregnancy, and use it for epilepsy or bipolar disorder only when other treatments fail.
- Do not stop lithium abruptly; taper over more than 15 days.
- Do not stop an effective antidepressant postpartum solely to start a different drug for lactation.

## Key Takeaways
- Screen with PHQ-9 or EPDS at least once antenatally and again postpartum, and act on positive results.
- Start with psychotherapy; add a single antidepressant at the lowest effective dose when illness is severe or relapsing.
- Prefer sertraline or paroxetine in lactation, and continue the drug that worked in pregnancy.
- Continue lithium in severe bipolar illness with fetal echocardiography, and avoid valproate entirely when possible.
- Treat postpartum psychosis as an emergency; onset clusters in the first 3 days after delivery.
