# Chapter 49: Cytomegalovirus

## Core Idea
Cytomegalovirus (CMV) is the most common cause of viral intrauterine infection, affecting 0.5–1.5% of all neonates. Most pregnant women acquire CMV from young children at home or at work, and approximately 1–4% of IgG-negative women seroconvert in pregnancy. Vertical transmission follows primary or recurrent infection; the transmission rate rises with gestational age, but disease severity is inversely proportional to gestational age. There is no in-utero therapy supported by robust level 1 data, so screening is not routine and management centers on prevention, accurate serology, amniotic fluid PCR, and counseling.

## Recommendations and Grades
- Do not perform routine CMV screening in pregnancy. The text states screening is "not routinely recommended in most countries, until an appropriate fetal intervention is proven to decrease neonatal disease." Grade: not formally graded; the stated reason is absence of Level 1 evidence for in-utero therapy.
- If a fetal intervention is ever proven, screen all pregnant women with IgM and IgG between 8 and 12 weeks, with a second and possibly a third antibody control at 18–20 weeks and 30–32 weeks ("could be recommended"). Grade: not stated.
- Do not use CMV hyperimmune globulin for prevention or treatment. "There is insufficient evidence to recommend CMV-specific hyperimmune globulin"; a randomized, placebo-controlled, double-blind trial of 124 women with primary infection at 5–26 weeks did not confirm the benefit seen in nonrandomized studies. Grade as stated: [I, RCT, n = 124].
- Do not adopt valacyclovir routinely. A double-blind placebo-controlled trial (n = 100) showed fewer positive amniocenteses (5/45, 11.1% vs 14/47, 29.8%; p = 0.027; OR 0.29, 95% CI 0.09–0.90), and an open-label phase II study (n = 41) showed 82% asymptomatic neonates versus 43% in controls. The text concludes use "cannot yet be routinely recommended" pending more Level 1 evidence. Grade as stated: [I, RCT, n = 100] and [II-1; n = 41].
- Diagnose fetal infection by amniotic fluid PCR, not by percutaneous umbilical blood sampling (PUBS). "PUBS should be avoided." Grade: not stated.
- Allow at least 6 weeks after maternal primary infection and at least 21 weeks' gestation before amniocentesis; repeat the test if performed earlier. Grade: not stated.

## Key Concepts
- Transmission: about one third of primary infections transmit (range 30–65%); 15–20% of infected infants develop sequelae, equivalent to 5–8% of infected mothers. Long-term mortality for severely affected infants is about 5%.
- Congenital findings: jaundice, petechiae ("blueberry muffin baby"), thrombocytopenia, hepatosplenomegaly, growth restriction, microcephaly, intracranial calcifications, nonimmune hydrops, preterm birth; later hearing loss, mental retardation, psychomotor delay, chorioretinitis, optic atrophy, seizures, language delay, learning disability.
- Serology: IgM is 75% sensitive and persists 4–8 months. IgG avidity index >65% excludes primary infection. Avidity and serum PCR have clinical value when IgM is positive.
- Amniocentesis: AF PCR sensitivity 80–100%; specificity and positive predictive value near 100%. Recent data show no difference when amniocentesis is done at ≥17 0/7 versus ≥20 0/7 weeks, provided 8 weeks have passed since seroconversion. Amniocentesis itself does not cause iatrogenic fetal infection.
- Negative AF PCR after primary infection: neonatal infection can still occur, but neurological sequelae are not reported and hearing impairment at birth is <3%; long-term outcome is excellent.
- Quantitative AF PCR helps predict infection and later sequelae. Viral culture takes 2–6 weeks. Neonatal diagnosis is by PCR of body fluids, especially urine.
- Hyperimmune globulin dosing used in the nonrandomized studies: 100 U/kg IV monthly until delivery for prevention; 200 U/kg IV for therapy, with additional amniotic fluid or cord infusions for persistent ultrasound findings. Symptomatic disease at birth fell from 50% to 3% in the treated cohort; this was not reproduced in the RCT.
- Valacyclovir regimens: 8 g total daily, divided as 4 g twice daily, from enrollment to amniocentesis at 21–22 weeks (prevention trial); 8 g total daily as 2 g four times daily, median start 25 weeks (treatment trial).
- Prevention: avoid kissing on the lips, sharing utensils or food, changing diapers, wiping the child's nose, and handling toys; handwashing and glove use. Prevention was associated with an 84% decrease in seroconversion [33: I, RCT, n = 166].

## Anti-patterns
- Routine serologic screening: no proven fetal intervention exists, and screening misses nonprimary infections.
- Diagnosing primary infection from a single positive IgM: IgM persists 4–8 months and most positive IgM results from non-reference laboratories are not recent infection.
- PUBS for diagnosis: it is invasive and is explicitly to be avoided.
- Using a normal ultrasound to exclude severe neurological damage or childhood sensorineural hearing loss.
- Offering hyperimmune globulin or valacyclovir as standard care: neither is supported by level 1 evidence, and the RCT of hyperimmune globulin was negative.

## Key Takeaways
- Counsel susceptible women on hygiene; this is the only intervention with consistent support.
- Confirm primary infection at a reference laboratory using paired sera, IgG avidity, and serum PCR.
- Perform amniocentesis at ≥21 weeks and ≥6 weeks after infection; a negative PCR carries an excellent prognosis.
- Treat ultrasound findings as counseling aids, not as exclusion tests.
- Do not promise benefit from hyperimmune globulin; discuss valacyclovir as promising but unproven.
