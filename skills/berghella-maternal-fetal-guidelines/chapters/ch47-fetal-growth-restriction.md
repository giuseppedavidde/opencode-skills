# Chapter 47: Fetal Growth Restriction

## Core Idea
Fetal growth restriction (FGR) is a sonographic estimated fetal weight (EFW) or abdominal circumference (AC) below the 10th percentile for gestational age, using population-based references such as Hadlock and confirmed by accurate early dating; small for gestational age (SGA) describes a newborn with birth weight below the 10th percentile. Severe FGR is an EFW below the 3rd percentile, or an EFW/AC below the 10th percentile with abnormal umbilical artery Doppler. Up to 70% of fetuses below the 10th percentile are constitutionally small and not at risk; abnormal umbilical artery Doppler is what separates pathologic FGR from constitutional smallness. Placental insufficiency causes 30–40% of cases, and chromosomal disorders or malformations cause about 20%. There is no proven treatment that improves growth once FGR is diagnosed; management consists of identifying etiology, structured surveillance, and delivery at a Doppler-specific gestational age.

## Recommendations and Grades
- Diagnose FGR with sonographic EFW or AC below the 10th percentile for gestational age, using population-based growth references (Hadlock). Source: SMFM Consult Series #52 [III], endorsed by the chapter.
- Confirm gestational age by first-trimester ultrasound before 13 weeks and 6 days; dating is a prerequisite to diagnosis.
- Classify by age at diagnosis: early-onset FGR before 32 weeks (20–30% of cases) and late-onset FGR at or after 32 weeks (70–80%).
- Offer chromosomal microarray when FGR is associated with a congenital malformation, polyhydramnios, or both, at any gestational age; offer it for unexplained isolated FGR diagnosed before 32 weeks, where the incremental yield over karyotype is 4–10%. Source: SMFM [III].
- Do not order routine TORCH serologies without risk factors or sonographic markers: congenital infection is uncommon (CMV 1.8% in one series of 319 FGR pregnancies, and 0.6% with no findings other than FGR; no toxoplasmosis, rubella, or herpes cases).
- Do not use low-dose aspirin for the sole indication of FGR prevention. Source: ACOG.
- Do not use low-molecular-weight heparin to prevent recurrent placenta-mediated complications including FGR: no benefit (18% versus 18%).
- Do not recommend bed rest, nutrient supplementation, betamimetics, calcium channel blockers, heparin, oxygen, plasma volume expansion, nitric oxide donors, or sildenafil to treat established FGR. Sildenafil was tested in the STRIDER NZAus RCT and did not increase fetal growth velocity (52.5% versus 68.4%).
- Perform growth ultrasound every 3–4 weeks; use a 2-week interval for severe FGR or abnormal umbilical artery Doppler.
- Perform umbilical artery Doppler at diagnosis, then weekly; if normal at the initial assessment, extend to every 2–4 weeks. With decreased end-diastolic velocity (indices above the 95th percentile) or severe FGR (EFW <3rd percentile), Doppler weekly. With absent end-diastolic velocity (AEDV), Doppler 2–3 times per week. With reversed end-diastolic velocity (REDV), hospitalize, give antenatal corticosteroids, and perform cardiotocography (CTG) at least 1–2 times per day.
- Perform CTG at least weekly in all FGR pregnancies, increasing frequency with A/REDV or comorbidities. Do not use the biophysical profile alone without CTG.
- Do not use middle cerebral artery, ductus venosus, or uterine artery Doppler for routine management. Practices that incorporate ductus venosus may restrict it to fetuses with absent or reversed umbilical artery flow between 28 and 32 weeks.
- Give antenatal corticosteroids if delivery is anticipated before 33 6/7 weeks, or at 34 0/7–36 6/7 weeks for women at risk of delivery within 7 days who have not had a prior course. Betamethasone 12 mg IM every 24 hours ×2 doses is associated with reduced RDS, IVH, necrotizing enterocolitis, and perinatal mortality.
- Give magnesium sulfate for fetal and neonatal neuroprotection before 32 0/7 weeks when delivery is likely.
- Coordinate care with neonatology and counsel comprehensively before active management at less than 26 0/7 weeks or 500 g.
- Consider cesarean delivery for FGR complicated by absent or reversed end-diastolic flow, based on the whole clinical picture. Four national guidelines recommend cesarean for A/REDV, because fetal heart rate decelerations requiring cesarean occur in 75–95% of such labors.
- Induce with mechanical methods rather than prostaglandins where possible: Foley balloon induction is associated with fewer adverse perinatal outcomes than dinoprostone or misoprostol, though the evidence quality is low.
- For a subsequent pregnancy, screen for FGR with third-trimester ultrasound surveillance; recurrence risk reaches 23% when the etiology is uncertain.

## Key Concepts
- Timing thresholds (SMFM Consult Series #52): EFW 3rd–10th percentile with normal umbilical artery Doppler, deliver at 38 0/7–39 0/7 weeks; decreased diastolic flow (S/D, RI, or PI above the 95th percentile) without A/REDV, or severe FGR (EFW <3rd percentile), deliver at 37 0/7 weeks; AEDV, deliver at 33 0/7–34 0/7 weeks; REDV, deliver at 30 0/7–32 0/7 weeks; FGR with oligohydramnios, deliver at 34 0/7–37 6/7 weeks. Deliver at any time for repetitive late decelerations or maternal indications, regardless of Doppler.
- Justification for the Doppler thresholds: with AEDV the stillbirth risk is 6.8% and exceeds the risk of prematurity at 33–34 weeks (OR for fetal death 3.59, 95% CI 2.3–5.6); with REDV the stillbirth risk is 19% and exceeds the risk at 30–32 weeks (OR 7.27, 95% CI 4.6–11.4).
- Late-onset FGR with cerebral redistribution: consider delivery at 38 0/7 and not later than 38 6/7 weeks (ISUOG).
- Umbilical artery Doppler reduces perinatal death by 29%. Mean time to delivery after an umbilical artery PI above the 95th percentile is 26 days, after AEDV 12 days, and after REDV 4 days.
- Severity by weight: EFW below the 3rd percentile carries a threefold stillbirth risk over the 3rd–5th percentile and a four- to sevenfold risk over the 5th–10th percentile at all gestational ages.
- Phenotypes: early-onset FGR prevalence 0.5–1%, 70% abnormal umbilical artery Doppler, 60% preeclampsia, high mortality; late-onset FGR prevalence 5–10%, <10% abnormal umbilical artery Doppler, 15% preeclampsia, low individual mortality but a large population attributable fraction.
- FGR accounts for up to 43% of stillbirths and is present in most unexplained stillbirths. SGA carries a 5-fold increased stillbirth risk beyond 37 weeks, and perinatal death may be up to 100 times that of normally grown babies. Intrapartum asphyxia complicates 50% of FGR pregnancies.
- Long-term sequelae: cerebral palsy, short stature, cognitive delay, and in adulthood hypertension, coronary disease, stroke, type 2 diabetes, and obesity.
- Maternal: hypertension in 50% at diagnosis and 70% at delivery in early-onset FGR (TRUFFLE); cesarean rate 43% when induction is performed with EFW <5th percentile, and up to 90% in the GRIT study.
- Genetics: chromosomal abnormalities in almost 20% of early-onset FGR; at least 50% of trisomy 13 and 18 fetuses are growth restricted; mean chromosomal anomaly rate 6.4% in fetuses without structural malformations. Recurrence of SGA is 23% after a prior SGA neonate versus 3.4% without (adjusted OR 8.1, 95% CI 7.8–8.5).
- Etiology: maternal hypertensive disease, pregestational diabetes (FGR in 10–20% regardless of glycemic control), autoimmune disease, antiphospholipid syndrome, cyanotic heart disease, renal or pulmonary disease, smoking (3.5-fold SGA risk), alcohol, cocaine, teratogens; fetal aneuploidy, structural anomalies, infection, multiple gestation; placental abruption, infarction, chorioangioma, velamentous or marginal cord insertion, single umbilical artery. Confined placental mosaicism is found in over 20% of idiopathic FGR. Twin FGR affects 20–30% of dichorionic and 40% of monochorionic pregnancies.
- Screening: fundal height detects 21% (abdominal palpation 48% in one RCT); PAPP-A below the 5th percentile has 10.4% sensitivity and 18.7% positive predictive value. Universal third-trimester ultrasound detects 57% of SGA and 77% of severe SGA versus 20% and 32% with selective scanning, but positive predictive value is only 35% and 12%. Uterine artery Doppler is not recommended for screening.
- Cerebral placental ratio (middle cerebral artery PI divided by umbilical artery PI) below 1.08 or the 5th percentile is abnormal; in 881 FGR fetuses at 24 0/7–36 6/7 weeks, abnormal CPR had 87% sensitivity and 61% specificity for adverse outcome, with an 11-fold increased risk.
- Ductus venosus: absent or reversed A-wave gives an OR for stillbirth of 11.16 (95% CI 6.31–19.73) and a stillbirth frequency of 20%; reversed A-wave before 34 weeks carries a 46% stillbirth risk. DV becomes abnormal 48–72 hours before the biophysical profile in 90% of cases, allowing steroids before delivery. TRUFFLE: survival without neurologic impairment 95% with late DV changes versus 85% with computerized CTG short-term variation.
- Biophysical profile: no difference in perinatal death (RR 1.33, 95% CI 0.60–2.98) but an increased cesarean rate (RR 1.60, 95% CI 1.05–2.44). Amniotic fluid abnormalities did not independently increase adverse outcomes in the PORTO study of over 1100 pregnancies.
- Before 26 weeks with abnormal Doppler, neonatal survival is 13% at 24 weeks, 43% at 25 weeks, and 58–76% at 26 weeks; intact survival is 0%, 13%, and 6–31% respectively.
- Twins with FGR: DC/DA with isolated FGR, deliver at 36 0/7–36 6/7 weeks; with abnormal Doppler or maternal comorbidity, 32 0/7–34 6/7 weeks; MC/DA twins, 32 0/7–34 6/7 weeks; consider delivery for REDF at 30–31 weeks, AEDF at 32–33 weeks, or abnormal (not absent/reversed) Doppler at 35–36 weeks.

## Anti-patterns
- Treating an EFW below the 10th percentile as synonymous with pathologic FGR: most such fetuses are constitutionally small and normal.
- Diagnosing FGR on a scan without confirmed early dating.
- Using customized growth standards expecting better outcomes: evidence does not show improved detection or outcome.
- Relying on middle cerebral artery, uterine artery, or ductus venosus Doppler for routine management.
- Using the biophysical profile alone without CTG: it increases cesarean delivery without improving outcome.
- Ordering TORCH serologies reflexively.
- Administering aspirin, heparin, sildenafil, betamimetics, oxygen, or bed rest to treat established FGR.
- Waiting for a biophysical profile to become abnormal when the ductus venosus has already reversed.
- Using the TRUFFLE results as direct United States practice: computerized CTG short-term variation is not widely available there, and the ductus venosus is not recommended routinely.
- Delivering at 37 weeks every FGR fetus: timing must follow the Doppler category.
- Inducing with prostaglandins when a mechanical method is suitable.

## Key Takeaways
1. Diagnose with EFW or AC <10th percentile on a Hadlock reference with confirmed dating; call it severe at EFW <3rd percentile or <10th with abnormal umbilical artery Doppler.
2. Early-onset is before 32 weeks and late-onset at or after 32 weeks; they differ in Doppler behavior, hypertension association, and outcome.
3. Look for a cause: detailed anatomy and umbilical artery Doppler in all, microarray for anomalies, polyhydramnios, or unexplained isolated FGR before 32 weeks.
4. Surveillance: growth every 3–4 weeks (every 2 weeks if severe or abnormal Doppler); umbilical artery Doppler weekly (2–4 weekly if normal, 2–3 times weekly if AEDV); CTG at least weekly, 1–2 times daily with REDV.
5. Timing: 38–39 weeks for EFW 3rd–10th with normal Doppler; 37 weeks for decreased end-diastolic flow or EFW <3rd; 33–34 weeks for AEDV; 30–32 weeks for REDV; 34–37 6/7 weeks for oligohydramnios.
6. Give corticosteroids before 33 6/7 weeks and magnesium before 32 weeks; consider cesarean for absent or reversed end-diastolic flow.
7. Counsel that recurrence is up to 23% and that no preventive treatment has proven benefit.
