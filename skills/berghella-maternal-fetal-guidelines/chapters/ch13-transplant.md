# Chapter 13: Pregnancy after Liver and Other Transplantation

## Core Idea
Pregnancy after liver transplantation (LTx) is high risk and needs joint transplant hepatology and maternal-fetal medicine care. Outcomes are best at least 1 year after transplant, with a stable graft and regimen, proteinuria below 1 g per 24 hours, creatinine below 1.5 mg/dL, and controlled or absent hypertension. Pregnancy does not harm a previously stable hepatic allograft, but acute cellular rejection occurs in 2-15% of pregnancies, usually early. Preterm birth, pre-eclampsia, fetal growth restriction, and low birth weight dominate the morbidity.

## Recommendations and Grades
The chapter states no formal evidence grades; recommendations are narrative.
- Defer conception at least 1 year after transplant with adequate contraception.
- Before pregnancy obtain a recent liver biopsy, 24-hour urine total protein, HBsAg and hepatitis C antibody, and CMV, toxoplasma, and herpes simplex IgG and IgM.
- Continue maintenance immunosuppression with serum level monitoring; never reduce it to prevent rejection.
- Avoid mycophenolic acid (MPA) products when feasible and discuss fetal risk.
- Baseline ALT, AST, creatinine, bilirubin, drug level, 24-hour urinary protein and creatinine clearance, urinalysis and culture, and serologies; repeat the first five each trimester until 32 weeks.
- Screen for gestational diabetes in the first trimester and at 24-28 weeks. Start weekly nonstress tests at 32 weeks.
- Plan vaginal delivery; cesarean delivery only for obstetric indications.
- Give hydrocortisone 100 mg IV every 8 hours for 24 hours if the woman received more than 20 mg prednisone daily for more than 3 weeks antepartum.
- Monitor drug levels for at least 1 month postpartum. Recommend an intrauterine device; oral contraceptives are relatively contraindicated.
- Vaccinate before transplant or pregnancy against influenza, pneumococcus, hepatitis B, and tetanus, and confirm rubella immunity.
- Give hepatitis B vaccine and immunoglobulin to newborns of HBsAg-positive mothers within 12 hours of birth.

## Key Concepts
- Definition: end-stage liver disease threatens survival or quality of life and transplant is the only therapy. Nonalcoholic steatohepatitis is the leading indication in women; autoimmune hepatitis leads in women of childbearing age.
- Epidemiology: 35.5% of LTx recipients are women and about 75% of female recipients are of reproductive age; more than 14,000 US women of reproductive age live after LTx, with about 500 transplants yearly.
- Fertility: infertility affects up to 50% with end-stage liver disease; 70-95% regain normal cycles within 1 year. Shortest reported transplant-to-conception interval is 3 weeks.
- Rejection: 2-15%, in early pregnancy. Exclude anatomic graft dysfunction with duplex ultrasound, then biopsy; biopsy is not contraindicated in pregnancy. Treat by adjusting immunosuppression, high-dose steroids, and thymoglobulin if steroid resistant.
- Preterm birth: up to 50%, mean gestational age 36-37 weeks (39% LTx, 51% kidney). Low birth weight: 30% LTx, 41% kidney. Growth restriction: 19.2% LTx, 31.3% renal.
- Pre-eclampsia: about 20% in LTx, mainly with cyclosporine; registry rates 24% liver, 31% kidney, 42% kidney-pancreas, 27% heart, 15% lung.
- Aggregate 1,203 LTx pregnancies: 76.7% live births, 7.95% spontaneous abortion, 30.8% preterm, 12.37% graft dysfunction, 47.76% cesarean, 25.7% below 2,500 g, 4.3% maternal and 3.1% neonatal deaths.
- CMV reactivation peaks 6-12 months after transplant and causes congenital malformation or liver disease in 10-15% of infected pregnancies. Genitourinary infection 5.3% versus 1.4%; anemia 23% versus 9.5%.
- Vertical hepatitis B transmission without prophylaxis is 10-20% from HBsAg-positive, HBeAg-negative mothers; vaccine plus immunoglobulin within 12 hours lowers neonatal infection below 10%. Hepatitis C transmission is 5.8% in HIV-negative and 10.8% in HIV-positive women.
- FDA categories: corticosteroids B; cyclosporine, sirolimus, tacrolimus C; azathioprine D; mycophenolate mofetil D (changed from C in 2007). Use two contraceptive methods from 4 weeks before MPA to 12 weeks after stopping.
- Azathioprine causes neonatal lymphopenia, hypogammaglobulinemia, and thymic hypoplasia that regress after birth; cyclosporine carries moderate growth-restriction risk; sirolimus data are three pregnancies with two live births. Breastfeeding is compatible with cyclosporine, azathioprine, glucocorticoids, and tacrolimus, which was undetectable in neonates.
- Other organs: heart 67% live births and 27% pre-eclampsia; lung 59% live births and 56% preterm with the most peripartum rejection; intestinal transplant needs endoscopic biopsy surveillance; uterine transplant produced a live birth in Sweden in 2013.

## Anti-patterns
- Do not stop or reduce immunosuppression in pregnancy; this precipitates rejection.
- Do not use oral contraceptives first line; thromboembolism, cholestasis, worsened hypertension, and cyclosporine interaction are the reasons given.
- Do not prescribe mycophenolate mofetil or enteric-coated mycophenolate sodium when an alternative exists.
- Do not attribute elevated liver enzymes or bilirubin to pregnancy alone; exclude rejection and bile duct obstruction.
- Do not withhold liver biopsy in pregnancy when rejection is suspected.
- Do not ignore an infra-renal aortic graft; uterine compression has caused fatal graft thrombosis.

## Key Takeaways
- Delay pregnancy until at least 1 year after transplant with stable graft function and immunosuppression.
- Check proteinuria, creatinine, liver enzymes, drug levels, and serologies before conception and each trimester to 32 weeks.
- Keep immunosuppression unchanged except MPA products, and monitor levels for at least 1 month postpartum.
- Start weekly nonstress tests at 32 weeks and screen for gestational diabetes twice.
- Expect preterm birth, pre-eclampsia, growth restriction, or low birth weight in roughly one third of pregnancies.
- Give vaccine plus immunoglobulin within 12 hours to newborns of HBsAg-positive mothers.
