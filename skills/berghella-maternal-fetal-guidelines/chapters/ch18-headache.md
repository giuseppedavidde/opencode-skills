# Chapter 18: Headache

## Core Idea
Most headache in pregnancy is migraine or tension-type headache, and most women with migraine improve in the second and third trimesters, with recurrence usually within 3-6 days postpartum. New-onset headache, or headache with red flags, demands full neurologic evaluation because subarachnoid hemorrhage, stroke, cerebral venous thrombosis, pituitary apoplexy, and pre-eclampsia with reversible cerebral vasoconstriction occur more often in pregnancy. Acetaminophen is the first-line acute drug in every trimester, and nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs must stop at 28 weeks. Propranolol or metoprolol is the preventive drug of choice.

## Recommendations and Grades
The chapter states no formal evidence grades; it reports US Food and Drug Administration pregnancy categories instead.
- Exclude secondary causes before treating a primary headache disorder.
- Image with computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging without contrast when an ominous cause is suspected; both are safe in pregnancy. Avoid gadolinium unless a bleed, mass, or meningitis is suspected, when benefit outweighs risk.
- Perform lumbar puncture only after computed tomography excludes a mass or edema.
- Use acetaminophen first line for acute headache in all trimesters (category B), alone or with codeine for refractory headache.
- Use parenteral metoclopramide (B), promethazine (C), or prochlorperazine (C) for severe or unrelenting migraine; these also treat nausea.
- Use ibuprofen or naproxen (category B before 28 weeks) only up to 28 weeks; all nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs become category D in the third trimester and are not recommended.
- Avoid ergotamine and dihydroergotamine (category X); they are abortifacients and cause fetal distress and birth defects.
- Do not start triptans in pregnancy (category C); reassure a woman who took sumatriptan before knowing she was pregnant, because registry data show no substantial increase in major birth defects.
- Avoid calcitonin gene-related peptide monoclonal antibodies and gepants such as ubrogepant and rimegepant; pregnancy safety data are lacking and half-lives are long.
- Use propranolol or metoprolol as first-line prophylaxis; verapamil may also help. Avoid valproic acid, and restrict topiramate and gabapentin.
- Use amitriptyline 10-25 mg daily (category C) when needed and taper before delivery to reduce neonatal sedation.
- Consider magnesium oxide 350-400 mg of elemental magnesium daily, and peripheral nerve blocks with lidocaine as low-risk options.
- Give prednisone or dexamethasone only in short 3-6 day courses for intractable migraine.
- Start non-pharmacologic measures in everyone: avoid food, caffeine, and alcohol triggers, do not skip meals, optimize sleep and exercise, and use yoga, meditation, relaxation training, thermal biofeedback, or cognitive behavioral therapy.

## Key Concepts
- Epidemiology: one-year migraine prevalence is 18% in US women and 6% in men, with a peak between ages 20 and 50; nearly half of sufferers remain undiagnosed. About 40% of women with headache have episodic or chronic tension-type headache. Migraine affects up to 18% of pregnant women, most already diagnosed before pregnancy.
- Migraine without aura (International Headache Society): at least five attacks of 4-72 hours with at least two of unilateral location, pulsating quality, moderate or severe intensity, and aggravation by routine activity, plus nausea or vomiting, or photophobia and phonophobia.
- Migraine with aura: at least two attacks with fully reversible visual, sensory, speech, motor, brainstem, or retinal symptoms that spread over at least 5 minutes, last 5-60 minutes each, are unilateral, and are followed within 60 minutes by headache.
- Tension-type headache: at least 10 episodes on fewer than 1 day per month, lasting 30 minutes to 7 days, bilateral, pressing, mild to moderate, not aggravated by activity, without nausea, and with no more than one of photophobia or phonophobia.
- Aura mechanism: cortical spreading depression travels at 2-3 mm/min. Headache arises from trigeminal activation with release of substance P and calcitonin gene-related peptide, peripheral sensitization, and central sensitization of the trigeminal nucleus caudalis.
- Red flags: thunderclap onset; HIV or systemic cancer; fever, weight loss, meningeal signs, or papilledema; focal signs such as confusion, impaired alertness, or incoordination; a new, different, or progressively worsening headache; and positional headache relieved by recumbency, which suggests cerebrospinal fluid leak.
- Pregnancy risks of migraine: low birth weight with preterm birth, pre-eclampsia, and cesarean delivery are increased. Estrogen rises pain thresholds, and estrogen withdrawal at menses triggers menstrual migraine.
- Acute migraine trial: in women refractory to oral acetaminophen, intravenous metoclopramide plus diphenhydramine relieved headache better than oral codeine at 30 minutes, 1 hour, and 12 hours.
- Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs: first-trimester miscarriage data are inconsistent, and third-trimester risks are premature ductus arteriosus closure, neonatal pulmonary hypertension, and oligohydramnios. Limit use to the second trimester.
- Caffeine is category C; one cup of drip coffee contains about 100 mg, and up to 200 mg daily is considered low risk.
- Opioids: meperidine is category B, codeine and morphine are category C. Chronic use in late pregnancy causes neonatal withdrawal, so limit to short courses.
- Steroids: chronic exposure risks fetal adrenal suppression, so limit to 3-6 days.
- Preventive drugs: valproic acid causes neural tube defects; topiramate and gabapentin are restricted for fetal defect concerns; fluoxetine (category B) can treat coexisting depression as monotherapy.

## Anti-patterns
- Do not attribute a new or thunderclap headache in pregnancy to migraine without imaging when a secondary cause is possible.
- Do not use nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs after 28 weeks; the ductus arteriosus and fetal kidneys are at risk.
- Do not use ergots at any gestational age; they are category X.
- Do not start triptans, gepants, or calcitonin gene-related peptide antibodies in pregnancy.
- Do not use valproic acid for prophylaxis; it causes neural tube defects.
- Do not order gadolinium routinely; it crosses the placenta.

## Key Takeaways
- Treat acute headache with acetaminophen, adding codeine if refractory, and use parenteral antiemetics for severe migraine.
- Stop nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs at 28 weeks and never use ergots.
- Image suspected secondary headache with non-contrast computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging.
- Choose propranolol or metoprolol for prophylaxis, with magnesium or non-drug therapy as adjuncts.
- Expect improvement in the second and third trimesters and recurrence within 3-6 days postpartum.
