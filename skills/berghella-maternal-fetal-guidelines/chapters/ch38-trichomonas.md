# Chapter 38: Trichomonas

## Core Idea
*Trichomonas vaginalis* colonization in the second trimester is associated with a 30–40% higher risk of low birth weight or preterm delivery, yet the only trial that treated asymptomatic women (two 2-g metronidazole doses 48 hours apart at 16–23 weeks, twice the usual dose) found an 80% *increase* in preterm birth. The chapter therefore does not recommend screening asymptomatic pregnant women, and states that treatment of asymptomatic women may be harmful. Diagnosis in pregnancy is by PCR or NAAT from vaginal secretions, which is the gold standard. Symptomatic infection is treated with metronidazole, 2 g orally as a single dose or 500 mg twice daily for 7 days, at any gestational age. Partner treatment prevents reinfection.

## Recommendations and Grades
- Do not screen asymptomatic pregnant women for *T. vaginalis*. The book states there is insufficient evidence to recommend screening, and some evidence that treatment may be harmful (no formal grade assigned; based on the RCT by Klebanoff et al. [I] and a Cochrane meta-analysis of two RCTs, n = 842 [II-1]).
- Screen all women with HIV for trichomoniasis, because co-infection rates reach 53% and treatment reduces genital HIV shedding. Source: CDC STD treatment guidelines.
- Diagnose by PCR or NAAT of vaginal secretions; these are the gold standard, with sensitivity and specificity as high as 95.3–99% and 95.2–99% respectively [II-2].
- Treat symptomatic infection with metronidazole 2 g orally as a single dose, or 500 mg twice daily for 7 days, at any gestational age. Oral nitroimidazoles achieve parasitologic cure in 90–95% in randomized trials.
- Prefer 500 mg twice daily for 7 days when HIV co-infection is present; the 7-day regimen is more effective than single-dose therapy in all patients (RR 0.55, 95% CI 0.34–0.70; p < 0.0001) [II-1].
- Rescreen for test of cure within 3 months of treatment; PCR can detect persistent infection as early as 2 weeks.
- Offer concurrent treatment of sexual partners or referral for treatment; partner treatment decreases reinfection [RCT, n = 484, II-1].
- Desensitize the rare patient with confirmed metronidazole allergy before treatment [Level II-3].

## Key Concepts
- Global incidence 240 million new cases annually; prevalence about 156 million (5.3%), up from 5.0% in 2012. United States: approximately 3.7 million non-pregnant and 80,000 pregnant women affected annually. WHO estimates 78 million new infections annually in Africa.
- Up to 50% of infected women are asymptomatic. Symptoms: vulvovaginal pruritus 7–11%, vaginal discharge 10–17%, dysuria 30–50%, dyspareunia 10–50%. Signs: copious discharge 50–75% (yellow/green 5–20%, frothy 10–50%), vaginal mucosal inflammation 40–75%, vulvar erythema 10–20%, abdominal pain 1–5%.
- Incubation 4–7 days on average, range 2–28 days. Male-to-female transmission during vaginal intercourse 66–100%. Vertical transmission to a female infant 2–17% when vaginal infection is present at delivery.
- Second-trimester colonization: 30–40% higher risk of low birth weight or preterm birth; 40% higher risk of an infant who is both preterm and low birth weight. Meta-analysis relative risks: preterm birth 1.42, PPROM 1.41, SGA 1.51.
- The trial that showed harm used two 2-g doses 48 hours apart at 16–23 weeks. A retrospective cohort of 4274 treated women found no increase, and possible protection (HR 0.69, 95% CI 0.52–0.92, p = 0.010) [II-2].
- HIV: infection roughly doubles the rate of HIV-1 transmission, through local CD4+ lymphocyte infiltration and mucosal disruption.
- Wet mount: sensitivity 62–80%, specificity >99%; sensitivity falls if the slide is read more than 1 hour after collection. Culture: sensitivity 95%, specificity 100%, 3–7 days. Modified Diamond's broth 95%, InPouch 87%, modified Columbia agar 98%.
- Pap smear is not accurate: sensitivity 60–70%, specificity 88% for conventional; liquid-based Pap sensitivity 61.4% and warrants treatment without further testing.
- In pregnancy, do not give more than a single 2-g metronidazole dose, to avoid a possible increase in preterm birth; an escalated regimen of 2 g daily for 3–5 days is reserved for resistant infection outside this constraint.
- Metronidazole resistance affects about 5% of clinical isolates (CDC estimate). Tinidazole cures up to 60% of metronidazole-resistant infections. Tinidazole is contraindicated in the first trimester.
- Metronidazole has not been associated with teratogenic or mutagenic effects in infants across multiple studies and meta-analyses.

## Anti-patterns
- Screening and treating asymptomatic pregnant women: no demonstrated benefit, and the Klebanoff RCT showed more preterm birth with the intensive metronidazole regimen.
- Using wet mount alone in a high-risk patient with a negative result: sensitivity is only 62–80%; culture or NAAT is needed.
- Relying on a Pap smear report of trichomonads without confirmatory testing (conventional Pap sensitivity 60–70%).
- Treating with tinidazole in the first trimester: contraindicated.
- Routine expedited partner therapy: it has not been shown in randomized trials to reduce reinfection compared with partner referral, and no clear evidence-based recommendation exists.
- Omitting test of cure: persistent infection is common in pregnancy.

## Key Takeaways
1. Treat symptoms, not asymptomatic colonization.
2. Use NAAT or PCR to diagnose; wet mount misses 20–38% of infections.
3. Metronidazole 2 g once, or 500 mg twice daily for 7 days (preferred if HIV-positive), at any gestational age.
4. Screen and treat all HIV-infected pregnant women.
5. Recheck a test of cure within 3 months, as early as 2 weeks by PCR.
6. Treat the partner to prevent reinfection.
