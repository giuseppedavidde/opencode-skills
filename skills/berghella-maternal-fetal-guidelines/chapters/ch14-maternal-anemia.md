# Chapter 14: Maternal Anemia

## Core Idea
Anemia in pregnancy is defined as hemoglobin (Hgb) below 11 g/dL and hematocrit (Hct) below 33% in the first or third trimester, and below 10.5 g/dL and 32% in the second trimester. Iron deficiency is the most common cause, and ferritin is the most sensitive and specific test for it. Universal screening with complete blood count and mean corpuscular volume (MCV) is recommended, with hemoglobin electrophoresis for all women of African ancestry. Severe anemia below 7 g/dL is associated with maternal death and poor perinatal outcome, so treatment thresholds matter more than the numeric definition.

## Recommendations and Grades
The chapter states no formal evidence grades; recommendations are narrative.
- Screen all pregnant women with complete blood count, Hgb, Hct, and MCV, plus ferritin.
- Offer hemoglobin electrophoresis to all women who identify as having African ancestry; screen women of Mediterranean and Southeast Asian descent with CBC and MCV and pursue further workup if abnormal.
- Diagnose microcytic anemia when Hgb is below 10.5-11 g/dL and MCV is below 80 fL; obtain ferritin and hemoglobin electrophoresis.
- Diagnose normocytic anemia when Hgb is 10.5-11 g/dL and MCV is 80-100 fL; obtain reticulocyte count, ferritin, vitamin B12, and red cell folate.
- Diagnose macrocytic anemia when Hgb is 10.5-11 g/dL and MCV is above 100 fL; obtain vitamin B12 and red cell folate.
- Treat iron deficiency anemia with ferrous sulfate 325 mg orally daily plus prenatal vitamin and 250 mg vitamin C, and recheck Hct in 4 weeks.
- Offer every-other-day dosing if oral iron is poorly tolerated; intermittent regimens give similar outcomes with fewer side effects.
- Consider intravenous iron when Hgb is below 9 g/dL, when oral iron is not tolerated or not effective, or in the third trimester.
- Obtain a genetic consult for inherited disorders and a nutrition consult for iron, B12, or folate deficiency.
- Consider growth ultrasound in the third trimester because of the association with growth restriction.

## Key Concepts
- Ferritin cutoffs: below 15 µg/L is the Institute of Medicine and CDC definition of iron deficiency anemia; below 30 µg/L improves sensitivity and specificity. Low is 15-30 ng/mL, low-normal 30-40 ng/mL, normal or high above 40 ng/mL, and transferrin saturation below 20% supports iron deficiency.
- African-American women: lower Hgb and Hct cutoffs by 0.8 g/dL and 2% respectively.
- Prevalence: 38-42% of pregnant women worldwide are anemic, from 22% in high-resource areas to 56% in Africa. In the United States 5% are anemic and 18% are iron deficient, rising from 7% in the first trimester to 28% in the third. Iron deficiency anemia affects 30% of African-American, 24% of Mexican-American, and 14% of white women.
- Physiology: plasma volume rises 40-60% while red cell mass rises 15-30%, so normal pregnancy Hgb is 11-12 g/dL and Hct 32-33%.
- High Hgb is not reassuring: CDC reports that Hgb above 15.0 g/dL or Hct above 45.0%, especially in the second trimester, predicts growth restriction, preterm birth, and fetal death because it reflects poor plasma volume expansion.
- Iron balance: pregnancy and lactation cost about 1 g of iron. The usual diet supplies 15 mg of elemental iron daily; the recommended intake is 27 mg in pregnancy and 10 mg during lactation.
- Severe anemia: Hgb below 7 g/dL is associated with cardiovascular compromise and maternal death, adjusted odds ratio 2.36 (95% CI 1.6-3.48). Symptoms usually appear only below 6-7 g/dL. First-trimester anemia is more consistently linked to adverse outcome than third-trimester anemia.
- Laboratory thresholds: reticulocyte count 3% separates underproduction from hemolysis or blood loss; RDW normal is 12-15% and elevated is above 15%; B12 deficiency is below 100 pg/mL; red cell folate deficiency is below 150 ng/mL; MCV below 70 fL suggests thalassemia; HbA2 above 3.5% indicates beta thalassemia.
- Trimester-specific ferritin ranges (2.5th-97.5th percentile): 6-130 ng/mL first, 2-230 ng/mL second, 0-116 ng/mL third.
- Screening policy differs: the American Society of Hematology advises universal ferritin, ACOG and the British Society for Haematology only with risk factors, and the USPSTF only when anemia is present.
- Oral iron elemental content: ferrous sulfate 65 mg per 325 mg tablet, ferrous fumarate 106 mg per 325 mg, ferrous gluconate 34 mg per 300 mg; preparations include iron dextran 50 mg/mL, ferric gluconate 12.5 mg/mL, iron sucrose 20 mg/mL, and ferric carboxymaltose 750 mg with a 1,500 mg maximum.
- Intravenous iron gives higher Hgb and ferritin, better compliance and quality of life, and higher birth weight than oral iron. Ferric carboxymaltose 750 mg over 15 minutes raises Hgb and ferritin more than iron sucrose with comparable safety; alternatively give 1,000 mg of low molecular weight iron dextran.
- Folate supplementation reduces megaloblastic anemia and raises mean birth weight but does not change preterm birth or stillbirth. B12 replacement is 1 mg intramuscularly weekly for 8 weeks, then monthly; folate is 1 mg daily with the prenatal vitamin.
- Postpartum: evidence is insufficient to show that transfusion improves outcomes over the risk of immunological sensitization; erythropoietin gives only small hematologic gains.

## Anti-patterns
- Do not use solubility testing for hemoglobinopathy screening; it fails to identify other important hemoglobinopathies.
- Do not repeat a documented prior hemoglobin electrophoresis.
- Do not give oral iron with meals or antacids; both reduce absorption.
- Do not accept a high hemoglobin as proof of adequate iron stores; it may signal failed volume expansion.
- Do not assume a single cause; algorithms fail when more than one cause of anemia coexists.
- Do not treat presumed iron deficiency without considering gastrointestinal blood loss and other causes.

## Key Takeaways
- Use trimester-specific cutoffs and lower them by 0.8 g/dL and 2% for African-American women.
- Check ferritin first in microcytic anemia and add hemoglobin electrophoresis for African, Mediterranean, or Southeast Asian ancestry.
- Start ferrous sulfate 325 mg daily with 250 mg vitamin C and recheck Hct in 4 weeks; switch to alternate-day dosing or IV iron if response or tolerance fails.
- Move to intravenous iron when Hgb is below 9 g/dL or in the third trimester with new iron deficiency.
- Treat Hgb below 7 g/dL as severe disease with maternal mortality risk.
- Expect nearly one third of women to be iron deficient by the third trimester.
