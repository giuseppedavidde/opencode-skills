# Chapter 42: Critical Care

## Core Idea
Fewer than 1% of maternity admissions in the developed world require intensive care, but up to 5% require intermediate or high-dependency care, and most maternal ICU admissions are postpartum. Antepartum ICU admission carries a high rate of preterm birth. Critical care recommendations are largely extrapolated from non-pregnant adults because pregnant women are excluded from most trials; the chapter explicitly notes that no randomized trial of sepsis management or lung-protective ventilation exists in pregnancy. Two principles dominate: delayed recognition and treatment of sepsis increases mortality, and delivery does not improve maternal survival in ARDS — neonatal survival depends on gestational age, which argues for continuing pregnancy when maternal condition permits.

## Recommendations and Grades
- Use a low-tidal-volume ventilation strategy (6 mL/kg predicted body weight) for ARDS. Source: ARDSNet multicenter RCT in non-pregnant adults; the strategy has not been formally tested in pregnancy.
- Maintain maternal SpO2 >95% or PaO2 >60 mmHg in ARDS in an effort to promote fetal well-being. The chapter states it is unclear what evidence supports this recommendation.
- Monitor the fetal heart rate continuously once viability is reached in a woman ventilated with a low-tidal-volume strategy, and treat a suspicious tracing by increasing minute ventilation (frequency or tidal volume) or switching to airway pressure release ventilation. This is a suggestion, not a graded recommendation.
- Do not deliver solely for ARDS: delivery does not improve maternal survival, though it may reduce maternal oxygen requirement, increase total respiratory system compliance, and facilitate prone positioning.
- Treat sepsis and septic shock as medical emergencies; begin resuscitation immediately. Give at least 30 mL/kg IV crystalloid within the first 3 hours for sepsis-induced hypoperfusion. Source: Surviving Sepsis Campaign 2016 [extrapolated; no pregnancy-specific evidence].
- Obtain two sets of blood cultures (aerobic and anaerobic) before antibiotics, provided this does not delay therapy. Give empiric broad-spectrum IV antibiotics within 1 hour for sepsis and septic shock; reassess daily and de-escalate; usual duration 7–10 days.
- Identify and control the anatomic source as soon as medically and logistically practical, using the intervention with the least physiologic derangement (percutaneous rather than surgical drainage).
- Use norepinephrine as the first-line vasopressor, targeting MAP ≥65 mmHg. Note that MAP is normally 4–5 mmHg lower in pregnancy and that a target of 60 mmHg cannot be extrapolated, because the uteroplacental circulation does not autoregulate.
- Consider IV hydrocortisone 200 mg/day only if fluid and vasopressor therapy fail to restore hemodynamic stability.
- Transfuse red cells only when hemoglobin is <7.0 g/dL; give platelets when the count is <10,000/mm³, or <20,000/mm³ with high bleeding risk.
- Give pharmacologic VTE prophylaxis with unfractionated or low-molecular-weight heparin when not contraindicated; use mechanical prophylaxis when pharmacologic prophylaxis is contraindicated.
- Give stress ulcer prophylaxis with a proton pump inhibitor or histamine-2 receptor antagonist to patients at risk of gastrointestinal bleeding; prefer oral or enteral feeding over parenteral nutrition or fasting; maintain glucose <180 mg/dL.
- Discuss prognosis and goals of care with the patient and family as soon as feasible, including end-of-life planning.

## Key Concepts
- ICU admission: 1–8 per 1000 obstetric admissions; mortality among these patients 2–11%. A further 1–5% of women admitted for delivery need intermediate or high-dependency care.
- Severe maternal morbidity in the United States rose from 101 to 147 per 10,000 delivery hospitalizations between 2006 and 2015 — more than 58,000 episodes per year. Compared with White women, severe morbidity is 110% more likely in Black women, 40% more in Hispanic women, and 20% more in Asian/Pacific Islander women; Black women are three times more likely to die from childbirth. More than half of pregnancy-related mortality is potentially preventable.
- ARDS Berlin definition (2012): onset within 1 week of a known inciting event or acute worsening of respiratory symptoms, bilateral opacities on chest radiograph or CT, and exclusion of cardiac failure or fluid overload (echocardiogram should be considered). Severity by PaO2/FiO2 with PEEP or CPAP ≥5 cm H2O: mild 200–300 mmHg, moderate 100–200 mmHg, severe ≤100 mmHg.
- ARDS incidence in pregnancy quoted from case series as 1/3000 to 1/6000 deliveries; United States 2008–2009 data show 6 delivery and 3 postpartum hospitalizations per 10,000 deliveries, up from 3.6 and 1.1 in 1998–1999. ARDS requiring mechanical ventilation rose from 36.5 to 59.6 per 100,000 live births between 2006 and 2012, with 9% mortality.
- Obstetric ARDS mortality: 24–44% in older series, 33% in a recent series, versus 38% in the general population; Canadian data show 6% (1991–2002) and <3% (2003–2007) in women without major pre-existing disease; a United States series found 9–14%. Mortality is higher with renal or hepatic failure, amniotic fluid embolism, influenza, and puerperal infection.
- H1N1 influenza: ARDS in 9.7% of pregnant versus 5.4% of non-pregnant women; ECMO required in 45% of pregnant or postpartum women with severe disease in Australia and New Zealand. ECMO mortality in pregnancy fell from about 54% to about 27% (ELSO registry 1997–2017, n = 280).
- Mechanical ventilation in pregnancy: aspiration risk is increased by the gravid uterus and progesterone-relaxed gastroesophageal sphincter; desaturation is rapid because functional residual capacity is reduced and oxygen demand is increased; airway edema and large breasts make intubation difficult.
- Pregnancy arterial blood gases: pH 7.43, PaCO2 26–30 mmHg, HCO3 17 mEq/L, PaO2 101–106 mmHg in the third trimester. A PaCO2 within the non-pregnant normal range is abnormal in pregnancy.
- Barotrauma occurred in 36–44% of obstetric patients ventilated with traditional tidal volumes, versus 11% in non-obstetric ARDS patients; one obstetric series used a mean tidal volume of 7.7 mL/kg predicted body weight.
- Permissive hypercapnia is a concern in pregnancy: CO2 transfer to the fetus depends on a maternal-fetal PCO2 gradient. In a CO2 rebreathing study of 35 healthy pregnant women, end-tidal CO2 up to 60 torr caused loss of fetal heart rate variability in 57%, and 90% normalized after the test.
- Sepsis is "life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection" (Sepsis-3, 2016). SIRS and "severe sepsis" categories are obsolete. Septic shock: vasopressors needed to keep MAP ≥65 mmHg and lactate >2 mmol/L despite adequate fluids.
- SOFA: a 2-point rise from baseline is positive; unknown baseline is assumed zero. qSOFA (respiratory rate ≥22/min, altered mentation, systolic BP ≤100 mmHg) scores 2 or 3 as abnormal. Neither is validated in pregnancy; SOMANZ produced obstetrically modified SOFA and qSOFA tools.
- Obstetric sepsis accounts for 10.7% of maternal deaths globally — the third most common cause. UKOSS 2011–2012 sources: genital tract 20%, urinary 34%, respiratory 9%, unidentified 30%. GLOSS: 28% urinary, 30% uterine, 15% skin/soft tissue, 9% respiratory.
- Missed sepsis: in Michigan maternal deaths 1999–2006, only 18% were febrile at presentation and 25% never became febrile. Antibiotics were delayed in 73% of sepsis deaths and escalation of care in 53%; United Kingdom data found delay in recognition or management in 70%, two-thirds within the obstetric unit.
- Fluid resuscitation in pregnancy: 30 mL/kg may be overly aggressive, because colloid oncotic pressure is lower and pulmonary edema risk higher. The Society for Maternal-Fetal Medicine recommends 1–2 L crystalloid first, then dynamic measures (passive leg raising, pulse-pressure variation, point-of-care ultrasound).
- If colloid is used in pregnancy, use albumin rather than hydroxyethyl starch, which is associated with harm.
- Norepinephrine has been studied in pregnancy for spinal-induced hypotension during cesarean: equal blood pressure control, more favorable chronotropic profile, higher cardiac output, no difference in Apgar scores or cord gases, and lower cord epinephrine than phenylephrine.
- Bedside cesarean in the ICU is hazardous: inadequate space, lighting, and neonatal equipment; unfamiliar staff; overcrowding; and the highest hospital rates of resistant health-care-associated infection. Map a route to the operating room for every obstetric ICU patient. For perimortem cesarean, these concerns are ignored, because the goal is return of spontaneous circulation.
- Fetal heart rate tracing in ICU reflects maternal end-organ (uteroplacental) perfusion and maternal acid-base status; loss of variability or decelerations should prompt a search for maternal hypotension, acidemia, or inferior vena cava compression.
- Tocolysis is ill-advised in critical illness: efficacy is equivocal, preterm labor may be adaptive, and all tocolytics have side effects. Keep a low threshold for antenatal corticosteroids, and consider substituting betamethasone or dexamethasone for hydrocortisone when steroids are given for another indication.
- Post-intensive care syndrome includes ICU-acquired weakness, cognitive impairment, and psychological distress, and can persist for years.

## Anti-patterns
- Delivering a woman to treat ARDS: it does not improve maternal survival.
- Targeting a PaCO2 in the normal non-pregnant range, or accepting marked permissive hypercapnia without fetal monitoring: fetal acidemia risk.
- Assuming a normal temperature or white count excludes sepsis: most deaths were afebrile at presentation.
- Giving 30 mL/kg fluid without reassessment in preeclampsia or sepsis: high risk of pulmonary edema; use dynamic measures after 1–2 L.
- Using hydroxyethyl starch for colloid resuscitation: evidence of harm.
- Withholding antibiotics to obtain cultures: cultures must never delay treatment.
- Attempting tocolysis in a critically ill pregnant patient.
- Routinely performing cesarean in the ICU when the patient can be stabilized and moved: reserve bedside delivery for perimortem cesarean.
- Neglecting goals-of-care and palliative discussion: the Surviving Sepsis Campaign calls for it as soon as feasible.

## Key Takeaways
1. Maternal stabilization first; fetal monitoring is an adjunct and a maternal vital sign.
2. Sepsis: cultures then antibiotics within 1 hour, 30 mL/kg crystalloid (or 1–2 L then dynamic assessment in pregnancy), norepinephrine to MAP ≥65 mmHg, and early source control.
3. ARDS: 6 mL/kg predicted body weight, PEEP ≥5 cm H2O, SpO2 >95%, and continuous fetal monitoring once viable.
4. Do not deliver for ARDS; deliver for obstetric or maternal indications.
5. Transfuse at hemoglobin <7.0 g/dL; give VTE prophylaxis, stress ulcer prophylaxis, enteral feeding, and keep glucose <180 mg/dL.
6. Anticipate preterm birth in ICU patients and give antenatal corticosteroids liberally.
