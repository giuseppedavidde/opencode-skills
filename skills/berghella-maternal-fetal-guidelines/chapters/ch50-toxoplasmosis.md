# Chapter 50: Toxoplasmosis

## Core Idea
Maternal infection with *Toxoplasma gondii* begins with ingestion of tissue cysts in undercooked meat or oocysts from cat feces, soil, unwashed produce, or water. Fetal disease is most severe when infection occurs in the first trimester, while the transmission rate rises steeply with gestational age (10–25% in the first, 30–54% in the second, 60–65% in the third trimester). Diagnosis rests on reference-laboratory serology and amniotic fluid PCR. Prevention by education remains the most important intervention, and treatment is now moving from spiramycin prophylaxis toward pyrimethamine plus sulfadiazine after the first trimester.

## Recommendations and Grades
- Do not introduce routine prenatal or neonatal screening where prevalence is low (United States, United Kingdom): the text cites low incidence, diagnostic difficulty, service availability, compliance, and false-positive results. In France and Austria screening is established, starting preconception or in the first trimester and repeated every month, bimonthly, or each trimester in IgG-negative women. Grade: not formally graded.
- Do not screen for toxoplasmosis in the presence of fetal growth restriction without other risk factors: the Society for Maternal-Fetal Medicine "currently recommends against serologic screening when there are no other risk factors or findings" [Review, III].
- Confirm maternal infection in a reference laboratory, using two serum specimens collected at least 4 weeks apart. Correct interpretation "decreases unnecessary anxiety and even terminations" [II-2].
- Perform amniocentesis after 18 weeks and no sooner than 4 weeks after primary maternal infection. Grade: not stated.
- Start spiramycin 3–4 g/day (1 g every 8 hours) when maternal infection is confirmed by a reference laboratory; spiramycin concentrates in the placenta. Grade: not stated.
- If AF PCR is positive, treat with sulfadiazine plus pyrimethamine plus folinic acid. A recent RCT favors pyrimethamine plus sulfadiazine over spiramycin and is the current recommendation, especially when started promptly. Grade as stated: RCT data; "larger studies are still needed."
- Do not use pyrimethamine in the first trimester: it is teratogenic. Use sulfadiazine alone in that period. Grade: not stated.
- Delivery: mode should not be influenced by maternal infection; cesarean delivery does not reduce vertical transmission.

## Key Concepts
- Incidence of primary maternal infection: 0.01–0.1% in the United States and United Kingdom. Seroprevalence of past infection: about 22% in the United States, up to 44% in France/Europe, 50–70% in Latin America, 5–35% in Asia, China, and Korea.
- Congenital infection: about 1.5 cases per 1000 live births worldwide; 0.5–1.5 per 1000 in France/Europe; 0.7 per 1000 in the United States.
- Transmission by timing: preconception 1%, first trimester 10–25%, second trimester 30–54%, third trimester 60–65%. Overall vertical transmission 20–50%, up to 65% in the third trimester. Congenital toxoplasmosis affects about 7% of offspring of women with primary infection in pregnancy. Risk is <1/1000 if infection occurs before 4 weeks.
- Of PCR-positive fetuses, 74–81% have subclinical infection and 19–26% develop fetal or childhood illness even when treated; newer Austrian register data report <10% with treatment.
- Complications: ventriculomegaly, increased placental thickness, hepatomegaly, ascites, intracranial calcifications, hydrocephalus, microcephaly, hepatosplenomegaly; neonatal chorioretinitis is the most prevalent consequence. Associated adverse outcomes: preterm birth OR 3.49 (95% CI 1.91–6.37), abortion OR 6.63 (4.56–9.65), stillbirth OR 4.63 (2.72–7.90), FGR OR 4.49 (2.10–9.57).
- Serology: IgG appears within 2 weeks and persists for life; IgM appears within 2 weeks and often persists 1–2 years; only 22–40% of positive IgM results from non-reference laboratories represent recent infection. IgA may persist more than 1 year; IgE disappears within 4 months. The Sabin–Feldman dye test is the gold standard. Titres >250 IU/mL are highly suggestive of recent infection.
- IgG avidity: high avidity indicates infection at least 3–5 months earlier. An avidity index above 30% was not associated with any positive AF test or congenital infection in a cohort of 139 women.
- High probability of infection at the reference laboratory: seroconversion in pregnancy; a >3-fold rise in both specific IgG and dye test titers; or specific IgM with dye test ≥300 IU/mL.
- AF PCR: specificity and positive predictive value close to 100%; sensitivity about 70–80%, best when maternal infection occurs at 17–21 weeks. Real-time PCR: sensitivity 92%, negative predictive value 98%.
- Treatment doses: spiramycin 3–4 g/day (1 g every 8 hours), or 3–4 g/day for 3–4 weeks in some protocols. Sulfadiazine 75 mg/kg initial dose, then 50 mg/kg every 12 hours, maximum 4 g/day. Pyrimethamine 50 mg every 12 hours for 2 days, then 50 mg daily. Folinic acid (leucovorin) 10–20 mg with each pyrimethamine dose. Check blood count every 1–2 weeks for neutropenia (neutrophils <1500/mm³). Stop treatment in the last few weeks of pregnancy.
- Prevention (Table 50.2): avoid raw or undercooked meat or eggs; wear gloves for soil contact; wash fruit and vegetables; avoid changing cat litter and wash hands after handling cats; keep cats indoors and feed commercial pet food.
- Treatment effect: meta-analysis of 7055 women — pooled congenital infection rate 16%; vertical transmission similar with spiramycin alone and spiramycin plus sulfadiazine (13%). About three infected fetuses must be treated to prevent one case of serious neurological sequela. In the Austrian cohort the number needed to treat was 2.

## Anti-patterns
- Routine serologic screening in low-prevalence settings: false-positive results and the risk of terminating a healthy pregnancy outweigh benefit.
- Acting on a single positive IgM without reference-laboratory confirmation.
- Pyrimethamine in the first trimester: teratogenic.
- Cesarean delivery to prevent congenital infection: it does not reduce transmission.
- Assuming a negative AF PCR excludes infection: it does not always rule out congenital infection.

## Key Takeaways
- Counsel on food, soil, and cat-litter hygiene at the first visit; education is the mainstay.
- Confirm infection with paired sera at a reference laboratory and use IgG avidity to date it.
- Amniocentesis after 18 weeks and at least 4 weeks after infection.
- Start spiramycin on confirmation; switch to pyrimethamine plus sulfadiazine plus folinic acid if AF PCR is positive, but never give pyrimethamine in the first trimester.
- Monitor blood counts every 1–2 weeks and stop therapy near term.
