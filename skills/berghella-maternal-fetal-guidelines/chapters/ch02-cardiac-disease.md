# Chapter 2: Cardiac Disease

## Core Idea
Cardiovascular disease is the most common cause of pregnancy-related death, although it complicates only 1–4% of pregnancies. Five physiologic changes drive decompensation: systemic vascular resistance (SVR) falls about 20%, plasma volume rises 50–70% and peaks at 32 weeks, a postpartum autotransfusion occurs, hypercoagulability develops, and cardiac output rises sharply in labor. Risk is stratified with the WHO classification from class I to IV; class IV conditions — pulmonary hypertension of any cause, LVEF <30% or prior peripartum cardiomyopathy with dysfunction, severe mitral or symptomatic aortic stenosis, Marfan aortic root >4.5 cm, bicuspid valve aneurysm >5 cm — carry extremely high mortality and should discourage pregnancy. All women with cardiac disease should be managed by a cardio-obstetrics team, and for severe pulmonary hypertension and severe aortic stenosis the intrapartum principle is relative hypervolemia and avoidance of hypotension, not fluid restriction.

## Recommendations and Grades
- Refer every woman with pre-existing or newly diagnosed cardiovascular disease to a cardio-obstetrics team of obstetricians, cardiologists, and maternal-fetal medicine specialists.
- Stratify risk with the modified WHO classification; expert input is often needed to separate class II from class III. *(Review)*
- Offer fetal echocardiography at about 22 weeks to women with congenital heart disease; it identifies most congenital heart defects. *(I)*
- Avoid strict bed rest; activity restriction is appropriate only for symptomatic mitral stenosis and severe pulmonary hypertension, because bed rest increases venous thromboembolism. *(Review)*
- Use epidural analgesia for labor; titrate hydration to avoid hypotension from vasodilation. It is contraindicated in the fully anticoagulated patient.
- Do not give routine antibiotic prophylaxis for vaginal or cesarean delivery; prophylaxis 30 minutes before delivery is reasonable for Eisenmenger syndrome and cyanotic heart disease.
- Prefer cesarean delivery for Marfan or Loeys-Dietz syndrome with aorta >4.5 cm, bicuspid aortopathy with aorta >5 cm, Ehlers-Danlos type IV at any aortic diameter, severe pulmonary hypertension, severe aortic stenosis, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy with obstruction, severe orthopnea, and for the fully anticoagulated patient on warfarin.
- Anticoagulate women with mechanical valves therapeutically throughout pregnancy and postpartum, with weekly level checks; monitor anti-Xa when low-molecular-weight heparin is used. *(I)*
- Consider pulmonary artery catheterization in selected severe pulmonary hypertension, heart failure, severe aortic stenosis, myocardial ischemia, coronary dissection, or aortic dissection.
- Manage cardiac arrest as in the non-pregnant patient: stop fetal monitoring, prioritize the airway, use upper-extremity intravenous or intraosseous access, and consider therapeutic hypothermia case by case. *(Review)*

## Key Concepts
- Normal pregnancy findings versus concerning findings: heart rate >120 bpm, SBP <90 mmHg with symptoms or >160 mmHg, jugular venous distension ≥2 cm above the clavicle, S3 with symptoms or S4, loud systolic or any diastolic murmur, rales, severe edema.
- WHO class II: uncomplicated unrepaired ASD or VSD, repaired tetralogy of Fallot, mild LV dysfunction (LVEF 45–55%), hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, native or tissue valve disease, Marfan without aneurysm. Class III: mechanical valve, systemic right ventricle, Fontan circulation, unrepaired cyanotic disease, Marfan aorta 4–4.5 cm, bicuspid aorta 4–4.5 cm.
- Mitral stenosis: severe when valve area <1.5 cm²; about 50% develop heart failure, 30% of that in the first postpartum week; peak heart rate and cardiac output occur at 28–32 weeks; intervene after the fourth month, with balloon valvuloplasty preferred in rheumatic disease with low Wilkins score.
- Aortic stenosis: complications in up to 10%, heart failure up to 42%, mortality <1%; balloon valvuloplasty may be used in highly symptomatic patients without significant regurgitation.
- Peripartum cardiomyopathy: EF <45%, onset in the last 4 weeks of pregnancy or within 5 months postpartum, peaking at 2 months; incidence 1 in 3000–4000. Anticoagulate if EF <35%. Bromocriptine data are too few for a recommendation.
- Recurrence risk after peripartum cardiomyopathy: persistent dysfunction predicts 19% mortality and 44% heart failure in a subsequent pregnancy; EF <25% leads to transplant or listing in 57%.
- Mechanical valves: valve thrombosis 4.7%, hemorrhage 23.1%, death 1.4%. Warfarin embryopathy is dose-related and less likely below 5 mg.
- Marfan: dural ectasia in about 90%; start beta-blockade early, avoid hypertension and positive inotropes, use retention sutures at cesarean.
- Pulmonary hypertension: mean pulmonary artery pressure >25 mmHg; follow monthly, then every 2 weeks in the third trimester with serial echocardiography; furosemide is the diuretic of choice.
- Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy: one third have outflow obstruction; avoid tachycardia, hypovolemia, hypotension, and inotropes.
- Coarctation: most common effect is uncontrolled hypertension; main danger is aortic dissection or rupture.

## Anti-patterns
- Fluid restriction in severe pulmonary hypertension or severe aortic stenosis: these patients need relative hypervolemia; restriction worsens preload-dependent cardiac output.
- Hypotension and hypercarbia during delivery in pulmonary hypertension: both accentuate right ventricular failure.
- Direct oral anticoagulants in mechanical valves: contraindicated.
- Angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors in pregnancy: teratogenic; switch before conception.
- Routine endocarditis prophylaxis at delivery: not recommended by American or European guidelines except in Eisenmenger syndrome and cyanotic heart disease.
- Strict bed rest: increases venous thromboembolism.
- Supine labor: inferior vena cava compression reduces cardiac output; use left lateral decubitus.
- Assuming a normal echocardiogram excludes risk after peripartum cardiomyopathy: up to 21% have subclinical low contractile reserve and later symptoms.

## Key Takeaways
1. Screen for cardiac disease in any pregnant woman with severe fatigue, dyspnea at rest, chest pain at rest, loud systolic or any diastolic murmur, or S3 with symptoms.
2. Use WHO class IV as the threshold for advising against pregnancy, and document contraceptive planning.
3. In severe pulmonary hypertension and severe aortic stenosis, give volume and avoid hypotension; in mitral stenosis, control heart rate and avoid tachycardia.
4. Plan delivery with the cardio-obstetrics and anesthesia teams; cesarean is reserved for defined cardiac indications or usual obstetric indications.
5. Anticoagulate mechanical valves continuously and convert warfarin before delivery, because warfarin at delivery mandates cesarean.
