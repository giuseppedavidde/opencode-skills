# Chapter 39: Group B Streptococcus

## Core Idea
Asymptomatic anovaginal group B streptococcus (GBS) colonization affects about 20% of pregnant patients (range 10–30%) and, without intervention, causes neonatal GBS disease in 1–2% of vertically exposed neonates. Universal prenatal culture screening at 36 0/7 to 37 6/7 weeks, with intrapartum antibiotic prophylaxis (IAP) for colonized patients and for those with unknown status plus a risk factor, is more effective than a risk-factor-only strategy and is the recommended United States approach. Penicillin G is first-line; cefazolin is used for non-anaphylactic penicillin allergy; clindamycin or vancomycin is used for the high-risk anaphylaxis group according to susceptibility. There is no effective prevention for late-onset disease.

## Recommendations and Grades
- Screen all pregnant patients with a combined vaginal–rectal culture at 36 0/7 to 37 6/7 weeks. Source: ACOG Committee Opinion No. 797 [Guideline; III]. ACOG moved the window from 35 to 36 weeks because IAP is now default for preterm delivery with unknown status, and a 36-week result stays valid to 41 weeks.
- Do not screen the two groups who are already committed to IAP: GBS bacteriuria at any colony count in the current pregnancy, and a previous infant with invasive GBS disease.
- Give IAP to: previous infant with invasive GBS disease; GBS bacteriuria in the current pregnancy; positive GBS culture in the current pregnancy (unless cesarean with intact membranes before labor); intrapartum NAAT positive; or unknown status with delivery <37 weeks, membrane rupture ≥18 hours, intrapartum temperature ≥100.4°F (≥38.0°C), or known GBS positivity in a previous pregnancy. Modified from CDC 2010 [Review/guideline].
- Do not give IAP for: a positive culture in a previous pregnancy alone (unless current status unknown), cesarean delivery without labor or membrane rupture, or a negative late-gestation vaginal–rectal culture regardless of intrapartum risk factors.
- Penicillin G 5 million units IV initial dose, then 3 million units IV every 4 hours until delivery. Alternative: ampicillin 2 g IV initial, then 1 g IV every 4 hours.
- Penicillin allergy without high anaphylaxis risk: cefazolin 2 g IV initial, then 1 g IV every 8 hours.
- High anaphylaxis risk with a GBS isolate susceptible to clindamycin and erythromycin: clindamycin 900 mg IV every 8 hours. Resistant to either agent, or susceptibility unknown: vancomycin 20 mg/kg IV every 8 hours, maximum 2 g per single dose. Note that erythromycin resistance often, but not always, predicts clindamycin resistance, and erythromycin should not be used for treatment.
- Suspected intraamniotic infection: replace prophylaxis with broad-spectrum therapy that covers GBS, such as ampicillin plus gentamicin.
- Do not give antepartum antibiotics for asymptomatic colonization, except GBS bacteriuria. Penicillin 1 million IU three times daily for 6 days at 27–31 weeks decreases preterm birth <37 weeks [placebo-controlled trial].
- Do not withhold indicated obstetric interventions such as amniotomy just to reach 4 hours of IAP [9].

## Key Concepts
- Incidence of early-onset GBS sepsis in the United States fell from 1.8/1000 live births in the 1990s to 0.23/1000 in 2015; after the 2002 CDC screening recommendation it fell from 0.47/1000 (1999–2001) to 0.23/1000.
- IAP decreases infant colonization by 90% and early-onset disease by 83%. Without IAP, 1–2% of neonates who experience vertical transmission develop early-onset disease. Highest efficacy when antibiotics are given ≥4 hours before delivery; a smaller but real benefit exists at 2 hours.
- 40–75% of neonates born to colonized patients are themselves colonized. With screening and treatment, 0.4% of colonized neonates develop early-onset sepsis; without treatment, 2%.
- Over 20% of neonates with early-onset GBS sepsis are born to patients without risk factors — the rationale for universal screening.
- Table 39.1: early onset occurs <1 week (80% of neonatal GBS sepsis without prophylaxis, 50% with screening and treatment), usually manifest at 24–48 hours; late onset ≥1 week (20% without prophylaxis, 50% with screening and treatment), usually at 3–4 weeks. Late onset is 95% serotype III. Case fatality overall 5%, term 2–3%, <33 weeks 30%; late-onset <2%. Meningitis in late onset carries 15–50% neurologic sequelae. Neonatal death in 4–6% of early-onset cases.
- GBS bacteriuria is defined as ≥10⁵ CFU/mL; any concentration of GBS in urine is a marker of heavy colonization and an indication for IAP.
- Culture technique: lower vagina then anorectal sampling, swab inserted internal to the anal sphincter; transport in Amies or Stuart's medium without charcoal; label "penicillin allergy" when applicable; incubate 18–24 hours in selective enrichment broth (Todd–Hewitt with antibiotics) then subculture on blood agar. For penicillin-allergic patients, perform clindamycin and erythromycin disk susceptibility.
- NAAT after 18–24 hours of incubation is equivalent to culture; rapid NAAT without incubation has reduced sensitivity but adequate specificity (92–99%) to guide IAP at term with unknown status and no risk factors. NAAT never allows susceptibility testing.
- A culture taken earlier in pregnancy is less predictive than one taken within 5 weeks of birth; cultures after prophylactic antibiotics may not reflect true status.
- Vaginal chlorhexidine does not reduce neonatal early-onset GBS disease or mortality.
- Penicillin anaphylaxis occurs in 4–40/100,000 women given prophylaxis; most patients reporting penicillin allergy are not truly allergic, and skin testing is safe in pregnancy.
- Cesarean delivery before labor or membrane rupture carries extremely low early-onset risk; prophylaxis is not indicated. Patients planning cesarean still need screening in case they labor.
- Preterm premature rupture of membranes at or after 34 weeks with GBS colonization: counsel to proceed with delivery.

## Anti-patterns
- Using a risk-factor-based strategy instead of universal screening: it is less effective, gives unnecessary prophylaxis to some GBS-negative patients and misses prophylaxis in others, and 20% of early-onset cases have no risk factors.
- Treating asymptomatic GBS colonization with antepartum antibiotics other than for bacteriuria: no proven maternal or neonatal benefit, and treatment does not eradicate genitourinary or gastrointestinal carriage.
- Treating GBS bacteriuria with the goal of eradicating carriage: the benefit is reduction of preterm birth, not decolonization.
- Using erythromycin for treatment: not recommended, and resistance is often linked to clindamycin resistance.
- Relying on a rapid NAAT result to select a penicillin alternative: NAAT gives no susceptibility data.
- Giving vancomycin to a patient whose isolate is susceptible to clindamycin and erythromycin.
- Delaying amniotomy or other indicated intervention to complete 4 hours of IAP.
- Expecting screening-based prophylaxis to prevent late-onset disease: it does not.

## Key Takeaways
1. One vaginal–rectal culture at 36 0/7 to 37 6/7 weeks for everyone except those with GBS bacteriuria or a prior affected infant.
2. Penicillin G 5 million units IV, then 3 million units every 4 hours until delivery, as early as possible and ideally ≥4 hours before birth.
3. Cefazolin 2 g then 1 g every 8 hours for non-anaphylactic allergy; clindamycin 900 mg every 8 hours or vancomycin 20 mg/kg every 8 hours for the high-risk group.
4. Unknown status plus preterm delivery, rupture ≥18 hours, or temperature ≥38.0°C means treat.
5. Cesarean before labor with intact membranes needs no prophylaxis.
6. GBS bacteriuria at any count means prophylaxis in labor, and treatment at 27–31 weeks reduces preterm birth.
