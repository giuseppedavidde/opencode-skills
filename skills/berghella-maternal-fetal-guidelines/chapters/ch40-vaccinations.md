# Chapter 40: Vaccinations

## Core Idea
Immunity should be assessed before conception, and indicated vaccines should be given before pregnancy. Once a woman is pregnant, inactivated, recombinant, subunit, and toxoid vaccines and immunoglobulins pose no threat to the fetus and should be given when the maternal indication is present: no vaccine is more dangerous to a pregnant woman or her fetus than the disease it prevents. Live attenuated vaccines are contraindicated on theoretical grounds, but inadvertent rubella, varicella, or BCG vaccination has not been associated with adverse fetal outcomes. Inactivated influenza vaccine is given in any trimester during influenza season, and Tdap in every pregnancy at 27–36 weeks. Breastfeeding is not a contraindication to any vaccine except smallpox.

## Recommendations and Grades
- Determine immunity to rubella, varicella, influenza, and hepatitis B in the preconception period and vaccinate as needed [Guideline: CDC/ACIP].
- Give inactivated influenza vaccine to all pregnant women in any trimester during influenza season; do not give live attenuated intranasal vaccine (LAIV, FluMist) [Guideline: ACOG Committee Opinion 732].
- Give Tdap to every pregnant woman in every pregnancy, regardless of prior vaccination history, optimally at 27–36 weeks [Guideline: CDC/ACIP 2013, reaffirmed 2019]. Vaccine effectiveness for newborn pertussis protection is 90–93%.
- Give hepatitis B vaccine in pregnancy when the woman is at high risk for HBV; it is safe. Three-dose series at 0, 1, and 4 months (third dose at least 2 months after the second and at least 4 months after the first) [Guideline].
- Recommend mRNA COVID-19 vaccines (Pfizer, Moderna) in any trimester and during lactation. Withholding FDA-approved vaccines from pregnant or lactating women solely because of pregnancy status is unethical.
- Vaccinate non-immune women against rubella (MMR) and varicella in the immediate postpartum period; advise avoidance of pregnancy for 4 weeks after a live vaccine [Guideline].
- Do not give MMR, varicella, BCG, or smallpox vaccine during pregnancy. Smallpox vaccination may be considered after exposure: vaccination within 3 days of exposure completely prevents or significantly modifies disease in most people, and 4–7 days after exposure likely gives some protection or reduces severity.
- For varicella exposure in a non-immune pregnant woman: varicella zoster immune globulin (VZIG) within 96 hours; if unavailable, IVIG 400 mg/kg as a single IV dose, or close monitoring and acyclovir at disease onset [Guideline].
- Immunize close contacts of the newborn against influenza and assess pertussis boosters in household contacts (cocooning) [Guideline].
- HPV vaccine is not recommended in pregnancy; if a woman becomes pregnant after starting the series, delay the remainder until after delivery, and no intervention is needed after inadvertent administration.

## Key Concepts
- Passive immunization: maternal IgG crosses the placenta mainly in the last 4–6 weeks of gestation; IgA passes in breast milk.
- Influenza in pregnancy: fourfold increase in serious illness and hospitalization. Physiologic drivers are decreased pulmonary volume, increased cardiac output, and suppressed cell-mediated immunity. No study since 1957 has shown an adverse consequence of inactivated influenza vaccine in pregnancy.
- Td: maternal tetanus toxoid vaccination is up to 98% effective in preventing neonatal tetanus; Td effectiveness in preventing neonatal deaths is 62%. WHO estimates 1.5 million neonatal tetanus cases prevented since the 1989 elimination initiative.
- Pertussis: family members are the source in 75% of early-infancy cases; infants under 12 months account for most pertussis morbidity and mortality. Maternal antibody peaks at least 2 weeks after vaccination, which is why 27–36 weeks is preferred.
- Hepatitis B: vertical transmission occurs in up to 90% of infected women depending on viral status, and 90% of infected children develop chronic infection. Risk indications include more than one sexual partner in the past 6 months, injection or non-injection drug use, an HBsAg-positive sex partner, other STIs, household contact with an HBsAg-positive person, health care work, dialysis, and travel of more than 6 months to a high-prevalence area.
- Pneumococcal polysaccharide vaccine: single dose for maternal indications (smoking, asthma, chronic pulmonary, liver, cardiovascular, or renal disease, asplenia, diabetes, immunosuppression); one-time revaccination after 5 years for renal failure, nephrotic syndrome, asplenia, or high-dose steroids. It is recommended for maternal indications only; a Cochrane review found insufficient evidence for neonatal protection.
- Meningococcal: quadrivalent polysaccharide preferred over conjugate based on safety data; revaccinate after 5 years if risk continues. No pregnancy data exist for serogroup B vaccines.
- Rabies: inactivated vaccine, three doses on days 0, 7, 21 or 28 for pre-exposure; post-exposure for the unvaccinated is rabies immune globulin plus four vaccine doses on days 0, 3, 7, and 14 (five doses, adding day 28, if immunosuppressed); if previously vaccinated, one dose immediately and one 3 days later. Multiple studies show no elevated risk in pregnancy.
- Yellow fever vaccine is live and not well studied in pregnancy; vaccinate if travel to a high-risk area is unavoidable.
- Acyclovir pregnancy registry: 124,748 exposures with no association with adverse pregnancy outcome.
- The only true contraindication applicable to all vaccines is a severe allergic reaction after a prior dose or to a vaccine component, unless the patient has been desensitized.

## Anti-patterns
- Giving live attenuated influenza vaccine (intranasal) in pregnancy: contraindicated; use the inactivated intramuscular product.
- Deferring all vaccines until after delivery: inactivated, recombinant, subunit, and toxoid vaccines are safe at any gestational age.
- Waiting until the postpartum period for Tdap: maternal antibody must be transferred before birth, and the 27–36 week window is what produces 90–93% neonatal protection.
- Withholding a vaccine because the patient is breastfeeding: breastfeeding does not adversely affect immunization and is not a contraindication, except for smallpox vaccine.
- Chasing rubella or varicella immunity during pregnancy with a live vaccine: screen prenatally and vaccinate postpartum.
- Giving the HPV series during pregnancy: delay remaining doses.
- Assuming one Tdap covers later pregnancies: give it in every pregnancy.

## Key Takeaways
1. Screen immunity before pregnancy; vaccinate before conception when possible.
2. Inactivated influenza vaccine in any trimester, every influenza season.
3. Tdap at 27–36 weeks in every pregnancy.
4. Give inactivated, recombinant, subunit, and toxoid vaccines whenever the maternal indication exists; they are safe.
5. Live vaccines are contraindicated; no adverse fetal outcomes have followed inadvertent rubella, varicella, or BCG vaccination.
6. Vaccinate household contacts (cocooning) against influenza and pertussis.
