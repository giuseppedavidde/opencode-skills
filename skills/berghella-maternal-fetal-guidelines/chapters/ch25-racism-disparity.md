# Chapter 25: Racism and Racial Disparity in Obstetrics

## Core Idea
Race is a lived experience, not a biological variable. The chapter argues that institutional, interpersonal, and internalized racism act as independent risk factors for maternal mortality, preterm birth, hypertensive disease, and poor pain control. Non-Hispanic Black women die from pregnancy-related causes 3–4 times more often than non-Hispanic White women, and this gap is not explained by poverty, education, or nativity. The recommended response is structural: standardized care bundles, doula support, group prenatal care, low-dose aspirin per USPSTF, diverse provider teams, and measured accountability.

## Recommendations and Grades
- Provide continuous one-to-one intrapartum support, including doulas, to Black patients — Grade [I] (Cochrane meta-analysis, 26 RCTs, n = 15,858; lower cesarean rates).
- Implement standardized, evidence-based quality care bundles with improvement metrics — Grade [I] (literature review; 1 RCT, n = 1,047).
- Use group prenatal care that builds social support — Grade [I] (meta-analysis; 4 RCTs, 10 observational studies, n = 10,321); single-program data Grade [II-2].
- Prescribe low-dose aspirin according to USPSTF criteria and use standardized protocols for end-organ evaluation in prenatal care — Grade [II-2] (SMFM special report).
- Use text-based remote blood pressure monitoring postpartum — Grade [I] (RCT, n = 206).
- Screen for social determinants of health; use home visits and accountable care organizations — Grade [I] (literature review; 4 systematic reviews, 1 RCT).
- Train providers in patient-centered communication and increase patient–provider racial concordance — Grade [II-2] (systematic review).
- Build standardized protocols for intrapartum and postpartum pain management — no high-quality interventional studies; disparity data Grade [II-2].
- Educate providers on racial disparities, shared decision-making, and cultural competency; deliver implicit bias training — Grade [III] for the structural-racism framing.

## Key Concepts
- Pregnancy-related mortality ratio (PRMR): 40.8 per 100,000 live births for Black women versus 12.7 per 100,000 for White women.
- Black women with the highest educational attainment have maternal death rates 1.6 times higher than White women with the lowest attainment.
- Black–White disparity ratio is 1.8 among women without a high school diploma and 5.2 among college graduates — protective factors for White women do not transfer.
- Preterm birth rate is double in non-Hispanic Black patients; recurrence is 4 times the White rate. Mothers born before 34 weeks carry the highest risk of delivering before 34 weeks.
- 17-alpha hydroxyprogesterone caproate (17-OHPC): 67.6% of Black women versus 32.4% of other women had more than one missed dose or started after 20 weeks (n = 472); Medicaid non-coverage of home administration contributed.
- Late prenatal care (third trimester): 10% of Black women versus 4% of White women. First-trimester entry: White 79%, Asian 78%, multiracial 71%, Hispanic 69%, Black 64%, American Indian/Alaska Native 59%, Native Hawaiian/Pacific Islander 55%.
- Black patients have three times higher odds of experiencing discrimination in prenatal care; over one fifth avoided necessary care to prevent discrimination.
- Pre-eclampsia/eclampsia mortality: Black patients 3.1 times as likely to die (1979–1992 data), with disparity persisting among those who received prenatal care.
- Postpartum hypertension readmission is twice as likely among Black patients.
- Epidural rates are lower for Black, Hispanic, and Asian patients (review of >29,000 Georgia Medicaid recipients); Black patients also receive less opioid analgesia and fewer discharge opioid prescriptions despite higher pain scores.
- 40% of women report communication problems in prenatal care (Listening to Mothers III).
- Housing audit: White renters favored over identically qualified Black applicants in 22% of tests and over Hispanic applicants in 26%.
- Allostatic load and "weathering" link chronic racism stress to cortisol, inflammation, and cardiovascular reactivity.

## Anti-patterns
- Explaining disparities by poverty, education, or social determinants alone — adjusted analyses show the Black–White gap is minimally altered by these measures.
- Invoking genetic or biological race as the cause — foreign-born Black women have higher maternal mortality than White women despite less education and later prenatal care, pointing to lived experience rather than ancestry.
- Assuming socioeconomic advantage is protective — the disparity ratio is widest among college graduates.
- Treating patient–provider racial discordance as an unsurmountable barrier — communication training can close the gap.
- Relying on individual-level fixes while ignoring system-level drivers such as residential segregation, referral bias, and unequal hospital quality.

## Key Takeaways
1. Measure and report your own institution's disparities; accurate measurement is itself an intervention ([I]).
2. Offer doula or continuous one-to-one support to every Black patient in labor ([I]).
3. Start low-dose aspirin per USPSTF and use standardized pre-eclampsia end-organ protocols ([II-2]).
4. Audit 17-OHPC and prenatal care initiation for late starts and missed doses in Black patients.
5. Extend postpartum blood pressure surveillance by text or remote monitoring ([I]).
6. Screen for social determinants and refer; do not let the screening substitute for antiracist structural change.
