# Chapter 6: Hypothyroidism

## Core Idea
Overt hypothyroidism complicates 0.2–1% of pregnancies and is diagnosed by elevated TSH with low free T4; subclinical hypothyroidism is elevated TSH with normal free T4. Hashimoto's thyroiditis is the most common cause, with thyroid peroxidase antibodies in over 90%. Untreated or partly treated disease raises the risk of pre-eclampsia (44%), abruption (19%), low birth weight (31%), and fetal death (12%). Levothyroxine is the only recommended replacement, titrated to a TSH between the lower reference limit (often 0.5–0.6 mU/L) and 2.5 mU/L. Most women need a dose increase, and the increase should start at the positive pregnancy test. Universal screening is not recommended; screen women with high-risk criteria and check thyroid peroxidase antibodies when TSH is >2.5 mU/L.

## Recommendations and Grades
- Screen women at high risk for thyroid dysfunction in the first trimester with TSH and free T4, and check thyroid peroxidase antibodies if TSH >2.5 mU/L. *(Guideline)*
- Do not perform universal screening for hypothyroidism, subclinical hypothyroidism, or hypothyroxinemia; no trial shows benefit from screening and treatment. *(Guideline)*
- Treat overt hypothyroidism with levothyroxine to a target TSH between the lower limit of the reference range and 2.5 mU/L. *(Guideline)*
- Check TSH and free T4 before conception, at the first prenatal visit, 4 weeks after any dose change (every 4 weeks until TSH is normal, especially in the first 20 weeks), and at least once each trimester. *(Guideline)*
- Increase the prepregnancy levothyroxine dose by 20–30% at a positive pregnancy test and contact the physician; 45–85% of hypothyroid women need up to a 45% increase during pregnancy.
- Start levothyroxine at 1–2 µg/kg daily, or about 100 µg daily, for a new diagnosis, then adjust by TSH.
- Space levothyroxine at least 2–3 hours from prenatal vitamins, ferrous sulfate, and calcium carbonate.
- Give iodine 150 µg daily when planning pregnancy and 250 µg daily during pregnancy and breastfeeding. *(Guideline)*
- Assess fetal heart rate by doptone at each visit to detect bradycardia <120 bpm; consider weekly non-stress tests from about 32 weeks and monthly growth ultrasound only in clinically hypothyroid women.
- Reduce levothyroxine to the prepregnancy dose immediately after delivery and measure TSH at 6 weeks; stop treatment if the daily dose is ≤50 µg.
- Evaluate every thyroid nodule with fine-needle aspiration and TSH; perform surgery for malignancy in the second trimester or postpartum, and defer neck irradiation until after pregnancy.

## Key Concepts
- Primary hypothyroidism: high TSH, low free T4, with thyroid antibodies positive or negative. Secondary: low TSH and low free T4.
- Subclinical hypothyroidism affects 2–5% of pregnant women; hypothyroxinemia affects about 1.3%.
- Incidence is 5–8% in women with type 1 diabetes; up to 25% of women with type 1 diabetes develop postpartum thyroid dysfunction.
- Thyroid-binding globulin rises about 200%; human chorionic gonadotropin peaks at 10–12 weeks and suppresses TSH; thyroid volume increases about 18%.
- Free T4, free T3, thyrotropin-releasing hormone, iodine, thyroid-stimulating immunoglobulin, and thyroid peroxidase antibody cross the placenta; TSH does not.
- If population-specific reference ranges are unavailable, use an upper TSH limit of 4.0 mU/L.
- Thyroid peroxidase antibodies are present in 90% of Hashimoto's thyroiditis and 2–17% of unselected pregnant women; levels >50 IU/mL are associated with abruption.
- High-risk criteria: personal or family history of thyroid disease, symptoms, goiter or nodules, head or neck irradiation or thyroid surgery, age >30, type 1 diabetes or other autoimmune disease, pregnancy loss or preterm delivery or infertility, ≥2 prior pregnancies, BMI ≥40 kg/m², amiodarone or lithium or iodinated contrast, and residence in an area of moderate to severe iodine deficiency.
- Euthyroid women with positive thyroid peroxidase antibodies need TSH every 4 weeks through midpregnancy; up to 30% develop permanent hypothyroidism after postpartum dysfunction.
- Over-replacement is harmful: it is associated with pregnancy loss and low birth weight.
- Postpartum thyroiditis occurs in 5% of women without prior thyroid disease; 28% have transient thyrotoxicosis then recovery, 28% thyrotoxicosis then hypothyroidism, and 44% hypothyroidism alone.
- Postpartum thyroiditis treatment: beta-blocker for symptomatic thyrotoxicosis for a few weeks; thyroxine 25–75 µg daily for 6–12 months for hypothyroidism. Recurrence is 70%; check TSH annually.
- Congenital hypothyroidism occurs in 1 in 4000 births; 5% are identified clinically at birth, the rest by newborn screening.

## Anti-patterns
- Universal screening for subclinical hypothyroidism or hypothyroxinemia: two large RCTs showed no IQ or cognitive benefit at 3, 5, and 9 years.
- Treating subclinical hypothyroidism without checking thyroid peroxidase antibodies: antibody status should guide the decision.
- Using liothyronine or desiccated thyroid extract: avoid in pregnancy; levothyroxine is the recommended preparation.
- Antithyroid drugs for the thyrotoxic phase of postpartum thyroiditis: not recommended; use a beta-adrenergic antagonist.
- Taking levothyroxine with prenatal vitamins, iron, or calcium: absorption falls; separate doses by 2–3 hours.
- Under- or over-replacement: both cause harm; monitor TSH every 4 weeks while adjusting.
- Radioactive iodine or neck irradiation during pregnancy: defer.
- Missing postpartum thyroid dysfunction in women with depression: screen all women with depression for thyroid dysfunction.

## Key Takeaways
1. Screen with TSH and free T4 only in high-risk women, and add thyroid peroxidase antibodies when TSH >2.5 mU/L.
2. Treat overt disease to TSH <2.5 mU/L and increase the dose by 20–30% as soon as pregnancy is confirmed.
3. Recheck TSH every 4 weeks until stable, then each trimester.
4. Use levothyroxine alone, separated from iron and calcium by 2–3 hours.
5. After delivery, return to the prepregnancy dose, check TSH at 6 weeks, and check TSH annually after postpartum thyroiditis.
