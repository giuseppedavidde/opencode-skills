# Chapter 43: Amniotic Fluid Embolism

## Core Idea
Amniotic fluid embolism (AFE) is a rare, often lethal event of labor and the immediate puerperium, classically presenting as sudden hypoxia, hypotension, and coagulopathy. The old mechanical-obstruction hypothesis has been discredited: injection of amniotic fluid into the pulmonary vasculature does not reproduce the syndrome, fetal squamous cells are neither sensitive nor specific, and the current model is a massive inflammatory response to fetal antigens with endothelin-driven pulmonary vasoconstriction. Management is entirely supportive and must begin before the diagnosis is confirmed. The first phase is right ventricular failure, best identified by bedside transthoracic echocardiography; the second is left ventricular failure with cardiogenic pulmonary edema; a third phase of distributive shock and nosocomial infection follows in survivors.

## Recommendations and Grades
- Begin high-quality cardiopulmonary resuscitation and follow advanced cardiac life support immediately for cardiac arrest. Source: standard ACLS, extrapolated; no AFE-specific trials.
- Deliver immediately in the case of a viable pregnancy. This is a key-point recommendation.
- Perform perimortem cesarean delivery if there is no return of spontaneous circulation within 4 minutes despite resuscitation in a patient with gestational age >23 weeks or a gravid uterus above the umbilicus. Earlier delivery is reasonable: after 2 minutes of unsuccessful CPR with a 38-week fetus, start the cesarean rather than wait.
- Perform operative vaginal delivery (forceps or vacuum) without delay when the cervix is dilated and the fetal head is at low station.
- Use bedside transthoracic echocardiography to identify right ventricular failure early; it also distinguishes AFE from anaphylaxis.
- Maintain MAP 65–70 mmHg after return of spontaneous circulation; avoid fever and hyperoxia, weaning FiO2 to keep SpO2 94–98%; keep glucose 140–180 mg/dL.
- Consider targeted temperature management at 36°C rather than 32–34°C in AFE survivors, because of the bleeding risk; consider it on an individual basis, as evidence in pregnancy is limited to case reports.
- Involve a multidisciplinary team: maternal-fetal medicine, intensivist, anesthesiologist, and neonatology.
- For coagulopathy, maintain platelets >50,000/mm³, aPTT <1.5 times the upper limit of normal, INR <2, and fibrinogen >150–200 mg/dL. Activate the massive transfusion protocol without delay in massive hemorrhage, regardless of laboratory results, using a 1:1:1 ratio of packed red cells, fresh frozen plasma, and platelets.
- Consider early antifibrinolytic therapy (tranexamic acid or epsilon aminocaproic acid) with bedside thromboelastography.
- Reserve recombinant activated factor VII for intractable hemorrhage after massive component replacement and surgical intervention, because of diffuse thrombosis and multiorgan failure risk when combined with the elevated tissue factor of AFE.

## Key Concepts
- Incidence 0.8–1.8 per 100,000; absence of a validated case definition can double the apparent incidence. Up to 10% of maternal deaths are caused by AFE; in a United States registry of about 1.5 million deliveries, AFE was second only to preeclampsia as a disease cause of maternal death.
- Mortality: 61% with 15% neurologically intact survival in the 1995 national registry; more recent series report mortality as low as 26% and intact survival up to 93%. Death or permanent neurologic injury 30–41% depending on case definition. Perinatal mortality as high as 25%.
- Timing: 70% of cases occur during labor, 11% after vaginal delivery, 19% during cesarean delivery. It rarely occurs in the second or third trimester, at amniocentesis, or at pregnancy termination. A prodrome of anxiety, altered mental status, agitation, and a sensation of "doom" may precede collapse.
- Risk factors, modifiable: medical induction of labor, instrumental vaginal delivery, cervical lacerations, uterine rupture, cesarean section. Non-modifiable: age >30 years, African American race, eclampsia, polyhydramnios, male fetus, placenta previa, placental abruption, multiple pregnancy.
- Oxytocin is unlikely to be causative given its ubiquity compared with the rarity of AFE. Uterine tone abnormalities are a consequence of hypoperfusion and adrenergic surge, not a cause. AFE remains unpredictable and unpreventable; modifying obstetric practice solely to prevent it is not recommended.
- DIC occurs in up to 83% of cases; it is a consumptive coagulopathy, driven by amniotic fluid tissue factor binding factor VII and activating the extrinsic pathway, plus monocyte and neutrophil tissue factor expression. Rarely, coagulopathy is the only manifestation. Complications include bleeding from venipuncture sites, gastrointestinal hemorrhage, hematuria, pelvic lacerations, and uterine bleeding.
- Right ventricular failure physiology: endothelin-mediated pulmonary vasoconstriction raises afterload and causes acute cor pulmonale without anatomical obstruction; right ventricular dilation causes ischemia by compressing the right coronary vessels and shifts the interventricular septum leftward, reducing left ventricular preload and output.
- Left ventricular failure in phase two results from hypoxic stunning plus cytokine-mediated depression (TNF-α, nitric oxide, platelet activating factor, interleukin-1), producing cardiogenic pulmonary edema.
- Do not give fluid boluses in right ventricular failure: they further dilate the right ventricle, worsen septal compression, and can cause pericardial coronary compression and right ventricular ischemia.
- Right ventricular drugs: dobutamine 2.5–5.0 µg/kg/min; milrinone 0.25–0.75 µg/kg/min; sildenafil 20 mg three times daily by mouth or nasogastric tube; inhaled nitric oxide 5–40 ppm (check methemoglobin every 6 hours, avoid abrupt discontinuation); inhaled prostacyclin 10–50 ng/kg/min; IV prostacyclin starting at 1–2 ng/kg/min through a central line (avoid in severe left ventricular systolic dysfunction). Norepinephrine 0.05–3.3 µg/kg/min; vasopressin 0.03–0.04 units/min.
- CPR: firm backboard, supine, hands at the lower half of the sternum, depth at least 5 cm (2 inches), full recoil, 30:2 before an advanced airway, then 100–120 compressions/min with one breath every 6 seconds. Continuous manual left uterine displacement if ≥20 weeks and undelivered. Vasopressors, antiarrhythmics, and defibrillation doses are unchanged; fetal monitors do not cause arcing and may stay in place.
- Diagnostic testing is by exclusion. IGFBP-1 is a proposed biomarker but not confirmatory. Serum tryptase is not elevated in AFE, unlike anaphylaxis. Low complement levels are too nonspecific to confirm the diagnosis.
- Differential diagnosis: myocardial infarction (troponin and 12-lead ECG), pulmonary embolism (right ventricular dilation; unlikely with profuse bleeding, and right ventricular failure with coagulopathy strongly suggests AFE), high spinal anesthesia, local anesthetic toxicity (seizures after injection with a short latency), air embolism (left lateral decubitus, 100% oxygen, aspirate air from a central line, hyperbaric oxygen for arterial air embolism), eclampsia, and anaphylaxis (urticaria, laryngospasm, bronchospasm — bronchospasm occurs in only 15% of AFE; coagulopathy and cardiac dysfunction are rare in anaphylaxis).
- ECMO: venoarterial ECMO for refractory shock; watch for hemolysis, bleeding, infection, circuit thrombosis, intracranial hemorrhage, and North-South syndrome (monitor right radial artery PaO2). ECMO may be started without anticoagulation during active bleeding and heparin begun after a few hours.
- Steroids are not indicated in AFE.
- Cryoprecipitate: 150–350 mg fibrinogen per unit, each unit raises fibrinogen by about 10 mg/dL. Fibrinogen concentrate (2 g per 100 mL) avoids viral transmission and TRALI and is stored at room temperature.
- Uterine atony: oxytocin, ergot derivatives, prostaglandins; then intrauterine balloon tamponade, uterine artery ligation, B-Lynch stitch, or hysterectomy. Inspect vaginal lacerations after vaginal delivery. Damage control surgery for uncontrolled cesarean bleeding.
- Recurrence: no recurrent cases have been published; multiple uneventful subsequent pregnancies are reported, but no data exist to counsel survivors on recurrence risk.

## Anti-patterns
- Waiting for a confirmed diagnosis before starting resuscitation: AFE is a clinical diagnosis of exclusion and treatment is supportive.
- Giving fluid boluses to a hypotensive patient with a dilated right ventricle: worsens septal shift and coronary compression.
- Treating suspected AFE with steroids: not indicated.
- Delaying perimortem cesarean until 4 full minutes have elapsed in every case: earlier delivery is appropriate with a near-term fetus.
- Withholding CPR drugs or defibrillation because of pregnancy: doses are unchanged.
- Using splanchnic or unselective IV prostacyclin in severe left ventricular dysfunction.
- Relying on fetal squamous cells in the pulmonary vasculature as proof of AFE.
- Using recombinant factor VIIa early: reserve it for intractable hemorrhage after massive replacement.
- Cooling an AFE survivor to 32–34°C without weighing hemorrhage risk.

## Key Takeaways
1. Suspect AFE in any pregnant or recently delivered woman with sudden hypoxia, hypotension, seizures, or unexplained coagulopathy.
2. Start ACLS immediately; give high-quality compressions first because oxygenation is normal at the outset.
3. Echocardiography early: a dilated hypokinetic right ventricle with septal shift confirms the phase and guides therapy.
4. Deliver the viable fetus; perimortem cesarean within 4 minutes of no ROSC, sooner if near term.
5. Treat right ventricular failure with inotropes and pulmonary vasodilators, not fluids; maintain MAP 65–70 mmHg.
6. Treat DIC aggressively with a 1:1:1 massive transfusion protocol, fibrinogen >150–200 mg/dL, and early antifibrinolytics.
