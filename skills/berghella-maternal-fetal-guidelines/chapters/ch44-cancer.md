# Chapter 44: Cancer

## Core Idea
Cancer complicates about 1 in 1000 pregnancies and 1 in 118 malignancies is associated with pregnancy; the incidence is driven by maternal age, not by pregnancy itself. Delay in diagnosis is the main avoidable harm, so diagnostic workup proceeds as in non-pregnant adults, using the safest study that answers the question. Chemotherapy is relatively safe in the second and third trimesters but carries the highest malformation risk in the first trimester; doses are calculated on the actual height and weight during pregnancy, not pre-pregnancy or ideal body weight. Iatrogenic preterm delivery before 34/35 weeks is to be avoided, chemotherapy stops by 34/35 weeks with at least 3 weeks between the last cycle and delivery, and delivery is planned at ≥37 weeks. Multidisciplinary care with an oncologist and maternal-fetal specialist is essential.

## Recommendations and Grades
- Do not delay diagnosis. Perform necessary diagnostic studies as in non-pregnant adults, with rare exceptions; choose MRI over CT when it gives similar information. This is a key-point recommendation without a formal grade.
- Postpone radiologic studies that will not alter cancer treatment or the patient's decisions during pregnancy.
- Avoid iatrogenic preterm delivery before 34/35 weeks.
- Use whole-body diffusion-weighted MRI (WB-DWI/MRI) for staging and detection of distant metastasis in solid tumors and lymphoma, and for assessing tumor response. This is described as a "could be used" option, not a graded recommendation.
- Choose the regimen with the most experience and proven safety in pregnancy, provided it offers a similar chance of cure.
- Give the same chemotherapy doses as for non-pregnant women, based on actual height and weight during pregnancy; do not use pre-pregnancy or ideal body weight.
- Allow at least 3 weeks between a chemotherapy cycle and delivery; interrupt or complete regimens by 34/35 weeks' gestation.
- Send placental pathology for all cancers, especially melanoma.
- Do not offer cell-free DNA (NIPT) prenatal diagnosis to pregnant women with known malignancy, because tumor-derived cfDNA can mask the fetal chromosomal result.
- Do not use trastuzumab (Herceptin) in pregnancy: it is contraindicated because of oligohydramnios and pulmonary hypoplasia.
- Do not use idarubicin in pregnancy: transient neonatal cardiomyopathy has been reported; daunorubicin is preferred.
- Do not give intrathecal methotrexate before 20 weeks.
- Avoid elective termination for stage-I melanoma or thyroid cancer: no survival advantage has been shown.
- Delay radiotherapy until postpartum for breast conservation; chemotherapy may be given between surgery and postpartum radiation.

## Key Concepts
- Most common cancers in pregnancy: breast, cervical, leukemia, lymphoma, thyroid, melanoma. Distribution matches that of young premenopausal women.
- Pregnancy does not alter the natural course of most cancers, and survival is comparable to age- and stage-matched non-pregnant women. Exceptions and cautions: breast cancer diagnosed within 1 year of delivery has a worse prognosis; pregnancy may be associated with metastatic presentation in melanoma, though survival is not decreased when matched for thickness and Clark level.
- Breast cancer: 15% of breast cancers occur in women of childbearing age. Pregnant women are more often stage II (74% versus 37%), largely from diagnostic delay. Mammography gives only 0.4 rads to the fetus and is not the deterrent; ultrasound is more accurate for palpable masses. MRI requires gadolinium and is not recommended. Core needle biopsy is preferred; cytology can be falsely positive because of the proliferative breast state. Most tumors are estrogen-receptor negative, as in premenopausal women.
- Breast staging: bilateral mammography; chest x-ray with shielding (0.06 mrad); abdominal ultrasound for liver, since alkaline phosphatase is physiologically elevated. Bone scan only if symptomatic or advanced disease (10 rather than 20 mCi Tc-99m MDP, Foley catheter, hydration, doubled imaging time); bony metastasis risk in stage I–II disease is 3–7%. PET is postpartum. WB-DWI/MRI detects about 80% of skeletal metastases.
- Breast surgery: modified radical mastectomy or breast conservation with axillary or sentinel node dissection at any gestational age; avoid supine position after 20 weeks; intraoperative fetal monitoring at or after 24 weeks. Sentinel node biopsy with Tc-99m sulfur colloid is safe (500–600 mCi, half-life 6 hours, stays at injection site); avoid blue dyes (0.5% anaphylaxis); indocyanine green is safe.
- Breast chemotherapy: AC or EC every 3 weeks is standard; 5-fluorouracil adds no survival benefit. Taxanes should be offered if anthracycline-based chemotherapy is completed before 32 weeks; paclitaxel is preferred over docetaxel. Tamoxifen is postponed until postpartum.
- Hodgkin lymphoma: ABVD is safe; BEACOPP is not recommended in pregnancy. Early-stage IA/IB or IIA non-mediastinal disease diagnosed in the third trimester may be deferred to postpartum. If treatment is needed in the first trimester, consider single-agent vinblastine or corticosteroids, then a full regimen in the second and third trimesters.
- Non-Hodgkin lymphoma: do not surgically remove breast or ovarian masses after biopsy confirms NHL; they respond to systemic chemotherapy. Asymptomatic indolent NHL (follicular, MALT) is observed; rituximab is used if symptoms occur.
- Acute leukemia: without treatment, maternal death may occur within 2 months. Chemotherapy during pregnancy gives higher maternal and fetal/neonatal survival than postponing treatment. Complete remission is achieved in 75%. Cytarabine and 6-thioguanine should be avoided in the first trimester if possible. ATRA is used for acute promyelocytic leukemia to control DIC but is contraindicated in the first trimester; daunorubicin is preferred over idarubicin. Arsenic trioxide is teratogenic in the first trimester.
- Chronic myeloid leukemia: discontinue imatinib during pregnancy; most patients regain remission postpartum. Leukapheresis is a temporizing measure for severe leukocytosis. If treatment beyond the first trimester is needed, imatinib is preferred over dasatinib or nilotinib.
- Melanoma: maximum normal nevus size change in pregnancy is 1 mm; ABCD signs and itching still warrant biopsy. About 100 cases of placenta or fetal metastasis are reported across all tumor types, 31% in maternal melanoma. If placental melanoma is found, follow the neonate for 1 year with frequent skin examinations.
- Ovarian cancer: usually early stage because of routine ultrasound. Laparoscopy is preferred over laparotomy; unilateral salpingo-oophorectomy is preferred over cystectomy for suspicious masses. Restage postpartum if the pelvic peritoneum and pouch of Douglas cannot be examined. Carboplatin is preferred over cisplatin except for germ cell tumors, where cisplatin is standard; cisplatin carries dose-dependent ototoxicity in exposed children.
- Cervical cancer: 1 in 2200 pregnancies; prognosis comparable to non-pregnant women. Cytobrush Papanicolaou is safe. Radical trachelectomy during pregnancy carries too high a risk of maternal and fetal complications. Pelvic lymphadenectomy under 22 weeks is safe with good oncologic and obstetric outcomes. Delaying hysterectomy 6–17 weeks for stage IA2–IB2 improves fetal outcome without worsening maternal survival. For stage IB3 and higher diagnosed before 18 weeks, immediate chemoradiotherapy with the fetus in situ is recommended; spontaneous miscarriage usually follows. Cesarean with classical incision plus radical hysterectomy is advised; microinvasion is not a contraindication to vaginal delivery. Episiotomy-site recurrence has been reported after vaginal delivery.
- Thyroid cancer: papillary is most common. Differentiated cancer diagnosed after 12 weeks can be treated postpartum, with surgery within 1 year of diagnosis; start levothyroxine to keep TSH 0.1–1 mU/L. Before 12 weeks, subtotal thyroidectomy is recommended. Medullary or anaplastic cancer should not be delayed: total thyroidectomy with lymph node dissection; radiotherapy if the airway is compromised.
- Chemotherapy toxicity: transient neonatal neutropenia and lymphopenia occur if delivery is within 3–4 weeks of chemotherapy; leukopenia 3.0% and neutropenia 7.4% in 135 exposed children. Ondansetron, metoclopramide, granisetron, and diphenhydramine are safe antiemetics. Hydrocortisone and prednisolone are preferred over dexamethasone or betamethasone because they are extensively metabolized in the placenta; repeated betamethasone is associated with attention problems and cerebral palsy.
- Growth: SGA 21% in the largest cohort, highest in hematologic cancers; odds ratio 3.12 for platinum agents and 2.07 for taxanes. Ultrasound every 2 weeks during antenatal chemotherapy to monitor growth and amniotic fluid.
- Chemotherapy can cause preterm contractions; admit and use tocolytics liberally, avoiding delivery at the nadir.
- Filgrastim or pegfilgrastim may be used once neutropenia is demonstrated, not prophylactically.
- Long-term outcomes: 84 children followed a mean of 18 years had normal growth, development, neurologic function, and school performance; the INCIP cohort at 6 years showed lower verbal IQ and visuospatial long-term memory and higher diastolic blood pressure, all within normal ranges. Follow-up until adulthood is recommended. Prior childhood cancer treatment does not increase congenital or chromosomal anomalies; prior irradiation lowers birth weight, and pelvic irradiation is associated with miscarriage, preterm birth, low birth weight, and placenta accreta.
- Cancer survivors treated with left-sided chest radiation or anthracyclines (daunorubicin, doxorubicin, idarubicin, epirubicin, mitoxantrone) or trastuzumab need cardiac evaluation before pregnancy, including baseline echocardiogram. Offspring of survivors are not at increased cancer risk unless the cancer was part of an inherited syndrome such as retinoblastoma.
- Pregnancy does not affect recurrence risk for any cancer except possibly gestational trophoblastic disease.

## Anti-patterns
- Attributing a breast mass, mole change, or vaginal bleeding to pregnancy and delaying biopsy.
- Using pre-pregnancy or ideal body weight to dose chemotherapy.
- Continuing chemotherapy beyond 34/35 weeks or delivering within 3 weeks of a cycle: neonatal myelosuppression.
- Elective preterm delivery before 34/35 weeks to fit chemotherapy scheduling.
- Offering NIPT in a woman with known cancer.
- Using trastuzumab, idarubicin, or first-trimester intrathecal methotrexate.
- Removing breast or ovarian masses surgically when lymphoma is confirmed.
- Using repeated betamethasone courses for fetal lung maturity in a patient already receiving steroids for chemotherapy.
- Assuming cancer itself requires cesarean delivery: obstetric management rarely changes, except for cervical or vulvar cancer.
- Omitting placental pathology.

## Key Takeaways
1. Diagnose without delay, stage with the least radiation that answers the question, and never attribute symptoms to pregnancy.
2. Start chemotherapy after the first trimester, dose by actual pregnancy weight, and stop by 34/35 weeks with ≥3 weeks before delivery.
3. Deliver at ≥37 weeks unless obstetric or oncologic indications dictate otherwise.
4. Use anthracycline-based regimens (AC/EC), paclitaxel if a taxane is needed, ABVD for Hodgkin disease, and ATRA for APL after the first trimester.
5. Monitor fetal growth every 2 weeks during chemotherapy and treat preterm contractions aggressively.
6. Send the placenta for pathology and check a neonatal complete blood count after in utero chemotherapy.
7. Screen cancer survivors for cardiac toxicity before pregnancy.
