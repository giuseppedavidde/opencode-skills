# Chapter 24: Intimate Partner Violence and Trauma Informed Care in Pregnancy

## Core Idea
Intimate partner violence (IPV) is any behavior within an intimate relationship that causes physical, psychological, or sexual harm, and its risk rises during pregnancy. Screening must be universal, repeated each trimester, and done without the partner or family present, because many survivors do not disclose spontaneously. Trauma informed care attends to past and ongoing trauma, rebuilds a sense of control, and avoids re-traumatization. Unplanned pregnancy is the single greatest modifiable risk factor, and IPV is associated with maternal death, perinatal death, preterm birth, abruption, and low birth weight.

## Recommendations and Grades
The chapter states no formal evidence grades; recommendations are narrative.
- Screen every pregnant patient for IPV each trimester, verbally or with a validated written tool, and document the result in the prenatal record.
- Screen without the partner or any family member present, and use framing statements that explain why the questions are routine.
- Apply the four Rs of trauma informed care: Realize the prevalence and impact of trauma, Recognize its signs, Respond by integrating trauma knowledge into practice, and Resist re-traumatization.
- Provide care guided by six principles: safety; trustworthiness and transparency; peer support; collaboration and mutuality; empowerment and choice; and attention to culture, history, and gender.
- Never confront the perpetrator and never instruct a patient to leave; both increase danger.
- Help the survivor build a safety plan, offer choices, and refer to psychological, relocation, or legal support if the patient agrees.
- Consider home visitation and behavior counseling as evidence-based adjuncts.
- Report violence when state law requires it, typically when a deadly weapon is used.
- Discuss the benefits and risks of early epidural anesthesia, because exam-related pain can be anticipated.
- Train lactation staff to avoid breast manipulation that could re-traumatize, and allow flexibility in encouraging breastfeeding.
- Offer postpartum or post-abortion contraception while avoiding reproductive coercion.
- Individualize additional antepartum visits and testing, since extra appointments can create fear or blame at home.
- Consider the possibility of IPV in every pregnant person, including LGBTQIAA relationships where rates may be higher.

## Key Concepts
- Epidemiology: outside pregnancy, 20-75% of women have experienced at least one emotionally abusive act and 13-61% report physical violence by a partner. In pregnancy, rates are estimated as high as 20%, more than 5% worldwide per the World Health Organization, and about 50% of affected women report their first episode during pregnancy. Anonymous telephone surveys show higher rates than clinic surveys.
- Screening benefit: a meta-analysis of 13 trials and more than 14,000 women found no harm from screening, and pregnant women may be more likely to disclose IPV than non-pregnant women. Screened women experienced less depression and better quality of life.
- Validated tools: HARK (Humiliation, Afraid, Rape, Kick) has 81% sensitivity and 95% specificity; HITS has 86-96% sensitivity and 91-99% specificity; E-HITS adds a sexual violence frequency item; the Partner Violence Screen has 49-71% sensitivity and 80-94% specificity; the Woman Abuse Screening Tool has 89-96% sensitivity but only 55% specificity.
- Signs: absence from or lateness to prenatal care; persistent pain unexplained by a condition; anxiety and depression, since up to 60% of patients with depression have a history of IPV; marks on the neck, facial petechiae, and neck swelling; hoarseness, sore throat, respiratory disturbance, or neck injury; dissociation or extreme anxiety during pelvic examination.
- Strangulation: petechiae and head or neck injuries may reflect strangulation, which is life-threatening. Injuries to the face, neck, and head have a higher association with IPV than injuries to the upper extremities, though defensive forearm injuries occur.
- Risk factors: societal factors include traditional gender roles, structural violence such as systemic racism, xenophobia, homophobia, and transphobia, and acceptance of violence for conflict resolution. Community factors include poverty, poor social cohesion, and weak social capital. Relationship and individual factors also contribute. Unplanned pregnancy is the single greatest modifiable risk factor, and it may result from reproductive coercion or sexual assault.
- Cycle of violence: a tension-building phase, a battering phase, and a honeymoon phase in which the perpetrator justifies behavior or blames the victim. Mutual IPV occurs and does not always follow this pattern.
- Complications: maternal death rates of 0.97-10.6 per 100,000 live births; homicide risk may be highest in the first trimester; perinatal death odds ratio 3.18 (95% CI 1.88-5.38), with 60% of these deaths attributed to IPV exposure; miscarriage, preterm labor, preterm birth, placental abruption, low birth weight, and uterine rupture are all increased.
- Psychological therapy: a Cochrane review of 33 trials in non-pregnant women found psychological therapies probably reduce depression and may reduce anxiety; body-based modalities such as mindful breathing also help in labor.
- Resources include Futures without Violence, the National Coalition against Domestic Violence, the National Network to End Domestic Violence, the National Resource Center on Domestic Violence, the Office on Violence against Women, the National Domestic Violence Hotline at 1-800-799-7233, and the RAINN hotline at 1-800-656-4673.
- Primary prevention requires societal change: gender equality, reduced systemic violence, systems to monitor violence against women, and use of reproductive health services as entry points for identification and referral.

## Anti-patterns
- Do not screen in front of a partner or relative; disclosure becomes unsafe.
- Do not tell a patient to leave the relationship; this increases danger.
- Do not confront the alleged perpetrator.
- Do not add appointments or tests without discussing risk and benefit, because they can provoke blame or fear at home.
- Do not coerce contraception or pregnancy decisions; reproductive coercion is itself a form of IPV.
- Do not assume the absence of visible injury excludes IPV.

## Key Takeaways
- Ask every patient each trimester, alone, using a validated tool such as HARK or HITS.
- Treat IPV as a common pregnancy complication with substantial maternal and perinatal mortality.
- Build a safety plan and refer, rather than advising separation.
- Apply trauma informed principles to pelvic exams, anesthesia planning, and lactation support.
- Address unplanned pregnancy as the main modifiable risk factor, while avoiding reproductive coercion.
