# Chapter 16: Von Willebrand Disease

## Core Idea
Von Willebrand disease (VWD) is the most common inherited bleeding disorder, affecting 0.1-1% of the population, and results from quantitative (types 1 and 3) or qualitative (type 2) defects in von Willebrand factor (vWF), which mediates platelet adhesion and carries factor VIII. Diagnosis in pregnancy is difficult because vWF and factor VIII rise progressively and may be normal at term, so the workup must be repeated and interpreted against pregnancy ranges. Type 2 patients raise dysfunctional antigen without raising activity, and type 3 patients have no rise at all. Management aims to keep vWF, factor VIII, and ristocetin cofactor activity at or above 50 IU/dL through delivery and for 5 days postpartum, because primary and secondary postpartum hemorrhage are the main complications.

## Recommendations and Grades
The chapter states no formal evidence grades; recommendations are narrative.
- Test vWF antigen, factor VIII, ristocetin cofactor activity or von Willebrand activity, and bleeding time; add multimeric analysis and factor VIII binding assay to assign type.
- Test desmopressin (DDAVP) responsiveness before conception, or in the second or third trimester.
- Obtain hematology consultation and offer genetic counseling; refer type 1 with factor VIII below 50 IU/dL, types 2 or 3, or severe bleeding history to a high-risk center.
- Target vWF, factor VIII, and ristocetin cofactor of at least 50 IU/dL, which is associated with lower bleeding risk.
- Give DDAVP 0.3 mcg/kg intravenously over 30 minutes (maximum 25-30 mcg), or 0.3 mcg/kg subcutaneously, or 300 mcg intranasally above 50 kg and 150 mcg below 50 kg, for type 1.
- Use vWF/factor VIII concentrates such as alphanate or humate-P, preferred over cryoprecipitate, with a loading dose of 40-60 IU/kg when DDAVP fails or for types 2A, 2N, and 3.
- Avoid DDAVP in type 2B; it exacerbates thrombocytopenia. Give platelet transfusion to a count above 50 x 10^9/L plus antifibrinolytic therapy instead.
- Give prophylaxis about 1 hour before delivery and again 12 hours after when levels are below 50 IU/dL; treat type 3 daily starting before delivery.
- Use oxytocin with misoprostol 400 mcg buccally, oxytocin with methergine, or carbetocin, all more effective than oxytocin alone; consider tranexamic acid 1 g intravenously or 10 mg/kg.
- Avoid fetal scalp electrode, fetal scalp sampling, pudendal block, episiotomy, and operative vaginal delivery when possible, because the fetus has up to a 50% chance of VWD.
- Consider neuraxial anesthesia for types 1 and 3 only when factor VIII, vWF, and ristocetin cofactor are at least 50 IU/dL and the aPTT is normal.
- Continue close monitoring of factor VIII and ristocetin cofactor for up to 2 weeks postpartum, especially after cesarean delivery, and use oral antifibrinolytics for delayed bleeding.
- Avoid nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs and delay neonatal circumcision until VWD status is known.

## Key Concepts
- Physiology: vWF is a multimeric glycoprotein encoded on the short arm of chromosome 12 and released by endothelium and megakaryocytes; over 250 mutations are known. aPTT is usually normal and prolonged only in severe VWD with low factor VIII.
- Type 1: over 80% of cases, autosomal dominant, partial quantitative deficiency with vWF and factor VIII reduced (normal 50-150 IU/dL).
- Type 2: 15-20%, autosomal dominant, qualitative defect. The activity-to-antigen ratio is below 0.5-0.7, whereas type 1 has a ratio above 0.5-0.7. Low-dose ristocetin-induced platelet aggregation is positive only in type 2B.
- Type 3: under 5%, autosomal recessive, undetectable vWF and ristocetin cofactor with markedly low factor VIII; the most severe form and no response to DDAVP.
- Pregnancy: vWF and factor VIII rise progressively and can normalize at term. Type 2 shows rising dysfunctional antigen without rising activity. Type 3 shows no rise.
- DDAVP releases vWF and factor VIII within 30-60 minutes, with high levels lasting 6-8 hours. It does not cross the placenta and is category B.
- Hemorrhage: risk of both primary (within 24 hours) and secondary (after 24 hours) postpartum hemorrhage is increased. VWD does not impair fertility or increase pregnancy loss.
- Postpartum: vWF and factor VIII return to baseline 1-3 weeks after delivery, so doses may need repeating.
- Type 2B carries additional morbidity from severe thrombocytopenia and from both primary and secondary hemorrhage.
- Platelet disorders: Glanzmann thrombasthenia is a congenital defect of glycoprotein IIb/IIIa with abnormal aggregation, prolonged bleeding time, and normal platelet count.
- Antepartum testing is not indicated in VWD because there is no direct fetal risk. Chorionic villus sampling and amniocentesis are possible with DDAVP or concentrate prophylaxis.

## Anti-patterns
- Do not diagnose VWD from a single set of levels in pregnancy; physiologic rises mask the defect.
- Do not give DDAVP in type 2B; it worsens thrombocytopenia.
- Do not use cryoprecipitate as first choice when concentrates are available; infectious disease risk is the stated reason.
- Do not combine DDAVP with hypotonic intravenous fluids or unmonitored oxytocin; both cause fluid retention and life-threatening hyponatremia.
- Do not perform operative vaginal delivery, fetal scalp sampling, or pudendal block without need when the fetus may be affected.
- Do not prescribe nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs postpartum.

## Key Takeaways
- Repeat vWF and factor VIII testing in the third trimester; term levels may look normal.
- Keep all three clotting measures at 50 IU/dL or above through labor, delivery, and 5 days postpartum.
- Choose DDAVP for type 1 responders and concentrates for type 2A, 2N, and 3; never DDAVP for type 2B.
- Expect and prevent both early and late postpartum hemorrhage, monitoring up to 2 weeks.
- Plan delivery with anesthesia review, avoid invasive fetal procedures, and delay circumcision.
