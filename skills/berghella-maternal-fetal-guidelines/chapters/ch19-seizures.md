# Chapter 19: Seizures

## Core Idea
Epilepsy affects 0.3-0.7% of pregnancies and about 1.5 million US women of childbearing age. For most women seizure frequency is unchanged in pregnancy, and status epilepticus is rare. The treatment principle is the antiepileptic drug (AED) that best controls seizures, used as monotherapy at the lowest effective dose, because uncontrolled generalized tonic-clonic seizures harm both mother and fetus. Valproate is the most teratogenic agent and should be avoided when possible. Seizure freedom for at least 9 months before pregnancy predicts continued freedom in 84-92% of women.

## Recommendations and Grades
- Counsel every woman with epilepsy before conception about AED teratogenicity and neurodevelopmental delay, folic acid, possible seizure change in pregnancy, adherence, AED level monitoring, inheritance, and breastfeeding.
- Give folic acid 0.4-4 mg daily, usually 2-4 mg, before conception and continue through pregnancy.
- Defer conception until seizures are well controlled on the minimum effective dose, preferably as monotherapy.
- Consider tapering and stopping AEDs if the woman has been seizure-free more than 2 years with a normal electroencephalogram, and observe 6-12 months off medication before conception.
- Do not stop or change an AED during pregnancy solely to reduce teratogenicity.
- Avoid carbamazepine, phenobarbital, primidone, phenytoin, valproate, and topiramate when possible; all are US Food and Drug Administration category D. Levetiracetam and lamotrigine are common pregnancy choices, though other safe options exist.
- Prefer monotherapy at the lowest effective dose, and spread the daily dose into smaller doses to avoid high peak levels.
- Monitor lamotrigine, carbamazepine, and phenytoin levels during pregnancy, and consider monitoring levetiracetam and oxcarbazepine; free levels are available for carbamazepine, valproic acid, phenobarbital, and phenytoin.
- If valproate is unavoidable, avoid plasma levels above 70 micrograms/mL and give divided doses three or four times daily.
- Perform first-trimester dating ultrasound, anatomy scan at 11-13 or 16-20 weeks, maternal alpha-fetoprotein at 15-18 weeks (up to 21 weeks), fetal echocardiography at about 22 weeks, and amniocentesis for alpha-fetoprotein and acetylcholinesterase if indicated.
- Continue AEDs during labor at the usual times, using intravenous formulations if oral intake is impossible.
- Give the neonate vitamin K 1 mg intramuscularly at birth; the benefit of prenatal maternal vitamin K is unknown.
- Encourage breastfeeding, but monitor for sedation with low-protein-bound AEDs and avoid extended-release formulations.
- Monitor AED levels through the eighth postpartum week and adjust doses.
- Prescribe hormonal contraception containing at least 50 micrograms of ethinyl estradiol to women taking enzyme-inducing AEDs.
- Grade note: the chapter cites class II studies for the absence of a substantial increase in cesarean delivery, late pregnancy bleeding, and preterm labor with AED use, and for the 84-92% seizure-freedom figure; pre-eclampsia, gestational hypertension, spontaneous abortion, change in seizure frequency, and status epilepticus risk are supported only by insufficient evidence or class IV studies.

## Key Concepts
- Definitions: epilepsy is at least two unprovoked seizures more than 24 hours apart, or one unprovoked seizure with a recurrence risk similar to that after two seizures over 10 years. The history is the most important diagnostic tool; electroencephalography poses no fetal risk and magnetic resonance imaging is more sensitive than computed tomography.
- Malformation risk: AED-exposed infants have a 4-8% risk of major congenital malformation, about twice the 2-3% baseline; offspring of treated women have 5% versus 3% in untreated epilepsy. Reported defects are neural tube, cardiac, craniofacial, and digital.
- North American AED Pregnancy Registry rates of major malformation: valproate 9.3%, phenobarbital 5.6%, topiramate 5.1%, carbamazepine 2.9%, phenytoin 2.6%, lamotrigine 2.3%, levetiracetam 2.3%.
- Valproate: associated with neural tube defects, oral clefts, hypospadias, cardiac malformations, and poor cognitive outcome, with a four- to fivefold increase in early neurodevelopmental disorders that persists at low doses and with second- and third-trimester exposure.
- Lamotrigine: associated with facial clefts, with the lowest major malformation rates below 300 mg daily. Clearance rises up to 94% by the third trimester, so frequent dose adjustment is needed.
- Obstetric outcomes: fetal loss 1.3-14%, perinatal death 1.3-7.8%, low birth weight 7-10%, preterm birth 4-11%. More than one generalized tonic-clonic seizure in pregnancy raises preterm birth risk about fivefold. Smoking with epilepsy possibly adds a substantial preterm birth risk.
- Other fetal risks: generalized tonic-clonic seizures cause maternal hypoxia, acidosis, and blunt trauma, and generalized seizures during labor can alter fetal heart rate.
- Mortality: a retrospective population-based study found a tenfold increase in mortality during pregnancy in women with epilepsy, mostly sudden unexpected death in epilepsy, so complete seizure control matters.
- Pharmacokinetics: hepatic cytochrome P450 induction and higher renal clearance lower some AED concentrations, while reduced protein binding raises free active drug and can cause toxicity.
- Postpartum: most AED pharmacokinetics return to prepregnancy values within 10-14 days; breastfeeding transfer is lower with highly protein-bound drugs, and repeated exposure can accumulate because infant elimination is immature.
- Counseling points: driving privileges are suspended for several months after a seizure, showers replace baths, avoid heights and heavy machinery, and avoid carrying the baby in front or back carriers.

## Anti-patterns
- Do not stop or switch AEDs abruptly; withdrawal can precipitate seizures.
- Do not change AEDs during pregnancy only to lower teratogenic risk.
- Do not use valproate as first choice in a woman who may conceive.
- Do not assume AED levels measured as total drug reflect free active drug when protein binding is low.
- Do not use extended-release AED formulations while breastfeeding.
- Do not rely on sub-50-microgram estrogen contraceptives in women taking enzyme-inducing AEDs.

## Key Takeaways
- Optimize to monotherapy at the lowest effective dose at least 6 months before conception.
- Give folic acid 2-4 mg daily before conception and through pregnancy.
- Monitor AED levels during pregnancy and to the eighth postpartum week, expecting large clearance changes with lamotrigine.
- Screen for neural tube and cardiac defects with alpha-fetoprotein, detailed ultrasound, and fetal echocardiography at 22 weeks.
- Continue AEDs through labor and encourage breastfeeding with infant monitoring for sedation.
