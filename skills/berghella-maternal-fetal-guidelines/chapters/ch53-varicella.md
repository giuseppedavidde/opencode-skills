# Chapter 53: Varicella

## Core Idea
About 95% of pregnant women are immune to varicella zoster virus (VZV), so primary chickenpox complicates only 0.5–3 per 1000 pregnancies. The two hazards are maternal varicella pneumonia, which occurs in up to 5–10% of pregnant women with chickenpox, and congenital varicella syndrome, which occurs in 0.4–2% of maternal infections and almost always when infection occurs before 20 weeks. Management is post-exposure passive immunization of seronegative women, early oral acyclovir for chickenpox, and careful timing of delivery around maternal viraemia.

## Recommendations and Grades
- Do not perform routine serologic screening of all pregnant women. Instead ask at the first prenatal visit about prior chickenpox; test VZV IgG in women who did not have chickenpox, are unsure, or had only one vaccine dose. Grade: not stated.
- Offer postpartum vaccination to seronegative women: two doses one month apart, safe in breastfeeding, with conception delayed one month after vaccination because it is a live attenuated vaccine. Grade: not stated.
- Give varicella zoster immune globulin (VariZIG™) to VZV-seronegative pregnant women as soon as possible after exposure and within 96 hours (4 days), up to 10 days post-exposure. Grade: not stated.
- Give oral acyclovir to pregnant women who develop chickenpox, within 24 hours of rash: 800 mg five times daily for 7 days; or valacyclovir 1 g three times daily for 7 days. Grade: not stated.
- Delay delivery until 5 days after the onset of maternal illness to allow passive transfer of IgG; Table 53.1 states delay until 5–7 days after rash onset if possible. Grade: not stated.
- Give VZV IgG to neonates born to women who develop chickenpox between 5 days before and 2 days after delivery. If neonatal infection occurs, give acyclovir. Grade: not stated.
- Hospitalize pregnant women with varicella pneumonia in isolation and give IV acyclovir 10–15 mg/kg every 8 hours for 7 days, started within 72 hours of symptoms, which decreases severity and mortality. Grade: not stated.
- Allow at least 5 weeks between onset of maternal symptoms and fetal ultrasound when assessing for congenital varicella syndrome. Grade: not stated.

## Key Concepts
- Incidence: 0.5–3/1000 pregnancies; women from tropical areas have only about 50% immunity. The vaccine, licensed in 1995, reduced disease incidence by 85–90% in the following decade.
- Risk factors for varicella pneumonia in pregnancy: cigarette smoking, more than 100 skin lesions, and advanced gestational age at infection.
- Congenital varicella syndrome: 0.4–2% of maternal infections; the risk for rash in the first 20 weeks is 0.5–2% above baseline, rare at 21–28 weeks, and none after 28 weeks. Only 130 cases were reported worldwide from 1947 to 2013.
- Clinical neonatal findings of congenital varicella syndrome: dermatomal skin scarring 73%, neurological abnormalities 62%, eye defects 52%, limb hypoplasia 46%, muscle hypoplasia 20%, gastrointestinal abnormalities 19%, genitourinary abnormalities 12%, internal organ effects 13%, developmental delay 12%.
- Screening yield: more than 97% of women who report typical prior varicella are IgG positive; of women with uncertain or negative histories, 80–90% have VZV IgG.
- Diagnosis: usually clinical. Confirm with newly positive VZV IgM by ELISA, or VZV antigen in vesicular lesions by immunofluorescence. Fetal infection can be shown by VZV DNA on amniotic fluid PCR, but this does not predict congenital varicella syndrome.
- Incubation period about 15 days (range 10–21). Nonimmune health workers exposed to VZV should minimize patient contact from days 8 to 21 post-contact.
- Neonatal timing risk: infants delivered when maternal symptoms developed 5 days before to 2 days after delivery have a 17–30% risk of neonatal varicella, and about 7% of these die.
- Neonatal exposure in the first 7 days of life (for example from an infected sibling): no intervention if the mother is immune; give VZV IgG if she is not. Neonates who develop chickenpox in the first 14 days of life should receive IV acyclovir.
- Maternal shingles (herpes zoster): no cases of congenital varicella syndrome have been reported, and maternal shingles is not a risk to the infant because of passively acquired maternal antibody. When treatment is needed (moderate to severe rash, acute neuritis), use oral acyclovir 800 mg five times daily for 7–10 days or valacyclovir 1000 mg three times daily for 7 days.
- Acyclovir is not teratogenic in the reported data; transplacental passage is prompt. High-dose IV therapy is 15 mg/kg or 500 mg/m² every 8 hours. There is no evidence that acyclovir or valacyclovir reduces the already low risk of congenital varicella syndrome.

## Anti-patterns
- Routine serologic screening of every pregnancy: selective history-based serotesting with postpartum vaccination is cost-effective.
- Giving VariZIG after chickenpox has developed: it has no therapeutic effect once disease is present.
- Elective delivery during maternal viraemia: it removes the chance of passive antibody transfer.
- Relying on amniotic fluid VZV DNA to predict congenital varicella syndrome: it does not.
- Vaccinating during pregnancy or conceiving within one month of vaccination: the vaccine is live attenuated.

## Key Takeaways
- Ask about chickenpox at booking; test only those with negative or uncertain history; vaccinate susceptibles postpartum.
- Give VariZIG within 96 hours of exposure, and up to 10 days.
- Start oral acyclovir within 24 hours of the rash.
- Delay delivery to 5 days after rash onset when possible, and give VariZIG to the neonate if born in the 5-days-before to 2-days-after window.
- Admit and treat varicella pneumonia promptly with IV acyclovir; it is the main maternal threat.
