# Chapter 23: Substance Use Disorders

## Core Idea
Substance use disorder (SUD) is a chronic medical illness, and continued use in pregnancy is usually a response to psychological or physical need rather than intent to harm the fetus. Reported use in pregnancy is 5.8%, with a range of 0.4-27%, and polysubstance use with tobacco and alcohol is common. Universal screening with a validated tool, brief intervention, and referral to treatment is the recommended pathway. Opioid agonist therapy with methadone or buprenorphine is the standard of care for opioid use disorder and is safer than detoxification, which carries a 23-53% relapse risk. No pharmacotherapy is approved for cocaine, marijuana, or amphetamine dependence.

## Recommendations and Grades
The chapter states no formal evidence grades; recommendations are narrative.
- Screen every pregnant patient for illicit drugs, tobacco, and alcohol using the 4 Ps (Parents, Partner, Past, Present); any yes answer triggers further questions. Screening is not diagnostic.
- Perform urine drug testing only with consent, and never use a urine screen to diagnose SUD or its severity.
- Use the SBIRT model: screening, brief intervention, and referral to treatment.
- Counsel about state law, because 23 states and the District of Columbia treat substance use in pregnancy as child abuse, 25 states and the District of Columbia require mandatory reporting, and 8 states require testing when use is suspected.
- Prefer prevention and preconception behavior change over treating established use during pregnancy; encourage contraception until treatment is established.
- Treat opioid use disorder with methadone or buprenorphine maintenance rather than detoxification.
- Titrate methadone to the lowest dose that prevents withdrawal and craving, starting at 10-30 mg and increasing by 5-10 mg; doses of at least 60 mg are more effective than lower doses.
- Continue or start buprenorphine, which has fewer drug interactions and does not require daily supervised dosing.
- Do not use naltrexone routinely in pregnancy; evidence is limited to retrospective data.
- Manage withdrawal with the Clinical Opiate Withdrawal Scale and treat overdose with naloxone after securing the airway.
- Avoid abrupt benzodiazepine discontinuation; taper with a long-acting agent because of seizure, autonomic instability, and suicidal ideation risk.
- Encourage breastfeeding in women taking methadone or buprenorphine; it may reduce neonatal opioid withdrawal.

## Key Concepts
- Epidemiology: in the 2019 National Survey on Drug Use and Health, 57.2 million Americans (20.8%) used illicit drugs in the past year and 35.8 million in the past month; 8.3 million had a diagnosed illicit drug use disorder. Use is lower in females (18%) than males (23.8%).
- Opioid withdrawal: symptoms begin within 4-6 hours for short-acting opioids and later for methadone, with cramps, restlessness, insomnia, diarrhea, tachypnea, hypertension, lacrimation, piloerection, and craving. The Clinical Opiate Withdrawal Scale scores 5-12 as mild, 13-24 moderate, 25-36 moderately severe, and above 36 severe.
- Neonatal opioid withdrawal occurs in about 60% of exposed neonates (range 26-95%).
- Detoxification evidence: a meta-analysis of 14 studies and 1,097 pregnancies found only one fetal loss in 888 pregnancies during detoxification; a second meta-analysis of 15 studies and 1,126 pregnancies found a 1.24% loss rate with detoxification versus 1.95% in comparison groups. Relapse averages 35% (range 23-53%), and 23-34% discontinue treatment, so maintenance is preferred.
- MOTHER trial: neonatal treatment for withdrawal was 57% with methadone versus 47% with buprenorphine (not significant), but buprenorphine needed less morphine (1.1 versus 10.4 mg), shorter treatment (4.1 versus 9.9 days), and shorter hospital stay (10.0 versus 17.5 days).
- Methadone in pregnancy: plasma levels fall and clearance rises, so dose increases are often needed in the third trimester; symptomatic patients had mean trough levels of 0.18 mg/L versus above 0.3 mg/L in asymptomatic patients. Metronidazole and other drugs can raise methadone levels.
- Marijuana: the most commonly used illicit drug in pregnancy, used by 2-5%; state-level self-report was 4.2% in 2017 and urine testing detects more than twice as many users. Use rose 62% between 2002 and 2014, and 18.1% of users meet abuse or dependence criteria.
- Marijuana outcomes: low birth weight odds ratio 1.77 (95% CI 1.04-3.01), preterm birth 12% versus 6.1% in a cohort of over 9,000 users (adjusted relative risk 1.41), stillbirth relative risk 1.74 in one meta-analysis, and anencephaly odds ratio 2.5 with first-trimester use. There is no withdrawal syndrome, though tremor and irritability are reported.
- Marijuana mechanisms: tetrahydrocannabinol binds cannabinoid receptors present in the fetal brain by 14 weeks and alters synaptogenesis, with reported attention and visuoperceptual deficits into adolescence. Medicinal marijuana should not be prescribed in pregnancy.
- Cocaine: crosses the placenta causing vasoconstriction and reduced uteroplacental blood flow. Overall malformation rate is 10%; cocaine alone carries relative risk 1.7 and with other drugs 2.10. Preterm birth odds ratio 3.38, abruption relative risk 4.55, premature rupture of membranes 1.85, birth weight lower by 492 g, low birth weight odds ratio 3.66, small for gestational age 3.23, microcephaly 16%, and fourfold higher vertical HIV transmission.
- Cocaine neonatal toxicity: tremulousness, high-pitched cry, irritability, hyperalertness, and autonomic instability in the first 2-3 days; no approved pharmacotherapy exists.
- Amphetamines: methamphetamine use in pregnancy is 0.1-1.0% and up to 5.2% in high-prevalence areas; ecstasy use in the United Kingdom ranged 0.6-8.8%. Methamphetamine is associated with gastroschisis, and the UK teratology service reported a 15% malformation rate after ecstasy exposure versus 2-3% expected.
- Benzodiazepines: global pregnancy use is 1.9%, mostly lorazepam and zolpidem. Risks include miscarriage (adjusted odds ratio 1.85), preterm birth (2.03), cesarean delivery (2.45), and low birth weight; neonates show hypoventilation, irritability, hypertonicity, and floppy infant syndrome.
- Phencyclidine causes growth restriction, low birth weight, and small for gestational age with impaired fine motor skills. LSD exposure is reported with limb reduction defects, central nervous system anomalies, and neural tube defects.
- Breastfeeding: methadone and buprenorphine are encouraged; 0.1-11% of the weight-adjusted benzodiazepine dose enters breast milk, and most are category L3.

## Anti-patterns
- Do not obtain a urine drug screen without consent, and do not treat a positive screen as a diagnosis of SUD.
- Do not discontinue benzodiazepines abruptly; seizures, autonomic instability, and suicidal ideation can follow.

## Key Takeaways
- Screen with the 4 Ps and use SBIRT, keeping the process nonjudgmental and confidential.
- Maintain opioid use disorder on methadone or buprenorphine and treat withdrawal with the Clinical Opiate Withdrawal Scale.
- Expect neonatal opioid withdrawal in about 60% and plan pediatric care.
- Counsel that marijuana is not a safe treatment for nausea of pregnancy and is linked to low birth weight and preterm birth.
- Avoid abrupt benzodiazepine cessation and encourage breastfeeding on opioid agonist therapy.
