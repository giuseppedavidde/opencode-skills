# Chapter 8: Prolactinoma

## Core Idea
Prolactinomas are lactotroph adenomas diagnosed by sustained prolactin elevation, usually >40 µg/L on two occasions with a normal non-pregnant level <20 µg/L, plus MRI evidence of adenoma. They cause infertility by suppressing gonadotropin-releasing hormone, and treatment with a dopamine agonist restores ovulation in over 90%. The main maternal risk in pregnancy is adenoma enlargement: 2–2.5% for microadenomas, 20–30% for macroadenomas without prior surgery or irradiation, and 4.7% after prior surgery or irradiation. Prolactin levels rise about 10-fold in pregnancy and are useless for monitoring; management depends on adenoma size, symptoms, and visual field testing. Bromocriptine and cabergoline are both safe for the fetus, and cabergoline is probably the drug of choice.

## Recommendations and Grades
- Diagnose with repeated prolactin >40 µg/L and MRI; exclude other causes of hyperprolactinemia and macroprolactinemia in asymptomatic women. *(III; review)*
- Treat with a dopamine agonist before conception to normalize prolactin and shrink the adenoma, and continue it until a positive pregnancy test. *(III; review)*
- Discourage pregnancy until prolactin is normal and neurologic or visual symptoms and suprasellar extension have resolved.
- Stop the dopamine agonist in pregnancy for microadenoma, especially when prolactin was normal before pregnancy and the adenoma has been stable for more than 2 years.
- Continue the dopamine agonist through pregnancy for macroadenoma; formal visual field testing every 3 months.
- Ask about headache and visual change at every visit, or at least every 3 months; perform non-contrast sellar MRI and visual field testing with neuro-ophthalmologic evaluation if symptoms appear.
- Do not measure prolactin during pregnancy; it rises physiologically about 10-fold and does not correlate with tumor growth.
- Reserve transsphenoidal surgery for maximal dopamine agonist therapy that fails to control the adenoma.
- Avoid gadolinium in pregnancy; non-contrast MRI is safe, including in the first trimester.
- Continue the dopamine agonist postpartum for macroadenoma, and for microadenoma with elevated prolactin; consider stopping in microadenoma stable >2 years with normal prolactin on low-dose therapy.
- Do not discourage breastfeeding with a microadenoma and no neurologic symptoms; if a dopamine agonist is required, advise against breastfeeding because these drugs suppress lactation.

## Key Concepts
- Microadenoma <10 mm; macroadenoma ≥10 mm. Prolactinomas are 40–60% of pituitary tumors, with prevalence 10–54 per 100,000 person-years.
- Symptoms: galactorrhea in 80%, oligomenorrhea; headache and visual field defects with macroadenoma.
- Pituitary volume more than doubles by the third trimester because estrogen drives lactotroph hyperplasia.
- Bromocriptine: start 0.625 mg at bedtime with a snack for one week, add 1.25 mg each morning for one week, then increase by 1.25 mg; usual total 5–7.5 mg daily in divided doses; may be given vaginally with fewer side effects.
- Cabergoline: start 0.25 mg twice weekly, increase monthly; usual 0.25–0.5 mg twice weekly, maximum 1 mg twice weekly.
- Macroadenoma doses: bromocriptine 7.5–10 mg daily; cabergoline 0.5–1 mg twice weekly.
- Bromocriptine normalizes prolactin in >80% of microadenomas and restores menses and fertility in >90%. About 70% of bromocriptine non-responders respond to cabergoline; about 18% are resistant to cabergoline.
- Large macroadenomas extending beyond the sella have a 23% risk of clinically significant enlargement despite dopamine agonist therapy.
- Untreated adenomas enlarge over time in about 20%; about 25% of women with microadenoma remain normoprolactinemic after stopping therapy, but most need to restart.
- Postpartum: prolactin and gadolinium-enhanced MRI at 6–8 weeks; prolactin may not normalize until 6 months. Remission occurs in 10% to almost 70%, more likely with smaller initial adenoma and normal postpartum MRI.
- Recurrence after stopping therapy is >30% for microprolactinoma and >50% for macroprolactinoma.
- Gadolinium exposure at any point in pregnancy was associated with 17.6 versus 6.9 stillbirths or neonatal deaths per 1000; first-trimester exposure alone showed no significant difference.
- Quinagolide and pergolide should not be used: not approved for hyperprolactinemia, poorly studied in pregnancy, and associated with cardiac valve defects.
- Oral estrogen-containing contraception is probably safe.

## Anti-patterns
- Checking prolactin during pregnancy to guide treatment: the physiologic rise makes values uninterpretable.
- Basing treatment on prolactin rather than symptoms and visual fields in pregnancy.
- Withholding dopamine agonist from a symptomatic pregnant woman with macroadenoma: continued therapy is standard.
- Advising pregnancy in a woman whose macroadenoma elevates the optic chiasm or is refractory to both agonists: treat and shrink it first.
- Using gadolinium contrast in pregnancy: avoid.
- Permitting breastfeeding while on a dopamine agonist: the drug suppresses lactation; advise against nursing.
- Assuming remission after pregnancy is permanent: recurrence exceeds 30–50% off therapy.
- Expecting normal menses to equal cure: recurrence of hyperprolactinemia is common and requires follow-up.

## Key Takeaways
1. Diagnose with repeated prolactin and MRI, and rule out macroprolactinemia in asymptomatic women.
2. Normalize prolactin and shrink the adenoma before conception, then stop the agonist at the pregnancy test for microadenoma and continue it for macroadenoma.
3. Monitor by symptoms and visual fields, not prolactin; use non-contrast MRI if symptoms appear.
4. Offer surgery only when maximal medical therapy fails, and prefer the second trimester if operating during pregnancy.
5. Reassess with prolactin and MRI 6–8 weeks postpartum; breastfeeding is acceptable for microadenoma if no agonist is needed.
