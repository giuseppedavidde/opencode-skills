# Chapter 22: Smoking

## Core Idea
Smoking is the most significant preventable risk factor for low birth weight, preterm birth, perinatal death, and other maternal and perinatal complications. Tobacco smoke contains more than 7,000 chemicals, and nicotine and carbon monoxide reach the fetus at concentrations about 15% above maternal levels. Behavioral counseling with support and rewards has the best evidence for cessation, while pharmacotherapy is either contraindicated or insufficiently studied in pregnancy. Women who quit by the third trimester reach birth weights similar to those of nonsmokers, but 50-60% of quitters relapse within 4 months of delivery.

## Recommendations and Grades
The chapter states no formal evidence grades; recommendations are narrative.
- Screen for tobacco at every initial and follow-up prenatal visit using a multiple-choice questionnaire, which improves disclosure by 40%.
- Ask three questions: does she smoke; if not, did she smoke in the past year; if not, does she use electronic cigarettes, hookah, snus, lozenges, chewing gum, or patches. Any yes answer requires counseling and intervention.
- Apply the 5 As (Ask, Advise, Assess, Assist, Arrange) for women willing to quit within 30 days, and the 5 Rs (Relevance, Risks, Rewards, Roadblocks, Repetition) for those unwilling.
- Give clear, personalized advice to quit at every visit; a 3-minute intervention raises abstinence rates.
- Use behavioral and educational interventions with support and reward techniques, which produced a 23% decrease in continued smoking late in pregnancy across more than 88 randomized trials and 28,000 women.
- Consider voucher-based contingency management, which raises abstinence and improves neonatal birth weight.
- Do not recommend nicotine replacement therapy as routinely effective in pregnancy; evidence for safety and efficacy is insufficient.
- Use nicotine replacement only with close supervision and after discussing the known risk of continued smoking against the possible risks of therapy.
- Do not use electronic cigarettes in pregnancy or breastfeeding; there are no trials and nicotine carries fetal risk.
- Do not prescribe bupropion, varenicline, clonidine, nortriptyline, moclobemide, selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors, or opioid antagonists for pregnancy cessation; evidence is absent or negative.
- Advise avoidance of second-hand smoke, and support partner cessation.
- Encourage breastfeeding even if the mother continues to smoke, and counsel her not to smoke at home.
- Document smoking status and any e-cigarette use in the record at each visit.

## Key Concepts
- Epidemiology: 176 million adult women smoke daily worldwide. In the United States, 13 of every 100 adult women smoked in 2019, and 7.2% of pregnancies were smoke-exposed in 2016, down from 12.3% in 2010. Pregnancy rates range from 0.1% to 50% across countries.
- Nicotine crosses the placenta; fetal levels exceed maternal levels by 15%, and amniotic fluid levels are 88% higher than plasma. Nicotine affects transmitter pathways, brain development, and later programming, with animal data linking it to diabetes, obesity, hypertension, and neurobehavioral deficits.
- Carbon monoxide crosses rapidly and forms carboxyhemoglobin, which shifts the oxyhemoglobin dissociation curve leftward. A 10% maternal carboxyhemoglobin reduces oxygen availability as much as a 60% reduction in blood flow.
- Low birth weight: relative risk 1.3-10, with a mean birth weight deficit of 200-300 g at term; up to 19% of term low birth weight is attributed to smoking.
- Preterm birth: relative risk 1.3-2.5, with 5-8% of preterm births attributable to smoking.
- Pregnancy loss relative risk 1.2-3.4; premature rupture of membranes relative risk 1.9-4.2; abruption 1.4-2.5; placenta previa 1.4-4.4; fetal death 1.2-1.4.
- Congenital anomalies: sufficient evidence for a causal link with orofacial clefts and suggestive evidence for clubfoot, gastroschisis, and atrial septal defects.
- Smoking in the second trimester is associated with a reduced incidence of pre-eclampsia by an unexplained mechanism.
- Postnatal effects: sudden infant death syndrome, respiratory infection, reactive airway disease, otitis media, bronchiolitis, short stature, hyperactivity, obesity, and poorer school performance; up to 34% of SIDS cases are attributed to smoking.
- Cessation benefit: quitting by the third trimester yields birth weights similar to nonsmokers, and cessation could prevent 19% of low birth weight and 5-8% of preterm births. Third-trimester smoking has the greatest effect on birth weight.
- Smokeless tobacco: Swedish registry data show an adjusted odds ratio of 1.6 (95% CI 1.1-2.3) for stillbirth, and Indian data show increased extremely preterm birth.
- Electronic cigarettes: non-combustible devices contain nitrosamines, diethylene glycol, and trace metals including arsenic, chromium, cadmium, nickel, and lead; nicotine content varies widely. In one survey 43% of pregnant women believed e-cigarettes are safer than cigarettes, and 29% of obstetric providers agreed. The CDC linked an outbreak of severe pulmonary disease to tetrahydrocannabinol-containing products and vitamin E acetate.
- Reward programs: serial vouchers of $68-$274, up to $548 total, produced 22.5% abstinence versus 8.6%; an $800 reward was more often accepted than a deposit-based scheme.
- Psychosocial interventions reduced preterm births and low birth weight infants by 18%.
- Nicotine replacement: in non-pregnant smokers it raises quit rates 50-70%, with 43% for 4 mg gum and 66% for the patch. In pregnancy, a 15 mg/16-hour patch added to behavioral support did not significantly increase abstinence to delivery, and 2 mg gum produced a nonsignificant increase in cessation but higher birth weight and gestational age.
- Bupropion dosing outside pregnancy: 300 mg daily in two divided doses, started 2 weeks before the quit date and continued 7-12 weeks.
- Breastfed infants of smoking mothers have urinary cotinine 50 times higher than infants of nonsmokers.

## Anti-patterns
- Do not rely on a yes/no question alone; use the multiple-choice format to improve disclosure.
- Do not recommend e-cigarettes as a harm-reduction substitute in pregnancy or lactation.
- Do not advise against breastfeeding because the mother smokes.

## Key Takeaways
- Screen for all nicotine products, including vaping and smokeless tobacco, at every visit.
- Deliver counseling with support and financial rewards; this is the best-supported intervention in pregnancy.
- Expect a birth weight gain of 200-300 g when a woman quits, with the largest benefit from quitting before the third trimester.
- Avoid pharmacotherapy unless the risk of continued smoking clearly outweighs uncertain fetal risk.
- Plan postpartum relapse prevention, because half to 60% resume smoking within 4 months.
