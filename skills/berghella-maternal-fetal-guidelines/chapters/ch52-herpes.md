# Chapter 52: Herpes

## Core Idea
Genital herpes in pregnancy matters almost entirely because of the newborn. Primary first-episode infection without prior HSV-1 or HSV-2 antibodies carries a 25–50% vertical transmission rate when delivery occurs vaginally during the episode, whereas recurrent infection carries <1%. Prevention of maternal infection, antiviral suppression from 36 weeks, and cesarean delivery for genital lesions in labor are the core measures. Universal serologic screening is not recommended because no trial has tested it and no intervention reliably prevents the already rare neonatal case.

## Recommendations and Grades
- Do not introduce universal maternal screening with HSV-1 and HSV-2 type-specific serology: it "has not been tested in a trial, and is not currently recommended." Grade: not stated.
- Ask every woman about her own and her partner's genital and oral herpes history and examine for lesions at delivery. Offer type-specific serology to asymptomatic women with a positive partner and to HIV-positive women. Grade: not stated.
- Treat primary or first-episode genital herpes with acyclovir 400 mg orally three times daily for 7–10 days, or valacyclovir 1 g orally twice daily for 7–10 days; extend if healing is incomplete. Consider continuing therapy until delivery when the primary outbreak occurs in the third trimester. Grade: not stated (adapted from CDC 2015 guidance).
- Treat symptomatic recurrent episodes with acyclovir 400 mg three times daily for 5 days, or valacyclovir 500 mg twice daily for 3 days, or 1 g daily for 5 days. Grade: not stated.
- Offer daily suppression from 36 weeks to delivery to women with a history of genital HSV, including those without a recurrence in the pregnancy: acyclovir 400 mg three times daily or valacyclovir 500 mg twice daily. Suppression reduces recurrences, shedding, and the need for cesarean delivery; evidence is insufficient to show it reduces neonatal HSV. Grade: not stated.
- Perform cesarean delivery if any genital lesion suspicious for HSV is present in labor. Grade: not stated; the text calls this a recommendation.
- For primary or non-primary first-episode infection in the third trimester, cesarean delivery "may be offered due to the possibility of prolonged viral shedding," particularly when symptoms develop within 6 weeks of expected delivery. Grade as stated: "Level C-1 Recommendation" [CDC 2015].
- Perform an indicated cesarean before membrane rupture, or as soon as possible and ideally within 4–6 hours after rupture; cesarean may benefit regardless of the duration of membrane rupture. Grade: not stated.
- Do not perform cesarean for a recurrent episode, for a history of HSV without active lesions or prodrome, or for nongenital lesions, which can be covered with an occlusive dressing. Grade: not stated.

## Key Concepts
- Seroprevalence in United States pregnant women: 21.1% HSV-2 IgG, 59.3% HSV-1 seropositive, 30.6% seronegative. About 12–20% of couples are HSV-discordant in early pregnancy. About 2–4% of IgG-negative women seroconvert in pregnancy, and 75–90% of HSV-2 infected people are unaware of infection. About 0.1–1% of pregnant women carry HSV in the genitalia.
- Neonatal herpes incidence: 1/60,000 live births annually in the United Kingdom; 12–60/100,000 in the United States.
- Neonatal disease: disseminated or CNS disease in about 55%; up to 30% die and more than 50% have neurological damage despite antiviral therapy. Classic triad: skin lesions, chorioretinitis, CNS abnormalities. Seventy percent of mothers of infected neonates are asymptomatic.
- Diagnosis: PCR of genital lesions is more sensitive than culture and is preferred; type the virus. Culture must be taken within 48–72 hours of lesion appearance. Send type-specific glycoprotein G serology. If serology and culture or PCR are discrepant, a new infection is diagnosed; repeat serology in 3–4 weeks when a new infection is suspected but virus is not isolated. Lack of HSV on PCR does not exclude infection because shedding is intermittent. Tzanck smear shows multinucleated giant cells and viral inclusions. Rapid HSV PCR in labor is not currently recommended.
- Severe or disseminated disease (pneumonitis, hepatitis, CNS involvement): IV acyclovir 5–10 mg/kg every 8 hours for 2–7 days until clinical improvement, then oral therapy to complete 10 days total.
- Antiviral safety: acyclovir and valacyclovir do not appear to increase overall birth defects; some data raise a possible association between first-trimester exposure and gastroschisis. Acyclovir is compatible with breastfeeding.
- Neonatal precautions: contact precautions if the mother delivered vaginally, or by cesarean after membranes had been ruptured more than 4 hours. Standard precautions suffice for cesarean without prior rupture or with rupture less than 4 hours before delivery. Mothers with lesions should wash hands and cover lesions but can handle the neonate.

## Anti-patterns
- Universal HSV serologic screening: untested in trials, and no intervention reliably prevents an already uncommon outcome.
- Cesarean delivery for a recurrent episode or for nongenital lesions: it adds maternal risk without benefit.
- Delaying cesarean until after membrane rupture when a genital lesion is present.
- Relying on a negative PCR or culture to exclude HSV: shedding is intermittent.
- Using rapid intrapartum PCR as standard practice before prospective validation.

## Key Takeaways
- Obtain a herpes history at the first visit and inspect the genitalia in labor in every woman with a history.
- Suppress with acyclovir 400 mg three times daily or valacyclovir 500 mg twice daily from 36 weeks.
- Deliver by cesarean for any suspicious genital lesion in labor, and do not wait after rupture.
- Treat first episodes for 7–10 days and consider continuing treatment to delivery after a third-trimester primary episode.
- Tell women that suppression reduces recurrences and cesarean need, but has not been shown to prevent neonatal HSV.
