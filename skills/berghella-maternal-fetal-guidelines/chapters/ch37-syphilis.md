# Chapter 37: Syphilis

## Core Idea
Pregnancy does not change the clinical manifestations, the stage classification, or the penicillin dose. Parenteral penicillin G is the only therapy with documented efficacy in pregnancy (success >98%); the penicillin-allergic pregnant patient is desensitized and then treated with penicillin, never switched to a macrolide. All pregnant women have a serologic test at the first prenatal visit; high-risk women, women in high-morbidity areas, and previously untested women are screened again at 28 weeks and at delivery. Perinatal transmission can occur at any stage but is most common in primary, secondary, and early latent disease, where up to 83% of fetuses and newborns are affected. Nearly all treatment recommendations rest on expert opinion, case series, and more than 50 years of clinical experience, not on randomized trials.

## Recommendations and Grades
- Screen every pregnant woman with a serologic (nontreponemal) test at the first prenatal visit. Source: CDC and ACOG/AAP *Guidelines for Perinatal Care* [III].
- Repeat screening at 28 weeks and again at delivery for women at high risk, in areas of high syphilis morbidity, or previously untested. Source: CDC/ACOG guidance [III].
- Treat with benzathine penicillin G 2.4 million units IM: single dose for primary, secondary, and early latent syphilis; weekly for 3 weeks (7.2 million units total) for late latent, unknown duration, and tertiary syphilis. Evidence base: opinions of STD experts reinforced by case series, clinical trials, and >50 years of clinical experience; no randomized trials.
- Desensitize the penicillin-allergic pregnant patient (history of anaphylaxis or positive skin test to a penicillin determinant) and treat with penicillin. Source: [II-2].
- Do not substitute erythromycin, azithromycin, or ceftriaxone; none has been shown to reliably cure maternal infection or prevent congenital syphilis.
- Perform lumbar puncture when neurologic or ophthalmologic signs, aortitis or gummas, treatment failure or treatment with an agent other than penicillin, HIV infection, or a titer >1:32 are present.
- Treat neurosyphilis with aqueous crystalline penicillin G 18–24 million units/day IV (3–4 million units every 4 hours or continuous infusion) for 10–14 days, or procaine penicillin 2.4 million units IM daily plus probenecid 500 mg PO four times daily for 10–14 days.
- Treat proven, highly probable, or possible congenital syphilis with a 10-day course of IV aqueous crystalline penicillin G or IM procaine penicillin G.

## Key Concepts
- Vertical transmission risk by maternal stage: 70–100% primary, 40% early latent, 10% late latent.
- Greatest risk of stillbirth from congenital syphilis is at 24–32 weeks' gestation; the longer the interval from infection to pregnancy, the more benign the infant outcome.
- Missed prevention opportunities in the United States in 2018: inadequate maternal treatment despite timely diagnosis 30.7%, no prenatal care 28.2%. Only 90% of states mandate antenatal screening; of those, 76% require one early test and 24% require third-trimester repeat.
- If a three-dose weekly regimen is used and the next dose is not given within 7 days, restart the whole course.
- For reverse-sequence algorithms: positive treponemal test → nontreponemal test → if discordant, a different treponemal test.
- Nontreponemal tests (RPR, VDRL) are for screening and for monitoring response; they are not useful in primary disease and detect antigens that are not treponeme-specific. Serology sensitivity 85.5%, specificity 97.1%.
- Dark-field microscopy: sensitivity 80%, specificity 99–100%; three negative examinations on different days are needed before a lesion is called negative. PCR 95.8%/95.7% (investigational). Point-of-care test 98.2%/97.3% (Syphilis Health Check, FDA approved 2011, CLIA waiver 2015, result in 12 minutes).
- CSF-VDRL is highly specific but only ~30% sensitive; a positive CSF VDRL is diagnostic of neurosyphilis; a negative treponemal CSF test rules it out.
- Follow-up titers at 1, 3, 6, 12, and 24 months. Primary and secondary syphilis: fourfold (two-dilution) decline by 6 months and eightfold (four-dilution) decline by 12 months. Early latent: fourfold decline by 12 months. A fourfold rise or inadequate fall means treatment failure or reinfection — repeat the regimen. Use the same test (RPR or VDRL) throughout.
- Neurosyphilis: repeat CSF every 6 months for 2 years or until CSF shows no disease.
- Jarisch–Herxheimer reaction occurs in up to 45% of pregnant women treated for syphilis; onset 1–2 hours, peak at 8 hours, resolution within 24–48 hours. It may induce labor or fetal distress but must not delay therapy.
- Oral desensitization: 14 escalating doses of penicillin V, 15-minute intervals, cumulative 1.3 million units, 3 hours 45 minutes total; observe 30 minutes before parenteral penicillin.
- Congenital syphilis: up to 40% of infants of untreated women are stillborn or die shortly after birth; >50% of affected infants are born to mothers with late or no prenatal care. Long-bone radiograph shows osteochondritis in 95% of these infants.

## Anti-patterns
- Treating a pregnant woman with a macrolide or ceftriaxone because of penicillin allergy: these agents do not reliably prevent congenital syphilis. Desensitize instead.
- Relying on serology to diagnose primary syphilis: tests are usually nonreactive for several weeks after the chancre appears; use dark-field microscopy or DFA-TP.
- Using treponemal tests for screening: they stay reactive for life in >85% of adequately treated patients and are false-positive in about 1% — they cannot show reinfection or response.
- Giving the second or third weekly penicillin dose more than 7 days after the previous dose: drug levels are not sustained; restart the course.
- Screening women with GBS-type assumptions about "one test is enough": a first-trimester serology does not cover acquisition later in pregnancy.
- Delaying treatment because of concern about the Jarisch–Herxheimer reaction.

## Key Takeaways
1. Serologic screen at the first prenatal visit for all; repeat at 28 weeks and delivery if high risk, high-prevalence area, or never tested.
2. Benzathine penicillin G 2.4 million units IM — one dose for early syphilis, three weekly doses for late latent/unknown duration — is the only regimen proven to prevent congenital syphilis.
3. Penicillin allergy in pregnancy means desensitization, not substitution.
4. Stage-specific transmission risk drives urgency: primary and secondary disease threaten up to 83% of fetuses.
5. Check titers at 1, 3, 6, 12, and 24 months; a fourfold rise or an inadequate fall requires retreatment.
6. Treat the partner and document treatment, because reinfection is the main cause of apparent failure.
