# Chapter 51: Parvovirus

## Core Idea
Acute primary maternal parvovirus B19 infection complicates about 1–2% of susceptible pregnancies. About 25–30% of fetuses of infected mothers become infected, and 5–20% of infected fetuses develop anemia. Surveillance with middle cerebral artery peak systolic velocity (MCA-PSV) detects anemia before hydrops appears, and intrauterine transfusion of the anemic or hydropic fetus is the only effective intervention. Universal screening is not worthwhile because the risk of fetal hydrops is about 1/5000 screened pregnancies.

## Recommendations and Grades
- Do not perform universal screening: the risk of fetal hydrops is about 1/5000 screened pregnancies, "making screening not warranted." Screening "may be warranted" in women who teach or care for young children, especially during epidemics. Grade: not stated.
- Diagnose maternal infection by IgM positivity or by IgG seroconversion; add PCR because serology can be falsely negative in up to 15%. Grade: not stated.
- Start fetal surveillance with MCA-PSV no later than 4 weeks after the onset of illness or estimated seroconversion, and continue weekly scans for 10–12 weeks after exposure when MCA-PSV is <1.50 MoM. Grade: not stated.
- Use MCA-PSV ≥1.50 MoM as the threshold for fetal anemia; the cited study reported 100% sensitivity and 100% specificity [19]. Grade as stated: single study, no formal recommendation grade.
- Transfuse when MCA-PSV is ≥1.50 MoM or hydrops is present: spontaneous resolution of hydrops occurs in only about 5%, and survival with transfusion is 70–75%. Grade: not formally graded; supported by nonrandomized controlled studies.
- Do not perform percutaneous umbilical blood sampling purely for diagnosis; fetal diagnosis is by amniotic fluid PCR.
- Give steroids for lung maturity between 24 and 33 6/7 weeks when intervention is planned.

## Key Concepts
- Seroconversion in susceptible pregnant women is 1–1.5% during endemic periods and 13–13.5% during epidemics. About 50–75% of reproductive-age women are IgG positive; 25–50% are susceptible.
- Transmission: 25–30% of fetuses of mothers with primary infection. Of infected fetuses, 80–95% have no sequelae; 5–20% develop anemia; 30–50% of anemic fetuses develop hydrops (2–6% of all infected fetuses; 1–4% of infected mothers). Hydrops rates reach 8–10% when infection occurs at 9–20 weeks.
- Fetal death occurs in 1–6% of infected fetuses, almost exclusively in hydropic cases diagnosed before 20 weeks. Untreated infection before 20 weeks carries about a 10% risk of fetal loss; timely transfusion after 20 weeks gives 90% survival. First-trimester miscarriage is associated with positive IgM (OR 1.71, 95% CI 1.02–2.86).
- Timing: the peak incidence of hydrops is about 4–6 weeks after maternal infection. If MCA-PSV is elevated without hydrops and cord sampling is not done, increase surveillance to 2–3 scans per week.
- Resolution: hydrops can resolve spontaneously over 4–6 weeks; spontaneous resolution of anemia is 49.6% and of hydrops 5.2%; resolution is more common after 20 weeks.
- Management by gestational age:
  - 24 to 33 6/7 weeks: steroids for lung maturity, cordocentesis to document anemia, transfusion as needed. One transfusion is frequently sufficient; up to 37% of hydropic fetuses need two or more.
  - Before 24 weeks with severe hydrops: termination may be offered, but transfusion can be beneficial and appears to carry minimal to no significant sequelae if successful.
  - After 34 weeks: consider delivery.
- Procedure: anemia may be detected before hemoglobin falls below 6 g/dL and before severe hydrops. Prepare platelets at the time of PUBS because fetal thrombocytopenia is common at transfusion.
- Diagnosis: IgM appears by day 3, peaks at 25–30 days, and disappears by 4 months; IgG appears a few days after IgM. Amniotic fluid PCR confirms fetal infection. Increased MSAFP has been used as a poor-prognosis marker but has been questioned.
- Ultrasound markers: pericardial or pleural effusion, ascites, skin or abdominal wall edema, bilateral hydroceles, oligohydramnios or hydramnios, cardiac biventricular outer diameter >95th percentile, and rarely hydrocephalus, microcephaly, intracranial and hepatic calcifications.
- Long-term: infants of IgM-positive mothers are born IgG positive, and 25% remain IgG positive at 1 year. Transfused survivors followed from 6 months to 8 years had a 32% incidence of psychomotor developmental delay, independent of pretransfusion hemoglobin, platelet count, or pH. Fetal hydrops carries a 9.5% risk of abnormal neurodevelopmental outcome versus 0% without hydrops.
- Prevention: avoid contact with infected children; frequent handwashing. No antiviral or vaccine exists. IVIG prophylaxis is considered for documented exposure in immunocompromised patients but is not recommended in pregnancy.

## Anti-patterns
- Universal serologic screening: the yield is too low (1/5000) to justify it.
- Waiting for hydrops before acting: anemia is detectable by MCA-PSV before hydropic markers appear.
- Expectant management of hydrops on the assumption that it resolves: only about 5% resolve, and survival with transfusion is 70–75%.
- PUBS for diagnosis alone: amniotic fluid PCR is sufficient.
- Quoting a uniformly normal neurodevelopmental prognosis after intrauterine transfusion for hydrops.

## Key Takeaways
- Take any maternal IgM-positive result as a trigger for weekly MCA-PSV, starting within 4 weeks of illness and continuing 10–12 weeks.
- Use 1.50 MoM as the action threshold; increased surveillance to 2–3 scans weekly applies when MCA-PSV is high but hydrops is absent.
- Offer cordocentesis and transfusion for MCA-PSV ≥1.50 MoM or hydrops.
- Time intervention by gestation: transfuse and give steroids at 24–33 6/7 weeks, individualize before 24 weeks, and deliver after 34 weeks.
- Counsel that hydrops, not infection alone, drives the neurodevelopmental risk.
