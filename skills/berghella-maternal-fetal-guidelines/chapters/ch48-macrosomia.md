# Chapter 48: Fetal Macrosomia

## Core Idea
Macrosomia is an estimated fetal weight (EFW) of at least 4000 g, graded I (4000–4499 g), II (4500–4999 g), and III (≥5000 g); large for gestational age (EFW >90th percentile) is not the same entity. Clinical and sonographic estimates both detect the 4000 g threshold reasonably but are poor at identifying the 4500 g fetus, and both systematically overestimate weight. Prevention is possible only where glucose metabolism is modifiable: treating gestational diabetes, using postprandial monitoring when insulin is required, targeting fasting glucose <95 mg/dL and 2-hour postprandial <120 mg/dL, and bariatric surgery or exercise in obese women. For the uncomplicated pregnancy with suspected macrosomia, induction at 38 0/7–38 6/7 weeks reduces fetal fractures and shoulder dystocia without increasing cesarean delivery, but ACOG advises against delivery before 39 weeks and reserves planned cesarean for EFW ≥5000 g without diabetes or ≥4500 g with diabetes — suggestions not based on level 1 evidence.

## Recommendations and Grades
- Treat gestational diabetes with diet, glucose monitoring, and insulin if needed, rather than no treatment or diet alone. Source: RCT, n = 1000 (impaired glucose tolerance: fasting <7.0 mmol/L, 2-hour 7.8–11.0 mmol/L; diet, monitoring, and insulin as needed versus routine care).
- Use postprandial rather than preprandial glucose monitoring in GDM requiring insulin. Source: RCT, n = 66 [Level I].
- Target fasting blood glucose <95 mg/dL and 2-hour postprandial <120 mg/dL to reduce macrosomia risk. Source: American Diabetes Association [Level III].
- Consider continuous glucose monitoring compared with standard intermittent self-monitoring. Source: RCT, n = 71 [Level I].
- Treat mild or borderline GDM (abnormal one-hour challenge, normal three-hour tolerance test) with nutrition instruction, glucose testing, and insulin if needed. Source: RCT, n = 300 [Level I].
- Screen for GDM with the one-step 2-hour test.
- Offer bariatric surgery to eligible obese women (BMI ≥40 kg/m², or ≥35 kg/m² with comorbidities) before pregnancy: odds of macrosomia OR 0.46 (95% CI 0.34–0.62).
- Recommend prenatal exercise: 31% reduction in the odds of a macrosomic newborn (OR 0.69, 95% CI 0.55–0.86).
- In uncomplicated pregnancy with suspected macrosomia, induction of labor at 38 0/7–38 6/7 weeks (EFW >4000 g or >95th percentile) can be considered: pooled data from 4 RCTs (1190 non-diabetic women) show fewer fetal fractures (RR 0.17) and no increase in cesarean delivery (RR 0.91), with no difference in shoulder dystocia, brachial plexus injury, intracranial hemorrhage, Apgar <7, or cord pH <7.
- A 2015 multicenter RCT found induction at 37 0/7–38 6/7 weeks for EFW ≥4000 g in non-diabetic women reduced shoulder dystocia (RR 0.32, 95% CI 0.12–0.85) and associated morbidity (RR 0.32, 95% CI 0.15–0.71) without increasing cesarean delivery.
- Do not deliver before 39 weeks for uncomplicated suspected macrosomia: ACOG recommends against it pending further evidence.
- Consider planned cesarean for a non-diabetic patient when EFW is at least 5000 g, and for a diabetic patient when EFW is at least 4500 g. Source: ACOG Practice Bulletin 216 [Level I–III]; the chapter notes these are not based on level 1 evidence, and others use thresholds of 4000 g or 4250 g in diabetes.
- For prior cesarean delivery, individualize: consider planned repeat cesarean if the predicted VBAC success rate is below 60% (MFMU calculator).
- For prior shoulder dystocia, consider offering planned cesarean at 39 0/7–39 6/7 weeks, especially with suspected macrosomia.

## Key Concepts
- Grades matter: newborn morbidity is higher in grade I than in the general obstetric population, rises further in grade II, and newborn mortality increases significantly in grade III.
- Prevalence: 2–10% in high-income countries; 1–5% in low- and middle-income countries, ranging 0.5–14.9%. In the United States about 8% of live births exceed 4000 g and about 1% exceed 4500 g. The ≥4000 g rate fell from 10.2% (1996) to 9.2% (2002) to 8.0% (2013); the ≥5000 g rate fell from 0.16% (1996) to 0.13% (2002) to 0.10% (2008). Chinese populations report 3.4%.
- Between 5% and 10% of macrosomic fetuses are associated with clinically detected maternal diabetes.
- Risk factors: maternal diabetes (pre-existing or gestational), gestational age ≥40 weeks, advanced maternal age, male fetal sex, excessive pregnancy weight gain, pre-pregnancy obesity, Black or Hispanic race/ethnicity, maternal birth weight >8 lb, grand multiparity, prior macrosomic fetus, and even one abnormal value on a 3-hour glucose screen. GDM, pre-pregnancy adiposity, and pregnancy weight gain are independently associated with macrosomia/LGA across races, except GDM among non-Hispanic Whites; the three together substantially increase LGA risk.
- Complications (Table 48.2): maternal — protracted or arrested labor, operative vaginal delivery, cesarean, postpartum hemorrhage, intra-amniotic infection, vaginal and perineal lacerations; fetal/neonatal — shoulder dystocia, clavicular fracture, obstetric brachial plexus injury, low Apgar scores, meconium aspiration, NICU admission, hypoglycemia.
- Birth weight >4500 g is significantly associated with shoulder dystocia and obstetric anal sphincter injury. Shoulder dystocia occurs in 0.2–3.0% of all vaginal deliveries and is usually unpredictable; most cases occur in non-diabetic women with normal-sized infants.
- A systematic review found that with birth weight >4000 g the risks of emergency cesarean, postpartum hemorrhage, and OASIS are increased, and rise further with higher macrosomia grades; the risks of shoulder dystocia, brachial plexus injury, and birth fractures are increased above 4000 g and rise further above 4500 g.
- Ultrasound biometry is 56% sensitive and 92% specific for macrosomia. During labor, clinical and sonographic estimates detect the 4000 g neonate similarly, but the likelihood ratio is 15 for clinicians' estimate versus 42 for biometric parameters. Neither method accurately identifies the 4500 g fetus, and both systematically overestimate birth weight.
- Diabetic management details: insulin twice daily versus four times daily did not significantly reduce macrosomia, and glyburide or metformin as alternatives to insulin did not significantly reduce it.
- Diabetes and induction: in insulin-requiring diabetic pregnancies, induction at about 38 weeks is associated with fewer macrosomic fetuses than expectant management, but the sample is too small for firm conclusions. There are insufficient studies in uncomplicated diabetic women with suspected macrosomia to make delivery recommendations.
- Prior cesarean: most patients attempting VBAC with a macrosomic fetus deliver successfully, but the uterine rupture rate may be 3.6% in a macrosomic trial of labor when the patient has never delivered vaginally.
- Prior shoulder dystocia: recurrence risk up to about 15%. Brachial plexus injury occurs in 1.4 per 1000 births in the general population, 13 per 1000 after a prior shoulder dystocia without recurrence, and 45 per 1000 with recurrent shoulder dystocia.
- There are no randomized trials of management for suspected macrosomia with diabetes, prior cesarean, or prior shoulder dystocia, because birth weight prediction is inaccurate.

## Anti-patterns
- Treating large for gestational age and macrosomia as synonyms: LGA is an EFW >90th percentile, macrosomia is an absolute weight ≥4000 g.
- Relying on ultrasound to rule out a 4500 g fetus: both clinical and sonographic estimates are inaccurate at that threshold and tend to overestimate.
- Inducing before 39 weeks for uncomplicated suspected macrosomia outside a trial or shared decision: ACOG recommends against it.
- Assuming a macrosomic fetus mandates cesarean: most VBAC and vaginal deliveries with suspected macrosomia succeed.
- Using glyburide or metformin expecting a reduction in macrosomia: not demonstrated.
- Changing insulin from twice to four times daily expecting a reduction in macrosomia: no significant difference.
- Offering planned cesarean to every woman with suspected macrosomia: thresholds of 5000 g without diabetes and 4500 g with diabetes are the ACOG suggestions, and they rest on limited evidence.
- Predicting shoulder dystocia from EFW: it is usually unpredictable.

## Key Takeaways
1. Define macrosomia as EFW ≥4000 g and grade it: 4000–4499, 4500–4999, ≥5000 g, because morbidity and mortality rise with each grade.
2. Prevent it through glucose control: treat GDM, monitor postprandially, target fasting <95 mg/dL and 2-hour postprandial <120 mg/dL.
3. In uncomplicated pregnancy with EFW >4000 g, offer induction at 38 0/7–38 6/7 weeks after shared decision-making; it reduces fetal fractures and shoulder dystocia without more cesareans.
4. Do not plan delivery before 39 weeks for uncomplicated suspected macrosomia.
5. Consider cesarean at EFW ≥5000 g without diabetes and ≥4500 g with diabetes, recognizing the weak evidence.
6. Counsel about recurrence after shoulder dystocia and individualize mode of delivery; the brachial plexus injury risk rises from 1.4 to 45 per 1000 with recurrence.
