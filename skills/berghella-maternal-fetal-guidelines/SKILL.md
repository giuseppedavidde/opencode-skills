# Maternal-Fetal Evidence Based Guidelines
**Editor**: Vincenzo Berghella | **Edition**: Fourth | **Chapters**: 59 | **Generated**: 2026-11-08

**Scope**: This is the medical-complications volume. It covers disorders that exist before pregnancy or arise during it: hypertension, cardiac disease, diabetes, thyroid disease, haematology, infection, rheumatology, neurology, psychiatry, and fetal surveillance. It does not cover obstetric management. Abruption, preterm birth management, labour, delivery technique, cesarean technique and postpartum haemorrhage are in the companion *Obstetric Evidence Based Guidelines*, Fourth Edition (same editor, same series). A reader who needs the management of abruption or spontaneous preterm birth will not find it here.

## Core Frameworks & Mental Models

### 1. How the book grades evidence
Every reference carries one level, using a modified U.S. Preventive Services Task Force scheme.

| Level | Definition |
|---|---|
| **I** | At least one properly designed randomised controlled trial |
| **II-1** | Well-designed controlled trials without randomisation |
| **II-2** | Well-designed cohort or case-control analytic studies, preferably from more than one centre |
| **II-3** | Multiple time series, with or without the intervention; dramatic uncontrolled results also qualify |
| **III** | Opinions of respected authorities, clinical experience, descriptive studies, expert committees |

The book grades **references**, not recommendations. The guideline text is a narrative that states the recommended action, often with the percent benefit. When evidence is absent the chapter says so: "no Level 1 evidence", "insufficient evidence", "there are no randomised trials". Those phrases are findings, not omissions. Use them to judge how firmly to apply a recommendation.

### 2. Hypertensive disorders — the operating definitions
- **Chronic hypertension (CHTN)**: BP ≥140/90 before 20 weeks, or pre-existing hypertension. **Severe CHTN**: SBP ≥160 or DBP ≥110 mmHg. **High-risk CHTN** adds secondary hypertension, target-organ damage, age >40, prior pregnancy loss, SBP ≥180 or DBP ≥110.
- **Gestational hypertension**: ≥140/90 after 20 weeks, on two occasions 6 hours apart, without proteinuria or severe features.
- **Pre-eclampsia**: new hypertension after 20 weeks with proteinuria ≥300 mg/24 h, protein/creatinine ratio ≥0.3, or dipstick >1+. Pre-eclampsia is also diagnosed without proteinuria when new hypertension coexists with thrombocytopenia, renal insufficiency, impaired liver function, pulmonary oedema, or cerebral or visual symptoms.
- **Severe features**: BP ≥160/110 on two occasions >4 hours apart; platelets <100,000/mm³; AST or ALT ≥2× upper limit; creatinine ≥1.1 mg/dL or doubling; pulmonary oedema; persistent headache or visual change.
- **HELLP**: LDH >600 IU/L or bilirubin ≥1.2 mg/dL, AST or ALT ≥2× upper limit, platelets <100,000/mm³. One or two criteria = partial HELLP.

Management rests on four levers. **Treat severe BP** with labetalol 20 mg IV then 40/80/80 mg every 10 minutes (maximum 220 mg), hydralazine 5–10 mg IV/IM every 20 minutes, or nifedipine 10–20 mg orally. **Prevent seizures** with magnesium sulfate. Versus placebo or no anticonvulsant, it reduces eclampsia by 59% **[I]** and is superior to phenytoin and diazepam. **Prevent pre-eclampsia** with low-dose aspirin 81 mg/day from 12–28 weeks, optimally before 16 weeks. A meta-analysis of 34 RCTs found large reductions in pre-eclampsia and severe pre-eclampsia when aspirin started before 16 weeks **[I]**, although two other meta-analyses found no difference by timing. **Time delivery**: 37 0/7 weeks for gestational hypertension or pre-eclampsia without severe features (29% reduction in composite maternal outcome, HYPITAT **[I]**), and 34 0/7 weeks for severe features.

### 3. Diabetes — two screening strategies, one set of targets
**One-step** uses a 75 g, 2-hour GTT with IADPSG thresholds: fasting ≥92, 1-hour ≥180, 2-hour ≥153 mg/dL. One abnormal value diagnoses GDM. **Two-step** uses a non-fasting 50 g, 1-hour glucose challenge at 130, 135 or 140 mg/dL (the book uses 135), followed by a 100 g, 3-hour GTT. Diagnosis needs two abnormal values on either the NDDG (105/190/165/145 mg/dL) or the Carpenter–Coustan criteria (95/180/155/140 mg/dL). The book favours Carpenter–Coustan because the lower cutoffs capture real morbidity. One-step screening diagnoses about twice as many women. Two-step is more cost-effective and is supported by ACOG, while the book notes that meta-analysis supports one-step for improved outcomes.

Treatment targets are the same for pregestational and gestational diabetes: fasting <95 mg/dL and 2-hour postprandial ≤120 mg/dL (1-hour ≤140 mg/dL). Insulin is first-line. Metformin is preferred to glyburide but many women fail monotherapy. Preconception HbA1c <6% prevents most malformations. Well-controlled pregestational diabetes delivers at 39 0/7–39 6/7 weeks, and cesarean may be offered when EFW ≥4500 g.

### 4. Anticoagulation — risk-stratified, not diagnosis-driven
The framework has three dose tiers, not two. **Prophylactic**: enoxaparin 40 mg SC daily or dalteparin 5000 U daily. **Intermediate**: enoxaparin 40 mg every 12 hours. **Therapeutic**: enoxaparin 1 mg/kg every 12 hours, or dalteparin 200 U/kg daily. Unfractionated heparin is dosed by trimester: 5000–7500 U q12h in the first, 7500–10,000 U in the second, 10,000 U in the third. Anti-Xa targets are 0.2–0.4 U/mL for prophylaxis and 0.5–1.2 U/mL for therapy (the VTE chapter uses 0.6–1.0 U/mL for twice-daily dosing).

Assignment follows history. Prior VTE with a low-risk thrombophilia: prophylactic LMWH. Prior VTE with a non-recurring cause and no thrombophilia: no routine antepartum prophylaxis, but postpartum prophylaxis. Prior unprovoked VTE: prophylactic or intermediate. High-risk thrombophilia (antithrombin deficiency, homozygous factor V or prothrombin, compound heterozygote) with prior VTE: therapeutic. APS with fetal loss but no thrombosis: aspirin plus prophylactic heparin. APS with prior VTE: therapeutic heparin, then lifelong warfarin. Acute VTE: therapeutic LMWH throughout pregnancy, or IV UFH for at least 5 days then LMWH, for a minimum of 6 months and at least 6 weeks postpartum. LMWH and UFH do not cross the placenta. Warfarin does, and causes embryopathy in 4–5% after exposure at 6–9 weeks.

### 5. Infection screening — a scheduled programme
Screening is a calendar, not a single test.
- **First prenatal visit**: HIV with an opt-out approach, HBsAg, syphilis serology, rubella IgG, varicella IgG, and urine culture. Add chlamydia and gonorrhoea when age <25 or risk factors are present, and HCV antibody, for which universal screening is now recommended.
- **Repeat before 36 weeks**: HIV if high-risk behaviour, high-prevalence area, or prior decline.
- **24–28 weeks**: GDM screen; repeat HIV, HBsAg and syphilis when high risk.
- **28 weeks**: anti-D 300 µg for unsensitised Rh(D)-negative women.
- **36 0/7–37 6/7 weeks**: GBS anovaginal culture.
- **Delivery**: syphilis serology if high risk or untested.

HIV: give combination antiretroviral therapy to all, target viral load <1000 copies/mL, and counsel for planned cesarean at 38 weeks when viral load exceeds 1000 copies/mL. With suppression, cesarean when indicated, and formula feeding, transmission falls below 2%. Syphilis: benzathine penicillin G 2.4 million units IM is the only recommended treatment. Desensitise and treat if allergic. Hepatitis B: give HBIg plus vaccine at birth. Give tenofovir when HBV DNA >200,000 IU/mL. GBS: penicillin G 5 million units IV then 3 million units every 4 hours. **Do not** screen universally for CMV or toxoplasmosis. Both chapters state that prenatal screening is not adopted in most countries.

### 6. Fetal surveillance — a hierarchy, not a single test
The hierarchy runs from least to most specific.
1. **Fetal movement counting** — no proven reduction in perinatal mortality. The AFFIRM trial showed more induction and more cesarean without fewer stillbirths.
2. **Non-stress test (NST)** — the commonest test, but no demonstrated effect on perinatal mortality or preventable death. Up to 50% of NSTs at 24–28 weeks and 15% at 28–32 weeks are non-reactive, so a non-reactive test before 32 weeks is often normal.
3. **Biophysical profile score (BPS)** — five components scored 0 or 2. 8/8 equals 10/10. 6/10 is repeated in about 6 hours. Persistent 6/10 at term delivers. A score of 4/10 delivers, and 0–2/10 delivers urgently. BPS increases induction and cesarean compared with NST but not NICU admission or perinatal mortality.
4. **Modified BPS** — NST plus maximum vertical pocket. A full BPS is needed as backup in 15–30%.
5. **Umbilical artery Doppler** — reduces perinatal death in growth restriction **[I]**, and should not be used in normal pregnancies. Absent end-diastolic flow raises surveillance to 2–3 times weekly. Reversed flow goes to daily.

The hierarchy's purpose is to prevent stillbirth without causing iatrogenic prematurity. No randomised trial shows that antenatal testing reduces cerebral palsy, and most schemes lack high-level evidence. Delivery decisions must weigh gestational age against stillbirth risk.
