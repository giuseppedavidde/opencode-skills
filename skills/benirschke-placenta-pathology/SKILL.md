# Benirschke's Pathology of the Human Placenta
**Editors**: Rebecca N. Baergen, Graham J. Burton, Cynthia G. Kaplan | **Edition**: Seventh | **Chapters**: 33 | **Generated**: 2026-11-08

## Core Frameworks & Mental Models

### 1. The Amsterdam consensus grid
The 2014/2016 Amsterdam consensus supplies the working vocabulary. It separates five families: maternal vascular malperfusion (MVM), fetal vascular malperfusion (FVM), villitis of unknown etiology (VUE), maternal inflammatory response (MIR), and fetal inflammatory response (FIR). "Other" covers chorangiosis, delayed villous maturation, chronic histiocytic intervillositis and the fibrinoid deposition disorders. Every diagnosis states the lesion, its grade where defined, and the percentage of parenchyma involved. Sampling follows the same logic: three pieces of villous tissue including fetal and maternal surfaces, two sections of umbilical cord, and one or two rolls of membranes, usually mounted as four slides.

### 2. Maternal vascular malperfusion (MVM)
MVM is the placental expression of abnormal maternal perfusion. The lesions are placental hypoplasia (weight below the 10th centile for gestation), infarction, retroplacental and marginal hemorrhage, distal villous hypoplasia (DVH), accelerated villous maturation, decidual arteriopathy and intervillous thrombosis. Two patterns coexist: global/partial MVM, with distal villous hypoplasia early and accelerated maturation late, and segmental/complete MVM, with spiral-artery occlusion and infarcts. Proposed grading: grade 1 has weight within norms, less than 30% parenchymal involvement and at most one marginal infarct; grade 2 has weight below the 10th centile, more than 30% involvement and multiple non-marginal infarcts. Infarcts are reported by location (central or peripheral), number, largest dimension and percentage of parenchymal volume. Any infarct in a preterm placenta is significant; at term, more than 5% of the non-peripheral parenchyma warrants documentation. Decidual arteriopathy covers deficient spiral-artery remodeling, fibrinoid necrosis, acute atherosis with CD68-positive foam cells, mural hypertrophy, chronic perivasculitis, arterial thrombosis and persistent intramural endovascular trophoblast. It is best sought in the decidua capsularis of the membrane roll, because the delivered basal plate is often incomplete. Spiral arteries occur at about one per 2–3 cm².

### 3. Fetal vascular malperfusion (FVM)
FVM reflects obstructed fetal flow. Causes include cord hypercoiling (>3 coils per 10 cm), velamentous insertion, true knots, stricture, chorionic-plate thrombosis, stem-villous thrombosis and fetal cardiac dysfunction. Segmental FVM means occlusion of a chorionic or stem villous vessel with all downstream villi affected; global FVM means partial or intermittent obstruction with venous ectasia, intramural fibrin in large vessels and small foci of fewer than five avascular or karyorrhectic villi. The components are thrombi, stem-villous obliteration (fibromuscular sclerosis), intramural fibrin deposition (intimal fibrin cushion), villous stromal-vascular karyorrhexis (small foci ≤10 villi, large foci >10) and avascular villi. Avascular villi are direct evidence of thrombosis even when the thrombus is not found. Low-grade FVM is a single focus of avascular villi or karyorrhexis, one vessel thrombus, or five or more villi in fewer than three foci. High-grade FVM is more than one focus of avascular villi (a cumulative ≥45 villi over three sections or ≥15 per section), or two or more occlusive or non-occlusive thrombi in chorionic plate or major stem villi, or multiple non-occlusive thrombi.

### 4. Gross examination sequence
Examine the fresh specimen, then weigh only the trimmed disc: 470 g at 40 weeks, 22 cm diameter and 2.5 cm thickness are the reference values; fixed tissue weighs more. Measure cord length (mean 59.4 cm, range 24–146 cm) and record the coiling index (normal 0.2 ± 0.1 per cm, one coil per 5 cm; hypocoiled <1 coil per 10 cm; hypercoiled >3 per 10 cm). Inspect the fetal surface for vessel thrombosis, subchorionic fibrin and cysts, and the maternal surface for cotyledons, retroplacental clot and calcification. Make serial cuts at 1–2 cm and describe colour, texture, lesion location and size. Sample three villous pieces that include fetal and maternal surfaces, two cord sections and one or two membrane rolls. Retain a membrane roll with the rupture site marked.

### 5. Villous maturation
Assess terminal villi at 5–10× and judge them against gestational age. Distal villous hypoplasia is a paucity of terminal villi relative to stem villi with widened intervillous spaces; it must involve more than 30% of distal villi and is linked to MVM before 32 weeks. Delayed villous maturation is a uniform lack of maturation, rare before 34 weeks and usually diagnosed after 36 weeks; it requires at least 30% of a full-thickness section and is associated with stillbirth, sudden infant death and postmaturity. Accelerated villous maturation shows small hypermature villi, syncytial knots in more than 33% of villi and excess intervillous fibrin; it requires at least 30% of a section and accompanies preeclampsia and fetal growth restriction (FGR). Syncytial knots count as increased above 20% of villi before 34 weeks and above 30% of 100 villi at term. Ignore changes at the margins and next to infarcts.

### 6. Major lesion differentials
Infarct is coagulative villous necrosis, firm and granular with a peripheral rim; intervillous thrombus is laminated, pushes villi aside and does not infarct. Perivillous fibrin is normal in small amounts; massive perivillous fibrinoid deposition requires more than 50% of at least one slide, and maternal floor infarction is its basal variant. Complete mole is androgenetic, diploid and p57-negative; partial mole is diandric triploid and p57-positive; placental mesenchymal dysplasia has normal trophoblast, normal p57 and diploid ploidy. Accreta spectrum shows villi directly on myometrium without intervening decidua. Chorangiosis is more than 10 capillaries in more than 10 terminal villi in at least 10 fields at 10×; chorangioma is a discrete hemangioma, "giant" above 4 cm; chorangiomatosis is a diffuse stem-villous proliferation.

### 7. Reporting discipline
State the placental weight and centile, cord length, coiling and insertion, membrane insertion, and each lesion with extent and grade. Separate maternal from fetal lesions. Distinguish "present" from "clinically significant": focal perivillous fibrin, a few syncytial knots and a single small avascular focus are common and often unimportant. When a lesion is diffuse or severe, give the percentage and the compartment. Correlate with gestational age, because the same histology means different things at 24 and at 40 weeks.

### 8. Compartment map
Assign every lesion to its compartment before naming it. Maternal: decidual arteriopathy, infarction, intervillous thrombosis, retroplacental hemorrhage. Villous: maturation disorders, VUE, chronic histiocytic intervillositis, perivillous fibrinoid, chorangiosis. Fetal: FVM, chorangioma, mesenchymal dysplasia. Membranes: acute chorioamnionitis, circumvallate insertion, amnion nodosum, meconium. Cord: coiling, insertion, single artery, vasa previa, funisitis. The compartment predicts the clinical direction: maternal lesions cause FGR and preeclampsia, fetal lesions cause thrombosis and neurologic injury.

### 9. Numbers that change management
Placental weight below the 10th centile defines hypoplasia. Distal villous hypoplasia and delayed or accelerated maturation need 30% involvement. MPFD needs more than 50% of one slide. High-grade FVM needs at least 45 avascular villi over three sections or 15 per section. Hypercoiling is more than 3 coils per 10 cm; velamentous insertion is 1.11%; vasa previa complicates 1 in 50 velamentous cords. Complete mole carries 8.9–20% persistent GTD, partial mole 0.2–4%.
