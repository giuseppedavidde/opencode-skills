# Patterns — Benirschke's Placenta

## Reduced fetal growth of placental origin
**When you see it**: Placental weight below the 10th centile with distal villous hypoplasia (DVH) (>30% of distal villi) or accelerated maturation (≥30% of section).
**How to confirm**: Low-power scan of a full-thickness section; compare with stem villi.
**Clinical correlation**: Early-onset fetal growth restriction (FGR) with absent end-diastolic umbilical flow; recurrence and later cardiovascular risk.
**Trade-offs / caveats**: Sampling error is large; thin sections over-call DVH. Only 30% involvement counts.

## Placental abruption
**When you see it**: Retroplacental hematoma, firm and adherent, with compressed or infarcted villi; 0.5–1.0% of births, 66% severe.
**How to confirm**: Inspect maternal surface and cut sections; crowding, hemorrhage and coagulative necrosis appear after 4–24 hours.
**Clinical correlation**: Leading stillbirth risk factor (3.4–51.8%); 11-fold recurrence, especially with smoking.
**Trade-offs / caveats**: Fresh clot mimics abruption; old clot is brown-green and adherent. Central bleeding differs from marginal.

## Massive perivillous fibrinoid deposition with recurrent loss
**When you see it**: Perivillous fibrinoid in >50% of at least one slide; small, firm, "tigroid" placenta; basal variant is maternal floor infarction.
**How to confirm**: Map areas; note basal concentration, calcification.
**Clinical correlation**: FGR, stillbirth, recurrence; antiphospholipid, LCHAD deficiency, renal tubular dysgenesis, high AFP.
**Trade-offs / caveats**: Fibrin is normal in small amounts. Exclude FVM when avascular villi lie outside.

## Maternal vascular malperfusion (MVM) in preeclampsia
**When you see it**: Hypoplasia, infarcts, DVH, accelerated maturation and decidual arteriopathy (atherosis, fibrinoid necrosis).
**How to confirm**: Membrane roll for decidual vessels; full-thickness sections for infarct percentage; grade by weight and involvement.
**Clinical correlation**: Preeclampsia, FGR, preterm abruption; later maternal cardiovascular and renal disease.
**Trade-offs / caveats**: No lesion is pathognomonic; lesions overlap with FVM, inflammation. The basal plate often lacks arteriopathy.

## Fetal vascular malperfusion (FVM) with cord abnormality
**When you see it**: Avascular villi, stromal-vascular karyorrhexis, stem-vessel obliteration and thrombi; hypercoiled (>3 coils/10 cm) or velamentous cord.
**How to confirm**: Serial sections of cord and chorionic plate; count avascular villi (high grade ≥45/3 sections or ≥15/section).
**Clinical correlation**: FGR, neurologic impairment and stillbirth; recurrence in later pregnancies.
**Trade-offs / caveats**: Postmortem change mimics FVM; cord thrombi may be artifacts. Small foci are unclear.

## Acute chorioamnionitis: stage and grade
**When you see it**: Neutrophils in chorion and amnion, usually rising from the rupture site; funisitis marks the fetal response.
**How to confirm**: Membrane roll, rupture site centred; stage maternal (subchorionitis–necrotizing) and fetal (phlebitis–necrotizing funisitis).
**Clinical correlation**: Preterm birth, preterm premature rupture of membranes (PPROM), cerebral palsy with fetal response; histology correlates poorly with maternal fever and white count.
**Trade-offs / caveats**: Focal severe area at rupture site is not significant. Eosinophilic/T-cell chorionic vasculitis is separate. Trichomonas is leukotactic without neonatal effect.

## Molar pregnancy triage
**When you see it**: Diffuse villous cisterns and trophoblast proliferation (complete) or admixed normal and hydropic villi with a fetus (partial).
**How to confirm**: p57 immunostain (negative in complete mole), ploidy (diploid versus triploid), STR genotyping.
**Clinical correlation**: Persistent GTD in 8.9–20% of complete and 0.2–4% of partial moles; choriocarcinoma 0.1–0.5% of partial moles.
**Trade-offs / caveats**: Early complete moles lack florid cisterns; hydropic abortus mimics molar change. Morphology alone is unreliable.

## Placenta accreta spectrum
**When you see it**: Villi adherent to myometrium without intervening decidua; accreta, increta, percreta.
**How to confirm**: Uterus with placenta in situ is best; hysterectomy or resection; document depth and site.
**Clinical correlation**: Massive postpartum hemorrhage; previa with prior cesarean gives 11%, 40%, 61% for one, two, three or more.
**Trade-offs / caveats**: Fragmented placenta may show only deficient decidua; one focus is not diagnostic. 18% primiparous.

## Twin-twin transfusion syndrome (TTTS)
**When you see it**: Monochorionic placenta with deep arteriovenous anastomoses; discordant growth and amniotic fluid.
**How to confirm**: Injection studies map anastomoses and chorionicity.
**Clinical correlation**: Quintero I–IV; cerebral palsy 22%; untreated severe TTTS has poor survival.
**Trade-offs / caveats**: Superficial anastomoses protect (5.6% TTTS); deep anastomoses alone give TTTS in 100%. Laser distorts the map.

## Hydrops with fetal anemia
**When you see it**: ≥2 fetal fluid collections with a pale, enlarged, immature placenta and Hofbauer hyperplasia.
**How to confirm**: middle cerebral artery (MCA) peak velocity >1.5 MoM; antibody screen; Kleihauer-Betke for fetomaternal hemorrhage; parvovirus PCR.
**Clinical correlation**: Hemoglobin <6 g/dL for 4–6 days produces hydrops; >50 mL fetomaternal hemorrhage causes hydrops in 7.5%.
**Trade-offs / caveats**: Non-immune causes dominate; the placenta rarely gives the cause. Anti-K leads hemolytic disease of the fetus and newborn (HDFN) in the USA.

## Infectious villitis
**When you see it**: Patchy or diffuse lymphoplasmacytic villitis, necrosis, inclusions or calcification.
**How to confirm**: CMV, toxoplasma, syphilis, HSV stains; Steiner for Treponema; plasma cells suggest infection.
**Clinical correlation**: CMV is a major cause; Zika causes villitis, calcification, MVM and intervillous thrombosis; syphilis causes necrotizing funisitis.
**Trade-offs / caveats**: Negative stain does not exclude Ureaplasma or unscreened organisms. VUE is the default.

## Placental mesenchymal dysplasia versus partial mole
**When you see it**: Placentomegaly with cystic stem villi, dilated chorionic vessels and normal trophoblast.
**How to confirm**: p57 normal (biparental), ploidy diploid, no trophoblast proliferation; look for Beckwith-Wiedemann features.
**Clinical correlation**: 1/4000; may coexist with a normal fetus or Beckwith-Wiedemann syndrome (BWS); FVM, anemia may occur.
**Trade-offs / caveats**: Grape-like cysts mimic partial mole. PMD spares trophoblast; partial mole is triploid, p57-positive.

## Extrachorial placenta with bleeding
**When you see it**: Circumvallate (plicated ring, 0.62–5.25%) or circummarginate (no ring, 1.8–25.5%) membrane insertion.
**How to confirm**: Inspect fetal surface and insertion; section the margin for fibrin and hematoma.
**Clinical correlation**: First-trimester bleeding, PPROM, preterm delivery, abruption; fetal vessels may rupture.
**Trade-offs / caveats**: Partial circumvallation is common; the ring is not the lesion. Not extramembranous pregnancy.

## Vasa previa with velamentous insertion
**When you see it**: Membranous vessels crossing the internal os; velamentous cord in 1.11% of deliveries.
**How to confirm**: Follow vessels; antenatal color Doppler; examine membranes after delivery.
**Clinical correlation**: Rupture exsanguinates in minutes; 1 in 50 velamentous insertions; mortality 58–73%.
**Trade-offs / caveats**: Velamentous vessels are not vasa previa; vasa previa can accompany a marginal cord.
