# Chapter 7: Architecture of the Villous Trees

## Core Idea
The villous tree is a hierarchy of five villous types with defined caliber, stroma, vessel type, and function, and their proportions change predictably with gestation. Diagnosis of maturation disorders rests on low-power pattern recognition across a full-thickness section, not on individual villous profiles. Villous maldevelopment means an abnormal preponderance of otherwise normal villous types for the stated gestational age.

## Frameworks Introduced

**Kaufmann classification.** Stem villi (truncus chorii, up to four generations of rami chorii, 2–30 generations of ramuli chorii with mean 10, and anchoring villi), intermediate villi (immature and mature), terminal villi, and mesenchymal villi.

**Amsterdam classification of maldevelopment.** Distal villous hypoplasia: paucity of peripheral villi, especially terminal villi, relative to stem villi, in at least 30% of a full-thickness section. Delayed villous maturation: uniform lack of maturation across peripheral villi, rarely before 34 weeks, in at least 30% of a section, graded focal or diffuse. Accelerated villous maturation: preponderance of small hypermature villi with clusters of terminal villi linked by false syncytial knots.

**Sampling rule.** At least four full-thickness blocks: three from normal-appearing parenchyma in the central two-thirds of the disc, one including a membrane roll.

## Key Concepts
- Stem villi make up 20–25% of villous volume at term; caliber falls from about 3000 μm at the plate to 80 μm in the smallest ramuli.
- Stem villus subtypes: type I above 250 μm, type II 120–300 μm, type III below 150 μm.
- Immature intermediate villi: first formed about week 6, dominant from 14 to 20 weeks, 0–5% of volume at term, absent in hypermature placentas.
- Mature intermediate villi measure 60–100 μm and make up about 25% of volume at term; trophoblast over capillaries is below 2–3 μm.
- Terminal villi measure 30–80 μm and make up about 40% of volume at 37 weeks. A terminal villus contains no vessels other than capillaries and sinusoids, and vascular lumens occupy at least half the stromal volume.
- Vasculosyncytial membranes cover 30–40% of terminal villous surface. Fetal blood velocity through a sinusoid decelerates by about 80%.
- Total villous surface area: 0.5–0.6 m² at 5–6 months, 2.8 m² at 24 weeks, about 12.5 m² at term. Capillary length is about 550 km at term.
- Mean trophoblastic thickness is 7.4 μm at 24 weeks. Villous membrane thickness at term is reported between 5.7 and 3.5 μm. Diffusion distance is about 10 μm at 24 weeks.
- Syncytial knots are on about 7% of villi at 24 weeks and about 30% at term.
- Lobules number 30–50, with diameter 2–3 cm centrally and 1 cm at the margin. Each has a villus-free central cavity.
- Distal villous hypoplasia: wall shear stress is 0.2 Pa in severe cases versus 0.05 Pa normally, which inhibits endothelial migration and favours elongation over branching.
- Fibrinoid above 10% of total villous volume is exceptional and suggests disease.

## Anti-patterns
- Assessing maturation from one field, a semithin section, an electron micrograph, or a chorionic villus sample.
- Assuming a villous profile's position in the tree from its two-dimensional appearance.
- Counting false syncytial knots as pathology.
- Calling reticular stroma edema.
- Diagnosing delayed maturation before 34 weeks or below the 30% threshold.
- Using tissue next to an infarct to judge maturation.
- Treating hypercapillarization as chorangiosis without excluding cord compression.

## Key Takeaways
1. Report villous type proportions by gestational age.
2. Apply the 30% threshold to all three maldevelopment patterns.
3. Sample four full-thickness blocks, three central and one membrane roll.
4. Diagnose at low power.
5. Use 2.8 m² at 24 weeks and 12.5 m² at term as maturity indices.
6. Read hypermature clustering with false knots as accelerated maturation.
