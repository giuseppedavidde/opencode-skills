# Chapter 30: Chorangiotic Lesions, Benign Tumors, and Heterotopias

## Core Idea
Vascular proliferations are by far the most common benign tumors and tumor-like lesions of the placenta. They form a spectrum defined by location: chorangioma is a discrete mass, chorangiomatosis is a non-expansile focus permeating stem and immature intermediate villi, and chorangiosis is a diffuse terminal-villous capillary proliferation linked to chronic hypoxia. Clinical effects depend mainly on tumor size, not histologic subtype. True placental sarcomas have never been documented, and other benign tumors and heterotopias are incidental.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Location-based classification:** chorangioma (discrete, often subchorionic mass), chorangiomatosis (focal, segmental, or diffuse; stem and immature intermediate villi), chorangiosis (diffuse; terminal villi; stem villi spared).
- **Altshuler criteria for chorangiosis:** more than 10 capillaries in more than 10 terminal villi in at least 10 microscopic fields at 10× objective, in three non-infarcted, non-ischemic areas. Grades 1 to 3 by vessel profusion. Subthreshold cases are called "focal chorangiosis."
- **Structural discriminator:** chorangioma and chorangiomatosis have a contiguous layer of MSA-positive pericytes and a loose lattice-like reticulin network; chorangiosis lacks pericytes, has distinct basement membrane around capillaries, and lacks the reticulin lattice.
- **Heterotopia catalogue:** hepatic tissue (often called hepatocellular adenoma), adrenal cortical tissue, teratoma, and smooth muscle tumors.

## Key Concepts
- Historical chorangioma incidence was 1 in 9000 to 1 in 50,000; systematic placental study finds up to 1 in 100. Wentworth found 1 in 77, Wallenburg 1 in 117, Soma 1 in 500. Frequency is higher at high altitude (Nepal) and in Japanese women (2.5–7.6%).
- Chorangioma gross: fleshy, dark red to tan, congested, often bulging from the fetal surface, often peripheral; may be partially infarcted. About 40% show prominent trophoblastic proliferation.
- **Size cutoff:** 4 cm is cited as the clinically relevant threshold for increased risk of complications. Complications include polyhydramnios, hydrops fetalis, fetal anemia, cardiac failure from arteriovenous shunting, growth restriction, preterm delivery, stillbirth, neonatal thrombocytopenia, Kasabach-Merritt syndrome, disseminated intravascular coagulation, prenatal cerebral embolism, abruption, and elevated hCG. Maternal "mirror" or triple-edema syndrome can occur.
- Giant tumors: 1500 g (30 × 20 × 5 cm) is the probable record; a 900 g (14 × 13 × 10 cm) tumor occurred in a 950 g placenta, and a 7-cm tumor accompanied periventricular leukomalacia.
- Multiple gestations, preeclampsia, vascular tumors of the neonate, and malformed neonates are overrepresented. There is no reproducible copy-number abnormality on array-CGH, supporting a non-neoplastic origin.
- **Chorangiomatosis:** placenta may be large and bulky, especially multifocal; may cause luteinized ovarian cysts from excess gonadotropin. Diffuse multifocal disease is associated with extreme prematurity (less than 32 weeks), IUGR, congenital malformations, abnormal villous maturation, and fetoplacental vascular abnormalities. Focal and segmental types resemble chorangioma and are linked to late preterm birth, multiple births, and preeclampsia.
- **Chorangiosis:** associated with stillbirth, IUGR, congenital malformations, cerebral palsy, low Apgar scores, cord abnormalities, smoking, air pollution, and high altitude (studied at 3000 m). Perinatal death occurred in 39% and congenital anomalies in 27% in one series.
- Chorangiosis counting is method-dependent: CD34 immunohistochemistry yields higher counts (8–15 in nonchorangiomatous villi), so H&E alone overestimates the lesion; chorangiomatous villi may contain 20 or more capillaries.
- **Hepatic heterotopia:** well-circumscribed nodule of hepatocytes without portal tracts, bile ducts, central veins, or bile pigment; may contain glycogen, α-fetoprotein, α1-antitrypsin, and extramedullary hematopoiesis. Reported mass 7.0 × 4.2 × 2.7 cm. Differential: decidua, X-cell islands, chorangioma, metastatic tumor; immunohistochemistry is definitive. No malignant behavior reported.
- **Adrenal cortical heterotopia:** small islands resembling zona fasciculata, typically a 2-mm nodule in a villus near the fetal plate, never in the intervillous space; no clinical significance.
- **Placental teratoma:** usually on the fetal surface between amnion and chorion, possibly in membranes; differential is fetus papyraceous; prognosis favorable.
- **Smooth muscle tumors:** most reported cases proved to be entrapped maternal leiomyomas on molecular analysis; two older reports describe primary benign leiomyomas of membranes and villous mesenchyme.

## Anti-patterns
- Do not overinterpret vascular congestion as chorangiosis; congestion distends existing vessels, whereas chorangiosis reflects endothelial proliferation.
- Do not count vessels on H&E alone without recognizing that blood removal or infusion alters the appearance.
- Do not assume every placenta with chorangiosis produces an affected infant; the lesion is a signal, not a diagnosis.
- Do not diagnose placental sarcoma. Mitoses and cellularity in atypical cellular chorangioma have not been followed by invasion or metastasis.
- Do not call hepatic heterotopia an adenoma without evidence of neoplasia; the nodules are well circumscribed and benign.
- Do not misclassify a teratoma as a vanishing twin without checking for organized fetal growth.

## Key Takeaways
1. Classify a vascular lesion by location first: mass, stem-villus permeation, or terminal-villus diffusion.
2. Measure chorangiomas and report any lesion 4 cm or larger because of hydrops and anemia risk.
3. Apply Altshuler criteria strictly for chorangiosis and state when criteria are only partially met.
4. Use MSA and reticulin to separate chorangioma/chorangiomatosis from chorangiosis when needed.
5. Recognize hepatic and adrenal heterotopias as benign incidental findings and distinguish them from metastases.
