# Chapter 11: Approach to Histologic Examination of the Placenta

## Core Idea
Placental histology is pattern-based and low-power. A fixed review of five compartments, plus eight questions about the villi, identifies nearly all significant pathology and avoids overdiagnosis of normal variation. Most diagnoses are made at low and medium power; high power is for organisms and fine detail. Reproducibility between experienced placental pathologists is limited, so the Amsterdam nomenclature is the common vocabulary.

## Frameworks Introduced

**Five-compartment review.** Umbilical cord; fetal membranes; chorionic plate; chorionic villi; basal plate and decidua.

**Eight-question villous checklist.**
1. Do villi fill the section with small gaps? Loss of maternal flow collapses the villous space and makes it look denser.
2. Are intervillous spaces very large and empty, or lost? Large spaces suggest poor growth or villous hypoplasia. Villi should never touch; agglutination suggests villitis or poor perfusion.
3. Is there gradation from stem to terminal villi? Malperfusion often shows small terminal villi with one capillary lumen and stem villi with no intervening sizes.
4. Is the tissue too pale or too dark? Knotting darkens term villi; fetal hematocrit and anemia determine pallor.
5. Is the appearance uniform? Extreme variation suggests dysmaturity. Some variation is normal near injection sites and at margins.
6. Are there non-vascularized or inflamed groups of villi? This indicates avascular villi or villitis.
7. Does the intervillous space contain excess cells or fibrinoid? Histiocytes suggest chronic histiocytic intervillositis. Fibrin increases with gestational age. Massive perivillous fibrin deposition requires diffuse or extensive maternal floor involvement.
8. Are all villous slides similar? If not, check whether a gross lesion was sampled and whether sampling was adequate.

**Standard slide set.** Four slides: three of villous tissue including both surfaces, one with two cord pieces plus one or two membrane rolls.
**Significance threshold.** Abnormal deposits must be fairly abundant, not a rare focus or one macrophage. A single abnormal area indicates a specific disease only in exceptional cases, such as acid-fast bacilli or spirochetes.

## Key Concepts
- Blocks nearly fill a cassette, so sections fill most of the slide. Ideally the section is a complete face.
- Membranes are often incomplete, missing amnion, chorion, or decidua, and the amnion is often detached.
- Cord: confirm two arteries and one vein. A full circumferential section is mandatory to diagnose a single umbilical artery; do not mistake Hyrtl's anastomosis. Most cord vessel disruption is artifactual.
- Cord inflammation is commonest in the vein but may involve arteries and extend into Wharton's jelly. Subamniotic microabscesses suggest Candida.
- Membrane inflammation is entirely maternal, from decidual vessels; neutrophils migrate toward the amnion and line up first where decidua meets chorion.
- A localized focus of intense neutrophilic infiltration and necrosis at the rupture site does not indicate significant ascending infection.
- Chronic inflammatory cells in attached decidua are usually lymphocytes, sometimes plasma cells, and are better associated with chronic villitis.
- Implantation site vessels normally lose smooth muscle; decidua vera vessels do not. Failure of conversion is seen in pre-eclampsia, sometimes with atherosis, fibrinoid, and lipid-filled cells.
- Meconium: recent passage shows loose squames with mucin pigment on the surface; later it lies in subamnionic connective tissue and then in macrophages of chorion and decidua.
- Chorionic plate vessels are the largest after the cord and a common site of fetal vascular thrombosis.
- Eosinophilic T-cell chorionic vasculitis occurs only in the chorionic plate.
- Villitis of unknown etiology obscures small villous vessels. Fetal vascular malperfusion produces avascular areas with degenerating vessels.
- Maturation must be compared with gestational age; aberrations of maturation are the commonest villous pathology.

## Anti-patterns
- Diagnosing at high power only; normal placenta can look alarming at 40x and 100x.
- Overcalling minimal or questionable inflammation.
- Diagnosing massive perivillous fibrin deposition from a localized aggregate.
- Diagnosing a single umbilical artery on an incomplete cord section.
- Treating a focal necrotic focus at the rupture site as ascending infection.
- Treating meconium pigment and hemosiderin as interchangeable without a stain.

## Key Takeaways
1. Review the five compartments in a fixed order.
2. Apply the eight villous questions at low power, then confirm at medium power.
3. Require abundance or wide distribution before calling a deposit or infiltrate pathologic.
4. Confirm two arteries and one vein on a full circumferential section.
5. State maturation as normal, delayed, accelerated, or distal villous hypoplasia.
6. Report only widespread or major processes in the final diagnosis.
