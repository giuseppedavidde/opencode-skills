# Chapter 22: Fetal Vascular Malperfusion

## Core Idea
Fetal vascular malperfusion (FVM) is the fetal counterpart of maternal vascular malperfusion: obstruction of the fetal–placental circulation produces ischemic damage to the most distal villi. Because the fetal circulation is a closed, low-pressure system with no collateral supply, obstruction at any level — cord, chorionic plate, stem villus, or terminal capillary — produces the same downstream lesion: avascular villi. The chapter's achievement is to replace a set of confusing historical names with the Amsterdam terminology and to convert FVM from a descriptive finding into a graded diagnosis with explicit quantitative thresholds.

## Frameworks Introduced

**Two axes: distribution and grade.** FVM is classified by distribution as *segmental* (a thrombosed vessel supplying part of the placenta, with a demarcated villous territory) or *global* (abnormally distributed over much of the placenta). It is separately graded as low or high grade.

**Amsterdam components of FVM.** Six named lesions:
1. **Thrombus** — recent or remote, in chorionic plate or stem vessels.
2. **Stem villous obliteration** — previously "fibromuscular sclerosis"; thickening of the vascular wall with luminal obliteration, usually adjacent to the cord insertion, with laminated fibrin in intima or muscular wall; older lesions may show dystrophic calcification.
3. **Intramural fibrin deposition** — previously "intimal fibrin cushion"; myofibroblastic proliferation protruding into the lumen like a cushion; by definition non-occlusive and generally not significantly obstructive.
4. **Villous stromal-vascular karyorrhexis (VSK)** — the preferred term for "hemorrhagic endovasculitis", a misnomer because there is no inflammation. Characterised by karyorrhexis with congestion, red cell extravasation, stromal apoptosis, and septation of the endothelial lumen.
5. **Avascular villi** — small foci contain fewer than ten villi, large foci more than ten.
6. **Vascular ectasia** — luminal dilation from increased venous pressure, in a chorionic plate or stem vessel.

**High-grade FVM threshold.** Any of the following:
- More than one focus of avascular villi — a cumulative assessment of at least 45 avascular villi over 3 sections examined, or an average of more than 15 villi per section — with or without thrombus.
- Two or more occlusive or non-occlusive thrombi in the chorionic plate or major stem villi.
- Multiple non-occlusive thrombi.

**Low-grade FVM.** One large vessel thrombosis, and/or small foci (fewer than five villi) involved in fewer than three foci.

## Key Concepts
- Avascular villi are direct evidence of thrombosis of the fetal circulation even when the thrombus is not identified.
- Avascular villi are distinguished from infarcted villi by the viable trophoblast surrounding the atrophic villus; avascular villi may show increased syncytial knotting.
- Stem villous obliteration in isolation is not diagnostic of FVM; the significance of one or two isolated lesions is unclear.
- VSK has a temporal evolution and represents a microangiopathy analogous to the glomerulopathy of hemolytic uremic syndrome, following endothelial injury and thrombosis.
- In VSK the villous trophoblast remains intact because the maternal circulation is unaffected.
- Umbilical coiling index: normal is one coil per 5 cm, or 0.2 ± 0.1 (1 SD) coils per cm. A coil is a complete 360° spiral. Hypocoiling is less than 1 coil per 10 cm; hypercoiling is more than 3 coils per 10 cm.
- Coiling is set by about 10 weeks' gestation; abnormal coiling is found in 6–21% of pregnancies.
- Gross patterns of hypercoiling (Ernst classification): segmented (indentations involving less than 50% of the cord diameter) and linked (deeper indentations, more than 50% of the diameter, between coils).
- Predisposing maternal conditions include diabetes (23%) and hypertension (about 30%).
- In one stillbirth series, associated gross findings were: body cord 44%, abnormal insertion 30%, long cord 33.5%, true knot 27.2%, and narrow or hypercoiled cord 4.5%; almost 30% had more than one gross abnormality.
- FVM is more frequent in monochorionic than dichorionic twin placentas (7.5% versus 3.1%).
- FVM occurs in about 30% of placentas with chronic villitis of unknown etiology.
- FVM is associated with fetal growth restriction and absent end-diastolic flow on Doppler.
- Magistral and dispersed patterns of placental surface arteries are described; the dispersed pattern is more common (61.8%) than the magistral type (38.2%) in one series.

## Anti-patterns
- **Calling avascular villi "infarcts".** Infarcts are maternal-side lesions with dead trophoblast; avascular villi retain viable trophoblast.
- **Using "hemorrhagic endovasculitis."** The lesion has no inflammation; use villous stromal-vascular karyorrhexis.
- **Using "fibromuscular sclerosis" and "intimal fibrin cushion."** Use stem villous obliteration and intramural fibrin deposition.
- **Diagnosing FVM from a single small focus.** One or two isolated lesions of stem villous obliteration are of unclear significance.
- **Counting thrombi without measuring the villous burden.** Grade depends on cumulative avascular villi over three sections, not on the thrombus alone.
- **Assuming a missing thrombus excludes FVM.** Avascular villi prove thrombosis even when no thrombus is found.

## Key Takeaways
1. Grade FVM explicitly as low or high using the 45-villi-over-three-sections and 2-thrombus thresholds.
2. State the distribution as segmental or global.
3. Distinguish avascular villi from infarcted villi by trophoblast viability.
4. Use the Amsterdam names: stem villous obliteration, intramural fibrin deposition, villous stromal-vascular karyorrhexis, vascular ectasia.
5. Measure the coiling index and record hypocoiling (less than 1 coil per 10 cm) or hypercoiling (more than 3 coils per 10 cm).
6. Correlate FVM with growth restriction, absent end-diastolic flow, and stillbirth, and look for cord and insertion abnormalities that explain the obstruction.
