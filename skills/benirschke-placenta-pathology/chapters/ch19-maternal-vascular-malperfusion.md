# Chapter 19: Maternal Vascular Malperfusion and Associated Maternal Diseases

## Core Idea
Maternal vascular malperfusion (MVM) is a pattern diagnosis, not a single lesion: it reflects abnormal oxygenation and flow dynamics in the intervillous space caused by failed or incomplete remodeling of maternal spiral arteries. No lesion is pathognomonic and lesions frequently coexist, so the Amsterdam Placental Workshop Group Consensus replaced idiosyncratic terminology with a defined set of gross and microscopic findings and explicit severity thresholds. The clinical stakes are high because MVM is the placental substrate of preeclampsia, fetal growth restriction, stillbirth, and preterm birth, and it programs the offspring for adult cardiovascular and metabolic disease.

## Frameworks Introduced

**Amsterdam Placental Workshop Group Consensus framework (2014 conference).** Replaced the term "maternal vascular underperfusion" with malperfusion, because not all of the pathology reflects intervillous hypoxia; some represents sequelae of hyperoxia. The consensus defines the lesions regarded as indicative of MVM:
- Placental hypoplasia.
- Evidence of loss of maternal vascular integrity: placental abruption (arterial) and marginal abruption (venous).
- Chorionic villous infarction.
- Abnormalities of villous development: distal villous hypoplasia and accelerated villous maturation.
- Decidual arteriopathy.

**Amsterdam severity threshold.** Definitive MVM requires **placental weight below the tenth percentile for the stated gestational week AND more than 30% parenchymal involvement**, separating partial MVM from segmental/complete MVM.

**Mestan two-grade system (published, not consensus-recommended).**
- **Grade 1 (mild to moderate):** placental weight within published norms for gestational week, less than 30% parenchymal involvement, and no more than one marginal infarct.
- **Grade 2 (severe):** multiple non-marginal infarcts.

**Two histopathologic patterns of MVM.**
- **Global/partial MVM:** most maternal decidual vessels are pathologically changed but spiral arteries are only partially occluded. Main features are distal villous hypoplasia (early change) and accelerated villous maturation (late change).
- **Segmental/complete MVM:** spiral artery occlusion is evident and is accompanied by areas of villous infarction.

**Hertig's three phases of decidual vasculopathy (1945, "acute degenerative arteriolitis").** Phase 1: collection of foamy fat-laden mononuclear leukocytes or phagocytes beneath the intima of the spiral artery. Phase 2: fibrinoid degeneration of the media. Phase 3: fibroblastic proliferation of the intima with near-complete obliteration of the lumen.

## Key Concepts
- Placental hypoplasia is defined as placental weight below the tenth centile for gestational age and context, and/or a thin cord (below the tenth centile, or less than 8 mm diameter at term). The National Collaborative Perinatal Project used below the tenth percentile for three dimensions: weight, thickness, and chorionic plate area.
- Chorionic plate area reflects how many maternal spiral arteries can supply the placenta and plateaus in the middle of the third trimester. Disc thickness reflects arborization of the villous capillary bed and increases mainly from 30–32 weeks.
- Early-onset preeclampsia (before 34 weeks) is typically associated with reduced placental weight, reduced chorionic plate dimensions, and reduced mural thickness.
- Placental infarction is localized ischemic villous necrosis from spiral artery occlusion. Infarcts occur in about 20% of uncomplicated pregnancies, 40% of mild preeclampsia, and 70% of severe preeclampsia.
- Infarcts are recent (early/acute), subacute, or remote. Recent infarcts are dark red, ill-defined, and firmer than the spongier normal parenchyma; aging produces yellow, then tan-gray colour as red cells lose integrity and fetal hemoglobin iron oxidizes. Fixation enhances the whitish appearance of chronic infarcts.
- Distal villous hypoplasia (synonyms: deficiency of intermediate villi, distal villous hypomaturation) is defined as paucity of villi affecting **more than 30% of all distal villi** in relation to the surrounding stem villi. Villi are thin, relatively elongated, and have decreased modal diameter; stem villous stroma is fibrotic with centralized vessels and diminished vasculosyncytial membranes.
- Accelerated villous maturation produces hypermature, hypoplastic, slender terminal villi resembling term histology with considerably increased intervillous space; the architecture is likened to "bonsai" trees. Features of accelerated maturation and distal villous hypoplasia may coexist.
- Syncytial knotting (increased syncytial knots, sprouts, bridges) is influenced by section thickness; most nuclear accumulations are flat sections of irregular villous surfaces. Increased knotting alone points to abnormal villous shape, not to a specific diagnosis.
- Decidual arteriopathy is an overarching term for abnormalities in decidual vessels of the membrane roll and/or basal plate: deficient spiral artery remodeling, fibrinoid necrosis with or without foam cells, acute atherosis, mural hypertrophy, chronic perivasculitis, arterial thrombosis, and persistence of intramural endovascular trophoblast in the third trimester.

## Anti-patterns
- **Searching for a pathognomonic MVM lesion.** No lesion is unequivocally linked to one physiologic cause or clinical syndrome, and lesions coexist.
- **Diagnosing MVM on a single finding.** The Amsterdam threshold requires low placental weight plus more than 30% parenchymal involvement.
- **Over-reading syncytial knots.** Knot counts vary with section thickness and reflect villous shape rather than hypoxia severity.
- **Using placental weight alone to exclude MVM.** Fetal growth restriction can occur with normal or even increased placental weight.
- **Examining only fixed tissue.** Some lesions are more detectable fresh, and unfixed inspection and palpation reveal colour, texture, and vascular patency better.
- **Treating the Mestan grades as consensus.** No grading system is currently recommended for MVM.

## Key Takeaways
1. Use the Amsterdam lesion names verbatim so reports are comparable across institutions.
2. Apply the tenth-percentile weight and 30% parenchymal involvement thresholds before calling MVM.
3. Distinguish global/partial from segmental/complete MVM, because the patterns imply different degrees of spiral artery occlusion.
4. Measure placental weight, thickness, and chorionic plate area, and record cord diameter against the 8 mm term cut-off.
5. Report infarct number, size, and distribution (marginal versus non-marginal), since multiple non-marginal infarcts define severe disease.
6. State the decidual arteriopathy elements individually rather than using the term alone.
