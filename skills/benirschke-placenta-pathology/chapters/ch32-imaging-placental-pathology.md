# Chapter 32: Imaging of Placental Pathology

## Core Idea
Antenatal imaging can identify placental lesions before delivery, but detection depends on lesion type, size, and timing. Ultrasound remains the mainstay; MRI is an adjunct with higher sensitivity for some parenchymal lesions. No single sign is diagnostic for abnormally invasive placenta. Imaging findings must be interpreted against gestational age and confirmed by postpartum pathology.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Systematic in utero placental evaluation:** location, size (thickness and/or volume), implantation, morphology, anatomy, and search for anomalies — mirroring the systematic approach to gross placental examination after delivery.
- **Modality hierarchy:** 2D gray-scale B mode for anatomy; color and pulsed Doppler for flow; 3D and 4D power Doppler for vascular volume; MRI (T2, T1, diffusion-weighted, BOLD, arterial spin labeling, dynamic contrast) for tissue characterization.
- **Lesion-specific sign sets:** molar disease, parenchymal infarction, intervillous thrombus and placental lake, retroplacental hemorrhage, subchorionic thrombohematoma (Breus mole), PMD, chorangioma, placenta accreta spectrum, placenta previa and vasa previa, and twin-twin transfusion.

## Key Concepts
- Ultrasound uses frequencies above 20,000 Hz. Real-time B mode became standard in the 1970s; color and pulsed Doppler became available in the early 1980s.
- Normal placental thickness increases by about 1 mm per week of gestation. Third-trimester placentas show normal echolucencies and calcification, usually near the basal plate and septa; calcification also occurs in infarcts and perivillous or subchorionic fibrin plaques.
- MRI: the placenta is homogeneous in early mid pregnancy; lobules appear in late mid pregnancy, and from 36 weeks stratification is seen. Higher field strength (3T versus 1.5T) improves detail. Gadolinium-based contrast safety remains uncertain, with no adverse effects reported in small series but concern from cohort data.
- **Molar pregnancy:** abnormal villi form "grape-like" cysts; complete moles have more florid sonographic features, partial moles are subtler; MRI is not usually needed except for malignant transformation or a mole with a coexisting twin.
- **PMD:** estimated incidence about 1 in 4000. Ultrasound shows a cystic placenta with hypoechoic areas in 80%, enlargement or thickening in 50%, and dilated chorionic vessels in 16%. Descriptions include "Swiss-cheese" or "moth-eaten." Diffuse change mixed with normal parenchyma and a normal fetus favors PMD over mole; PMD carries no malignant trophoblastic risk.
- **Parenchymal infarction:** antenatal detection by ultrasound is low. Infarcts lie near the maternal surface, are more visible with hemorrhage, begin hypoechoic and become echogenic, sometimes with calcification. Cooley described an echo-poor lesion with a characteristic halo. MRI detected infarcts with 96% sensitivity and 63% specificity.
- **Retroplacental hemorrhage:** only about one-third of clinically suspected lesions are visualized. Acute hemorrhage is hyperechoic to isoechoic to placenta; it becomes hypoechoic after about 1 week and sonolucent after about 2 weeks. Criteria include preplacental or retroplacental collection, "jello-like" movement, marginal, intra-amniotic and subchorionic hematoma, and heterogeneous placental thickness greater than 5 cm in the perpendicular plane. Color Doppler shows no flow within the clot.
- **Breus mole (subchorionic thrombohematoma):** a large maternal clot separating from the villous chorion, not a molar pregnancy; mixed echogenicity, possible septations and fluid-fluid levels; MRI and color Doppler help separate it from PMD and chorangioma.
- **Chorangioma:** hypoechoic mass, often near cord insertion; larger tumors (more than 5 cm) produce hyperdynamic fetal vascularity pulsating at fetal heart rate, polyhydramnios, and fetal anemia; assess with middle cerebral artery Doppler.
- **Placenta accreta spectrum:** no single imaging feature is diagnostic. Ultrasound signs include multiple placental lakes with irregular margins, loss of the retroplacental space, placenta previa, heterogeneous parenchyma, asymmetric thickness, myometrial thinning, bladder wall abnormality, and myometrial or bladder hypervascularity. MRI signs include bladder invasion and placental bulge (high accuracy by expert opinion), placental heterogeneity, asymmetric shape, T2-hypointense parenchymal bands, tortuous intraparenchymal vessels, and enlarged pelvic veins.
- **Placenta previa:** transvaginal ultrasound is the method of diagnosis; definitive diagnosis is usually made around 34 weeks because low-lying placentas may resolve with uterine growth.
- **Vasa previa:** color Doppler, often transvaginal, is required; waveforms must be analyzed because maternal vessels at the placental edge can mimic vasa previa.
- **Twin-twin transfusion:** velamentous cord insertion and unequal placental sharing are common; MCA peak systolic velocity greater than 1.5 multiples of the median in the donor suggests fetal anemia.

## Anti-patterns
- Do not rely on a single sign for placenta accreta spectrum; sensitivity and specificity vary with the criteria used.
- Do not assume a normal ultrasound excludes abruption; most suspected cases are not visualized.
- Do not equate placental lakes with disease; most are harmless, and significance depends on gestational age, number, and size.
- Do not confuse PMD with partial mole on imaging; the presence of a normal fetus and diffuse change mixed with normal parenchyma favors PMD.
- Do not diagnose vasa previa from gray-scale alone; confirm fetal waveforms with Doppler.

## Key Takeaways
1. Evaluate the placenta systematically in utero, as one would at gross examination.
2. Measure thickness and describe echogenicity, calcification, and vascularity in every abnormal placenta.
3. Use MRI when ultrasound is equivocal for infarction, accreta, or a complex cystic placenta.
4. Confirm all antenatal placental diagnoses against postpartum pathology.
5. In twin pregnancy, document cord insertion and placental sharing because they drive transfusion risk.
