# Chapter 9: The Placental Circulations

## Core Idea
The placenta carries two circulations that must be matched in pressure, velocity, and timing. The fetal-placental side is a low-resistance, high-flow system that receives at least 40% of fetal cardiac output and ends in a capillary bed about 550 km long. The maternal side is made safe by physiological conversion of spiral arteries, which turns muscular resistance vessels into flaccid conduits and lowers inflow velocity by roughly two orders of magnitude. Failure of conversion produces high-velocity jet inflow, uneven perfusion, and chronic ischemia-reperfusion injury rather than simple global hypoxia.

## Frameworks Introduced

**Fetal hierarchy.** Umbilical arteries, chorionic plate vessels, stem villous arteries (the resistance vessels), arterioles in intermediate villi, terminal capillaries and sinusoids, venules, umbilical vein.

**Maternal hierarchy.** Uterine, arcuate, radial, and spiral arteries, intervillous space, uteroplacental veins through the basal plate.

**Physiological conversion.** Remodelling of the distal spiral artery into a dilated, flaccid, non-contractile conduit. Its extent mirrors invasion depth and covers the endometrial segment and inner third of the myometrium, including the contractile junctional segment.

**Terminal villus definition.** No vessels other than capillaries and sinusoids, with vascular lumens occupying at least half the stromal volume.

## Key Concepts
- The placenta receives at least 40% of fetal cardiac output.
- Total capillary length reaches about 550 km at term. The capillary-to-villus length ratio is constant at about 3–4 from the second trimester.
- Arterioles of 20–40 μm continue directly into terminal capillaries by gradual loss of smooth muscle. No precapillary sphincters have been identified.
- Stem villous arteries are the resistance vessels. They are not innervated; tone depends on nitric oxide, carbon monoxide, and hydrogen sulfide.
- Spiral artery diameter increases from 0.4–0.5 mm at the myometrial-endometrial junction to 2.4 mm at the intervillous opening.
- Inflow velocity falls from 2–3 m/s to about 10 cm/s. Calculated flow rises only from 0.27 to 0.37 ml/s, so conversion mainly prevents uneven perfusion and mechanical damage.
- Intervillous pressure is about 10 mmHg; fetal capillary pressure is about 20 mmHg. This gradient distends capillaries and thins the membrane, the "sluice flow" phenomenon.
- Maternal blood transit time is about 25–30 s.
- Maternal arterial flow does not begin throughout the placenta until 10–12 weeks; endovascular trophoblast plugs the arteries before then.
- Arterial openings in the basal plate number 263 in one fresh-placenta count and 25 by cineradiography in another.
- Oxygen tension in the subchorial lake falls from about 60 mmHg at 16 weeks to about 40 mmHg at term. Values near 30 mmHg occur at Cesarean delivery in women breathing air.
- Conversion is greater in the centre of the bed than at the periphery.
- When conversion fails, maternal blood enters at 1–2 m/s. Turbulent flow ruptures anchoring villi and forms echogenic cystic lesions lined by thrombus.
- Deficient conversion is associated with the "Great Obstetrical Syndromes" and a chronic ischemia-reperfusion pattern of injury.

## Anti-patterns
- Attributing all placental hypoxia to failed conversion; conversion mainly affects uniformity of perfusion.
- Assuming maternal blood enters the intervillous space at implantation.
- Measuring villous or capillary dimensions after delayed clamping or immersion fixation; sinusoids collapse within minutes.
- Calling every hypercapillarized villus chorangiosis without excluding cord compression, knots, torsion, or nuchal cord.
- Assuming a low-resistance uterine artery waveform proves low intraplacental resistance.
- Comparing spiral artery counts across studies without stating the method.

## Key Takeaways
1. Use 40% of fetal cardiac output and 550 km of capillary length as the scale of the fetal circulation.
2. Treat conversion as a velocity- and pressure-reducing mechanism, not a flow-increasing one.
3. Expect intervillous pressure near 10 mmHg and fetal capillary pressure near 20 mmHg.
4. Date onset of the maternal circulation to 10–12 weeks.
5. Interpret failed conversion as a cause of uneven perfusion and ischemia-reperfusion injury.
6. Standardize fixation and cord clamping before any morphometric comparison.
