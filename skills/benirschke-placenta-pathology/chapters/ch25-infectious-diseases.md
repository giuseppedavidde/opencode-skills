# Chapter 25: Infectious Diseases

## Core Idea
Prenatal infection reaches the placenta by ascent from the endocervix, by hematogenous spread through maternal blood, rarely by iatrogenic procedures, or directly from the endometrium. Many organisms leave no specific placental trace, so the pathologist must interpret lesions against the route of infection and the gestational age. The chapter separates general acute chorioamnionitis from organism-specific patterns that carry diagnostic weight. Proof of infection often requires stains, immunohistochemistry, or molecular testing rather than morphology alone.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Route-based classification:** ascending, hematogenous, iatrogenic (amniocentesis, CVS, PUBS, intrauterine transfusion), and endometrial.
- **Leukocyte origin rule:** neutrophils in the membranes come from the intervillous space (maternal) and fetal surface vessels (fetal). Before 20 weeks, neutrophils are mainly maternal; by midtrimester the fetus contributes.
- **Directional migration ("amniotropism"):** leukocytes move toward the amniotic cavity. In funisitis they emigrate first from the umbilical vein, later from the arteries, and rarely toward the cord center.
- **Necrotizing funisitis:** rings of mineralized old exudate around umbilical vessels, a chronic marker and a clue to syphilis.
- **Organism-specific pattern set:** villitis, intervillositis, intervillous abscess, plasma cell deciduitis, funisitis, and intranuclear inclusions.

## Key Concepts
- Chorioamnionitis incidence: 24.4% of 1000 consecutive live births; 4% of uncomplicated term deliveries, of which 1.2% clinically silent. Preterm deliveries show infection in 67% versus 21% at term. Chorioamnionitis accounts for about 25% of premature deliveries.
- Gross chorioamnionitis: loss of the blue fetal-surface sheen, opaque yellow-green exudate, roughened amnion, membrane edema, and increased placental weight. Candidiasis adds small punctate yellow-white cord nodules.
- **CMV:** about 1% of pregnant women are infected and 40% transmit. Placenta may be small with growth restriction and surface vessel thrombosis; no uniform gross finding. Histology: chronic lymphoplasmacytic villitis, villous capillary thrombosis with hemosiderin, villous and trophoblast necrosis, stromal sclerosis, and cytomegalic cells with nuclear owl-eye or cytoplasmic inclusions in capillary endothelium and stromal cells, also decidua and amnion. Confirm by immunohistochemistry, PCR (including on paraffin tissue), or culture.
- **Parvovirus B19:** estimated 16% of nonimmune hydrops (other authors 5–15%); maternal infection about 1 in 400; transmission 25–33%; serious fetal disease in 9%. Inclusions are ground-glass intranuclear bodies in normoblasts, composed of 20-nm virus particle crystals; they resist autolysis. Placenta is pale, friable, enlarged, edematous, sometimes with villous necrosis and calcification. Inclusions are more likely in fetal than placental tissue.
- **HSV:** about 25% of adults have HSV-2; silent shedding 2.3% (other data 0.1–0.4%). Disseminated neonatal infection is acquired in utero in 4%, natally in 86%, postnatally in 10%. Placental pattern: bland villous necrosis without inflammation, deciduitis, plasmacytic funisitis, amnion necrosis; inclusions may be absent.
- **Syphilis:** Treponema pallidum is 4–10 µm long and 0.5 µm wide. Spirochetes appear in fetuses from 9–10 weeks. Placentomegaly is the most common finding. Russell and Altshuler criteria: relative villous immaturity, decidual plasma cell infiltration, perivascular fibrous proliferation, altered capillary endothelium. Also proliferative endarteritis with concentric fibrosis, villous abscesses, necrotizing funisitis. Gummas have never been reported. Inflammatory cells are largely maternal CD3 lymphocytes.
- **Toxoplasmosis:** Desmonts and Couvreur found 45% of women with primary infection gave birth to infected infants; transmission rose by trimester to 17%, 25%, and 65%. Fetal destruction is most severe with early infection. Placenta may be as pale as erythroblastosis; plasmacellular deciduitis, chronic or necrotizing granulomatous villitis, and cysts in villi, cord, and membranes. Tachyzoites cannot be identified on H&E.
- **Listeria:** pregnancy carries an 18-fold increased risk. Lesions are intervillous abscesses (white nodules), villous necrosis, acute necrotizing villitis, and abundant bacteria on the amniotic surface. Warthin-Starry shows curved organisms.
- **Candida:** funisitis is usually slight relative to the severity of chorioamnionitis. Cord nodules show acute inflammation beneath epithelial necrosis; H&E shows hyphae poorly, GMS or PAS is needed, and scrapings of cord lesions are diagnostic.
- **Zika:** placental changes are often absent or nonspecific — chronic villitis, increased Hofbauer cells, villous immaturity and edema, stromal fibrosis, perivillous fibrin, calcification, hypervascularity, lymphocytic deciduitis. Antigen is demonstrable in Hofbauer cells, fetal endothelium, and maternal leukocytes, mainly in first trimester.

## Anti-patterns
- Do not treat parvovirus inclusions as diagnostic. Many artifacts mimic them; PCR confirmation is mandatory.
- Do not attempt to diagnose toxoplasma tachyzoites on H&E; only cysts may be visible, and even cysts can be mimicked by artifact.
- Do not expect a specific lesion in every infection. Coxsackie and many parvovirus cases leave few or no characteristic traces.
- Do not overread necrotizing funisitis as specific; it suggests syphilis but occurs in other chronic infections.
- Do not assume transplacental HSV is the usual route; most neonatal herpes is acquired at delivery.
- Do not equate a histologically normal placenta with absence of HIV or Zika infection.

## Key Takeaways
1. Use gestational age to interpret who produced the neutrophils: maternal before 20 weeks, mixed thereafter.
2. Apply the organism-specific pattern list, then confirm with IHC, PCR, or culture before assigning an etiology.
3. In hydrops with pale, enlarged placenta, test for parvovirus and examine normoblasts for inclusions, but confirm by PCR.
4. In syphilis, look for placentomegaly, plasma cell deciduitis, villous immaturity, and perivascular fibrosis; organisms are sparse in placenta and most abundant in fetal liver.
5. Recognize Candida funisitis by cord nodules and diagnose with PAS or silver stain on a scraping.
