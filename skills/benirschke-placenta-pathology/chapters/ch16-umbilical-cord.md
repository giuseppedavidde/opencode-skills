# Chapter 16: Anatomy and Pathology of the Umbilical Cord

## Core Idea
The umbilical cord is a hydrostatic and mechanical organ with no neural control: its vessels are nerve-free effectors governed by vasoactive mediators, and its geometry is set early in gestation. Because helices are visible by the ninth week and the number of turns is fixed early, later growth occurs by increasing the pitch, not by adding coils. Almost every clinically important cord finding is therefore either a fixed developmental parameter (length, insertion, vessel number, coiling direction and index) or a mechanical failure of that parameter. The pathologist's job is to measure these parameters accurately and to separate true lesions from the many named normal variants.

## Frameworks Introduced

**Coiling nomenclature.** The umbilical coiling index (UCI) is the number of coils divided by cord length; the reported mean is 0.21/cm (0.19/cm in another series). Absent coiling is uncommon and ominous, with 4.3–4.9% of newborns lacking twists. Hypocoiling is an index below the tenth percentile; hypercoiling is at least 3 twists per 10 cm (0.3/cm). Direction is described as left (counterclockwise) or right; mixed spiralling is a separate category.

**Cord length categories.** Short cords are less than 35 cm; long cords exceed 80 cm. Gardiner's older scheme called less than 32 cm "absolutely" short and slightly more than 32 cm "relatively" short. Cord length shortens up to 7 cm in the first hours after delivery, so measurement must be standardised.

**Cord insertion classification.** Central or eccentric insertion, marginal insertion, velamentous (membranous) insertion, and furcate insertion. Marginal insertion with a broad, flattened attachment is termed a battledore placenta.

**Vessel-count classification.** Normal is two arteries and one vein. Single umbilical artery (SUA) is the commonest macroscopic placental anomaly. More than three umbilical vessels is normal for many other species.

**Named normal variants.** Hyrtl anastomosis (artery-to-artery anastomosis near the placental insertion), false knots (vascular redundancies), Hoboken nodes, Wharton's jelly edema, yolk sac remnant, and squamous metaplasia of the cord surface.

## Key Concepts
- Hyrtl anastomosis is present in 96% of cords, usually 1.5 cm from the placental insertion. In one injection series, 76 of 83 placentas had an anastomosis; 3 of the 7 without had a single umbilical artery.
- Helices are visible by the ninth week and usually number up to 40, though as many as 380 turns have been described. A left (counterclockwise) spiral predominates by about 7:1; one series found 79% left with a left/right ratio of 3.7:1.0. In twins, left direction was 61% and mixed spiralling 26%.
- Cord length: mean 59.44 cm (range 24–146 cm) in one series, 66.54 cm in another, average 61 cm (30–129 cm), 54.1 cm (17.8–121.9 cm), and 52 cm with tenth percentile 40 cm and ninetieth percentile 69 cm. Cords up to 300 cm are recorded.
- Cord grows from 32 cm at 20 weeks to 60 cm at term; about 0.5 cm in early gestation, 16–18 cm by the fourth lunar month, 33–35 cm by the sixth month.
- Males have longer cords than females (58.46 versus 56.90 cm in vertex births; 53.78 versus 52.51 cm in breech). Cords are 4.5 cm shorter in breech than vertex births and 7.9 cm shorter in twins.
- Cord diameter ranges 1.25–2.00 cm and circumference 2.4–4.4 cm.
- Nuchal cord: 23% of deliveries in one series, 20.4% in another. One coil 20.6%, two coils 2.5%, three coils 0.5%; another series 19% one encirclement, 5.3% two, 1.2% three, 0.2% four. Body entwinement 2%, knots 1%.
- Cord prolapse occurs in 0.41% of 75,000 deliveries, with 13.4% perinatal mortality.
- Velamentous insertion is about 1% of singletons and 8.1% in twins; one series found 15.3% velamentous and 14.7% marginal cords. Central insertion is present in nearly 7% of term placentas in one report and is described as near-central in about 1%.
- SUA: about 1% of newborns; 0.63% in a large compilation; 40,159 of 56,919 infants in another; twins 8.8%; 0.45% among 4138 consecutive deliveries; 0.95% in another series. Associated anomalies in 44.7% of infants, renal anomalies 18.5%, growth restriction 8%. A muscular remnant of a vanished second artery is found in 4% of term cords.
- True knots occur in 0.4–0.5% (0.35% in another series), with fetal mortality around 10%.
- Cord rupture occurs most often (22.5%) at the placental attachment, and hematoma carries about 50% fetal mortality; a reported hematoma measured 6 × 9 × 3 cm and contained about 100 mL.
- Placental transfusion: fetal blood remaining in the placenta is about 2.5% of fetal body weight at birth, or about 9.4 mL per 100 g of placenta; the fetal/placental distribution shifts from 67%/33% at birth to 87%/13% by 3 minutes.

## Anti-patterns
- **Listing false knots as knots.** They are vascular redundancies and should not be enumerated as true knots.
- **Calling all cord strictures artefact.** Stricture, torsion, and coarctation are real; they cluster with excessively long, heavily spiralled cords, macerated fetuses, and placental surface venous thrombi. The counter-argument that strictures are merely diminishing Wharton's jelly is noted but not accepted.
- **Attributing abnormal coiling to mechanical factors alone.** Other mechanisms must be considered.
- **Diagnosing SUA from one segment.** A single artery may be apparent at one end only; examine both ends and look for the muscular remnant.
- **Treating cord edema as disease.** Edematous cords are common in normal newborns and in 10–28% of babies in some reports.
- **Ignoring insertion site in twins.** Velamentous and marginal insertion are far more frequent in twins and carry disproportionate risk.

## Key Takeaways
1. Measure and report cord length, diameter, insertion site, vessel number, coiling index, and direction as routine parameters.
2. Treat absent coiling and a coiling index below the tenth percentile as risk markers and correlate with fetal surveillance.
3. Use the 35 cm and 80 cm boundaries for short and long cords, and state the measurement method.
4. Confirm SUA at both ends of the cord and search for the regressed artery.
5. Report velamentous insertion explicitly because of the risk of membranous vessel injury, thrombosis, and fetal hemorrhage.
6. Distinguish Hyrtl anastomosis, false knots, Hoboken nodes, and yolk sac remnants from true pathology.
