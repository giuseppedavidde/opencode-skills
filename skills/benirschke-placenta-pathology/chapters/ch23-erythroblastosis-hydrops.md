# Chapter 23: Erythroblastosis Fetalis, Hydrops Fetalis, and Transplacental Hemorrhage

## Core Idea
Hydrops fetalis is abnormal fluid accumulation in two or more fetal compartments. It results from imbalance of extracellular fluid, caused by raised intravascular hydrostatic pressure, low oncotic pressure, leaky vessels, or failure of fluid return. Potter divided hydrops into immune and nonimmune types by the presence of erythroblastosis. Immune hydrops (hemolytic disease of the fetus and newborn, HDFN) is now rare where prophylaxis exists, so nonimmune causes dominate. Placental change follows fetal anemia severity, not maternal anemia.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Hydrops definition (ultrasound):** two or more fluid collections — pleural effusion, pericardial effusion, ascites, skin thickening. Placental thickening and polyhydramnios are frequent but not required.
- **Potter classification:** immune (erythroblastosis present) versus nonimmune. Modern series reassign cases across categories, so frequency tables are approximate.
- **Table 23.1 frequency tiers:** cardiovascular, chromosomal, and anemia are high (10% or more). Infection, tumors, malformations, twin-twin transfusion, and lymphatic anomalies are medium (2–9%). Skeletal dysplasia, nephrotic syndrome, and inborn errors are low (less than 2%). Unknown remains high.
- **Anemia mechanism triad:** red cell destruction (immune hemolysis), red cell loss (fetal-maternal hemorrhage, FMH), poor production (thalassemia, Diamond-Blackfan anemia, congenital dyserythropoietic anemia).

## Key Concepts
- Fetal hematocrit persistently below 15% makes hydrops likely. Nicolaides gave normal fetal hemoglobin 11–15 g/dL; hydropic fetuses had 7–10 g/dL. Hemoglobin below 6 g/dL produces hydrops after 4–6 days.
- **Erythroblastosis fetalis** means a hydropic fetus with severe anemia and abundant circulating red cell precursors. Its four gross placental features are pallor, uniform enlargement, marrow elements in the fetal circulation, and villous "immaturity".
- Histology of severe HDFN: enlarged villi, especially intermediate villi; reduced fetal vessel number; increased nucleated red cells inside vessels; persistent Langhans layer (not specific); small amounts of hemosiderin in chorionic macrophages (not prominent, because fetal iron affinity is high); rare yellow staining of surface vessels and cord.
- Pilz morphometry (8 EF placentas vs 20 normal): intermediate villi enlarge in proportion to disease severity; cytotrophoblast and Hofbauer cells increase; terminal villi are unaltered; no chorangiosis. Placental calcification is uncommon in EF.
- Intervillous thrombi occur in about one third of HDFN placentas (Busch and Vogel) and often contain nucleated red cells. They are also frequent in hydatidiform moles that have no fetal vessels, so villous edema with local eddying and stasis is the proposed shared mechanism. Fetal bleeding follows villous surface necrosis and capillary distension.
- After intrauterine transfusion, villous size is normal when hemoglobin exceeds 11 g/dL; villous volume rises in more severely affected infants.
- **Massive FMH:** gross pallor plus increased nucleated red cells; placentomegaly is rarely described. The index case showed scattered small irregular bright red foci in pale parenchyma. Histology shows small syncytiotrophoblast breaks with nucleated red cells in the adjacent intervillous space ("Kline's hemorrhages"), nearly empty fetal vessels, and scant perivillous fibrin. No intervillous thrombi were seen.
- Only 7.5% of large-volume bleeds (more than 50 mL fetal blood) present with hydrops (Giacoia). Kleihauer film: fetal cells stain dark; illustrated example about 5%.
- Association of intervillous thrombi with FMH is inconsistent: Ravishankar confirmed pallor, intervillous thrombi, and increased nucleated red cells; Lewis, Marszalek, and Ernst did not find the thrombus association.
- Hemoglobin Bart's hydrops: placenta larger than in HDFN (1900 g illustrated; weights to 3500 g), pale and friable; histology cannot separate it from HDFN. Fetal death usually between 23 and 38 weeks.
- Turner syndrome placenta is not truly hydropic; it is smaller and may show Breus mole features.
- Parvovirus: hydrops 4–6 weeks after maternal exposure; second trimester carries the greatest risk; virus infects erythroid precursors, causing erythroid hypoplasia and increased red cell fragility. Inclusions may be sparse in placenta compared with fetal liver.

## Anti-patterns
- Do not diagnose HDFN from placental histology alone. The changes overlap with alpha-thalassemia, parvovirus, and other nonimmune hydrops; ancillary testing is required.
- Do not equate "friable" with true tissue crumbling; villous enlargement only makes individual villi look more distinct.
- Do not read a small placenta as hydropic. Small or normal size points to chromosomal abnormality (especially 45,X) or a primary lymphatic anomaly.
- Do not count every red placental focus as an infarct or intervillous thrombus. In massive FMH the lesions are syncytial breaks with fetal red cells (Kline's hemorrhages), and intervillous thrombi may be absent.
- Do not expect hemosiderin to be conspicuous in HDFN; fetal iron avidity limits placental deposition.

## Key Takeaways
1. Confirm hydrops by two or more fluid collections, then classify as immune or nonimmune and check fetal hematocrit.
2. In suspected HDFN, weigh pallor, placentomegaly, intermediate villous enlargement, and circulating erythroblasts together; treat the histology as supportive, not diagnostic.
3. Interpret intervillous thrombi cautiously: they are common in HDFN and molar pregnancy, and their link to FMH is not consistent.
4. In sudden fetal anemia or demise, look for syncytial breaks with adjacent fetal red cells and empty fetal vessels; this pattern supports massive transplacental hemorrhage.
5. Correlate placental thickness and MCA-PSV timing: placental thickness is most useful in the first trimester, MCA-PSV in the second.
