# Asset Management: A Systematic Approach to Factor Investing

**Author**: Andrew Ang | **Chapters**: 18 | **Generated**: 2026-07-14

Part of the **Financial Management Association Survey and Synthesis Series** (Oxford University Press, 2014). The book reframes asset management around *factors* rather than asset-class labels: risk premiums are compensation for losses in bad times, diversification is the only free lunch, and alpha is mostly disguised factor exposure. The three parts cover (I) the asset owner's preferences and life-cycle choice, (II) the theory, identification and implementation of factor risk premiums, and (III) delegated portfolio management (mutual funds, hedge funds, private equity).

## Core Frameworks & Mental Models

### 1. Factors, Not Asset Classes
Risk premiums are driven by a small set of **common factors** — equity market, value, size, momentum, low-vol/low-beta, quality/profitability, carry, term, credit. Asset class labels (equities, bonds, PE, hedge funds) are downstream vehicles; the *factor exposures* are the investable primitive. Every investment decision is reducible to: (a) which factors am I exposed to? (b) how much do I under/overweight each? (c) at what cost?

### 2. Bad vs Good Factors
A **bad** factor loses money precisely when the marginal investor is worst off — low consumption growth, recessions, disasters — and therefore commands a positive premium (equity, value, carry, momentum). A **good** factor pays off in those bad states and therefore carries little or negative premium (vol strategies, safe-haven assets). Holding "good" factors can hedge the bad ones but is not a return source by itself. Refuse factors with no economic story — they are likely data-mined.

### 3. Risk Premiums as Bad-Times Compensation
Returns come only from absorbing losses when other investors suffer most. The equity premium is disaster insurance you collect for bearing disaster risk; the value premium is compensation for owning cheap stocks precisely when they are under duress; the credit premium is premium for absorbing default risk in recessions. Identifying your *bad times* relative to the marginal investor is the foundation of every allocation call.

### 4. Diversification is the Only Free Lunch
Markowitz mean-variance optimizers are notorious for noisy, unstable weight estimates. The book's prescription is **robust portfolio construction**: shrink expected return estimates, use risk-budgeting rather than return-forecasts, and lean on diversification (across factors, vintages, vehicles, geographies) as the irreducible source of risk-adjusted improvement. Rebalancing to constant weights *is* a free lunch — the only systematic way to extract the rebalancing premium ≈ ½σ² × (1−ρ) × weights × frequency.

### 5. Long Run ≠ Time Diversification (Samuelson's Fallacy)
Horizon alone does not make risky assets safer — terminal wealth variance scales with horizon under i.i.d. returns. The genuine long-run edge is **rebalancing to constant weights** (countercyclical buying when assets fall) plus **volatility timing** (forecast σ, not μ — vol is far more predictable than returns). Use the Gordon formula (r ≈ D/P + g) and Shiller-PE as valuation sanity checks, not as primary timing alpha.

### 6. Factor-Based Alpha Attribution (Alpha = Residual After Factors)
Reported "alpha" shrinks dramatically when regressed against Fama-French 3-factor, Carhart 4-factor, Fama-French 5-factor, or Betting-Against-Beta (BAB). The prescription: only declare alpha when the regression intercept remains ≥2 t-stat over ≥5y non-overlapping out-of-sample windows. **Buffett's alpha shrinks ~1%/yr after FF+momentum**; most active-manager alpha survives only as factor exposure in disguise.

### 7. Low-Volatility / Low-Beta / BAB Anomaly
Leverage constraints (40-Act funds, pension risk limits, mandate restrictions) force constrained investors into high-beta names — compressing their returns and inflating low-beta returns. Long low-beta / short high-beta (BAB) is one of the largest persistent equity-style premiums. Construction: min-variance portfolio, low-volatility indices, or leverage-targeted low-beta longs. Capacity is real — premiums always shrink post-publication.

### 8. Tax-Efficient Investing as Structural Alpha
Taxes are a persistent factor — they shape asset prices (dividend-tax penalty, lock-in, capitalization effects add ~2.5% tax-yield premium). After-tax compounding dominates pre-tax comparisons in taxable accounts. Three levers: (i) **asset location** (taxable bonds and REITs to tax-deferred, equities to taxable, munis only to taxable), (ii) **tax-loss harvesting** with non-identical substitutes (Constantinides tax-timing option), (iii) **long-term-holding discipline** + deferral of gains until stepped-up basis at death.

### 9. Reference Portfolio + Risk Budgeting
The CPP / Canada Pension Plan model: start with a passive two-factor (equity / bond) reference portfolio; fund every active bet as a deviation from it; risk-budget the deviations. Manager selection becomes a factor regression test on alpha above reference. Avoid factory-style strategic asset allocation in favor of explicit macro-and-style factor decomposition.

### 10. Implementation Cost & Capacity Discipline
Every Sharpe-shiny factor decays post-publication: competition, crowding, market impact. Ang's factor-recipe checklist: (1) academic basis, (2) persistence >50y out-of-sample, (3) bad-times track record, (4) implementable in liquid vehicles with low cost. Reject fashionable factors that fail two or more criteria.

### 11. Delegated Investing Reality
Active management requires investor heterogeneity in beliefs, risk tolerance, and time horizons. Aggregate alpha net of fees is **zero** (Berk-Green equilibrium): managers' talent accrues to firm equity, not LP returns, because flows chase past performance and capacity constrains future skill. Implication: prefer high-Active-Share (>80%), low-fee (<0.5%) truly-active funds with verified post-factor-regression alpha; else use passive ETFs (in-kind creation/redemption is tax-efficient).

### 12. Illiquid & Private Asset Prudence
Private equity returns after public market equivalent (PME) and factor adjustments show alpha ≈ 0 — buyouts decompose to leveraged small-value beta; illiquidity premium is real but modest. Fees (~2/20 with two-thirds fixed) require ≥6-7% gross alpha to break even vs passive public equivalent. Vintage diversification across ≥5-7 vintages is mandatory to smooth J-curves and avoid peak-vintage traps.

## How to Use This Skill

1. **Macros first**: when asked about asset allocation, start with macro-factor exposures (growth, inflation, real rate) from Ch 11/14, then layer style-factor tilts.
2. **Cost-aware**: every factor recommendation must clear the capacity/liquidity check (Ch 14, 16).
3. **After-tax**: never quote pre-tax return in taxable client contexts (Ch 12).
4. **Attribution before alpha**: any "skill" claim must survive Fama-French + Carhart + BAB regression (Ch 10, 15, 16).
5. **Bad-times lens**: identify an asset's "bad times" before quoting its premium — a premium that does not dent in bad times is suspicious (Ch 2, 6, 8).
