# Chapter 18: Private Equity

## Core Idea
Private equity is not an asset class—it is public equity risk wrapped in leverage, illiquidity, and opaque contracts. PE returns decompose into well-known factor exposures (small-size, value, leverage, illiquidity) plus a thin slice of manager selection; on a risk-adjusted basis the average PE fund does **not** beat public equities, yet dispersion across funds and vintages is enormous and persistence in top-quartile managers is real but hard to capture in real time.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **IRR vs Multiples (TVPI/MOIC) vs PME** — three performance measures, none of which is a return: IRR is distorted by reinvestment/timing assumptions and routinely manipulated; multiples ignore time value, duration, and leverage; PME (Kaplan & Schoar 2005) discounts cash flows at realized market returns to benchmark PE vs. public equity.
- **J-curve & vintage-year effect** — early funds show negative returns as fees and write-downs hit before exits (J-curve), and return dispersion is dominated by the vintage year; capital committed in a vintage and that vintage's return are strongly negatively correlated.
- **Pro-cyclical commitments ("money chasing deals")** — LPs pour capital in at valuation peaks (when future expected returns are low) and pull back at troughs; fundraising collapsed in 2009–10 exactly when expected returns were highest.
- **GP/LP limited-partnership structure** — GP raises commitments, calls capital on demand over a ~10-year horizon; LPs are locked in (secondary markets carry huge discounts, e.g. Harvard's 50% in the crisis). Committed capital, not invested capital, is the fee base.
- **PE as leveraged small-value beta** — buyout funds buy small, cheap firms with ~3x leverage; once size, value, and leverage factors are controlled, alpha shrinks toward zero (Phalippou & Gottschalg, Driessen-Lin-Phalippou). Illiquidity risk premium (~3%) is the only residual, also collectable in public markets.
- **Alpha persistence (Kaplan-Schoar)** — top-quartile funds repeat, but the result is ex-post; everyone claims top quartile (77% of firms), vintage year and benchmark are manipulable, and usable real-time info at the next fundraise is scarce. Size is strongly negatively correlated with performance.

## Key Concepts
- IRR assumes reinvestment at the IRR; early dividends inflate it while destroying value—GPs game timing and aggregate at vintage level to hide bad funds.
- Break-even thresholds: IRR 13–19%, multiple 3–6x, PME 1.2–2.1 (beta-dependent) before LPs earn excess returns; average buyout fund since ~2006 sits right at break-even.
- Selection bias and infrequent NAV observation inflate reported PE returns; zombies (>5% of investments) are held at cost to milk fees, upward-biasing NAVs.
- Leverage accounts for over 50% of gross PE returns (Acharya et al.); without adjusting for it, benefits are vastly overstated.
- Fees: ~$18 (buyout) / $23 (VC) per $100 committed in PV terms (Metrick-Yasuda); only 30–40% is incentive-based, two-thirds is fixed revenue charged on capital never actually invested.
- Contract mechanics: 2% mgmt fee on committed capital, 20% carry, 8% hurdle, catch-up, clawback, waterfall; plus opaque uncapped portfolio-company transaction (2%) and monitoring (0.4%) fees.
- Funds-of-funds and side letters layer extra fees and preferential terms; most-favored-nation status goes to large/long-standing LPs.
- LP performance puzzle: endowments earned ~39% IRR vs. ~3% for banks, driven by early access and networks; endowment edge has decayed since 1999 as PE institutionalized.
- Value created at portfolio-company level (better governance, professionalism, skin-in-the-game) accrues largely to GPs, not LPs—consistent with Berk-Green.
- Smarter structure (Ang): raise committed capital into a (tilted) public-equity benchmark, pay GPs only incentive fees on excess over that benchmark.

## Anti-patterns
- **Buying yesterday's winners / chasing hot vintages** — committing at fundraising peaks when past distributions are large but forward expected returns are lowest.
- **Vintage concentration** — over-allocating to one or two vintage years amplifies J-curve and pro-cyclical risk; diversification across vintages is essential.
- **Fooled by stale, smoothed NAVs** — treating infrequent, GP-discretionary, upward-biased NAVs as mark-to-market returns; zombies mask true losses.
- **Attributing leverage to skill** — celebrating a 3x multiple on a 5x-levered buyout as alpha when it is mostly small-value beta plus debt.
- **Trusting industry self-research** — PE-funded studies use selection-biased data, non-return metrics, and inflated NAVs; "like believing cigarette companies on smoking."
- **Sub-coinflip persistence bets** — selecting top-quartile funds on ex-post data without usable real-time information; 77% of firms self-claim top quartile.

## Key Takeaways
1. **PE is factor exposure, not an asset class** — replicate its size/value/leverage/illiquidity premia cheaply in public markets; the residual alpha is ~0 after risk adjustment.
2. **Use PME, never IRR or multiples alone** — always benchmark cash flows against the public-market equivalent and demand break-even PMEs of 1.2–2.1 before calling it outperformance.
3. **Diversify across vintages and invest counter-cyclically** — commit steadily (or tilt toward trough years) to avoid the money-chasing-deals trap where most capital enters at peak valuations.
4. **Audit fees ruthlessly** — push fixed fees off committed capital onto a passive public benchmark; reserve two-thirds of compensation for genuine incentive carry.
5. **Capture persistence only with access and scale** — top-quartile repeat winners are real but closed to new money; large, well-governed owners (Canada PP, OTPP, sovereign funds) keep fees by going direct and outperform small LPs.
6. **Demand transparency** — independent NAVs, fee disclosure net of all portfolio-company fees, and consistent benchmarking neutralize the zombie, selection, and manipulation biases that dupe the average LP.
