# Chapter 1: Asset Owners

## Core Idea
All asset owners—from the largest sovereign wealth funds (SWFs) to the smallest individual investors—face the same three problems: (1) defining and meeting liabilities, (2) deciding where to invest and how much risk to take, and (3) overseeing the delegated intermediaries who actually manage the portfolios. The scale changes by orders of magnitude, but the canonical issues (liabilities, risk, governance) are universal. Understanding who owns the assets is the necessary preface to factor investing: optimal portfolios are shaped by the owner's liabilities and bad times, not by abstract market weights.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Asset-owner taxonomy** — SWFs, pension funds (public/private, DB/DC), endowments & foundations, individuals & families; intermediaries (banks, insurers, asset managers) are excluded as they serve these owners.
- **Liability-driven framing** — every owner is defined first by its liabilities (future claims to be paid), then by surplus = assets − liabilities; the goal is to maximize surplus subject to tolerable surplus volatility.
- **Optimal sovereign wealth (Norway / Timor-Leste Petroleum Fund)** — diversify a windfall away from a single commodity, immunize against Dutch disease, smooth consumption across generations, quarantine wealth from short-term political capture.
- **Dutch disease** — resource windfalls swell the local currency and hollow out manufacturing; SWFs combat it by investing abroad and releasing money only gradually.
- **Integration into government policy** — a SWF succeeds only if coordinated with fiscal rules, budget process, and monetary/central-bank reserves; otherwise it amplifies, rather than dampens, procyclicality.
- **Pension plan types** — (1) national/Social Security, (2) private defined benefit (DB), (3) private defined contribution (DC), (4) individually managed accounts (e.g., 401(k)s, IRAs); ERISA governs private U.S. plans.
- **DB vs DC** — DB: sponsor bears investment and longevity risk, pre-defined benefit. DC: employee bears risk, pre-defined contribution; shift from DB to DC transfers risk from sponsor to individual.
- **Pension underfunding** — Novy-Marx & Rauh estimate ~$3.7 trillion public pension hole; sources include insufficient contributions, benefit rises, non-optimal design, longer life, and low returns.
- **Endowment model / Swensen** — long-horizon owner with stable liabilities tilts heavily into illiquid alternatives (PE, real estate, hedge funds) to harvest illiquidity and diversification premia.
- **Restricted vs unrestricted funds** — foundations spend the minimum (5% IRS payout), with spending smoothing; restricted endowments protect corpus, unrestricted allow opportunistic deployment.
- **Life-cycle household portfolio** — labor income is the dominant "asset" for the young; family dynamics (fights, nepotism, home bias, under-diversification, overspending) and idiosyncratic risks (labor income, leverage, health) shape individual portfolios.

## Key Concepts
- **Liabilities define bad times** — for a SWF, bad times are low oil prices; for a pension, low growth + low rates + longevity; for an endowment, spending shortfalls; for an individual, job loss + health shocks.
- **Surplus** — assets minus liabilities; the right optimization objective when liabilities are well-defined.
- **Funding ratio** — assets/liabilities; Rhode Island ERSRI was 59% funded; U.S. public plans collectively far short of full funding.
- **Generational equity** — Norway's fund exists to share oil wealth with future citizens, not the current electorate.
- **Time horizon and risk capacity** — long-horizon owners (SWFs, endowments, DB plans) can bear illiquidity and harvest factor premia that individuals and short-horizon DC savers cannot.
- **Agency issues (owner level)** — SWF misuse (politicians directing capital), pension governance scandals (NYSCRF sole-trustee abuses), family nepotism and spending — all are principal-agent problems *before* delegation to managers even begins.
- **Individual idiosyncratic risks** — labor income (bond-like vs stock-like), leverage (mortgage), health care; these factor risks dwarf portfolio holdings for most households.

## Anti-patterns
- **Treating market-cap weights as self-evident for sovereigns** — sovereign bond benchmarks by market cap ignore credit, reserve, and macro factors and can be dominated by distressed issuers.
- **Spending the windfall** — Timor-Leste could have spent oil money immediately on necessities; instead it built a fund to avoid Dutch disease and intergenerational theft.
- **Procyclical political capture** — SWFs that double as stimulus vehicles buy domestic assets precisely when commodity revenues collapse, concentrating risk in the worst times.
- **Sole-trustee concentration** — NYSCRF put one elected comptroller in sole control, inviting pay-to-play scandals (Regan, McCall, Hevesi convictions).
- **Family under-diversification and inertia** — holding employer stock, nepotism, and slouching (failing to prune and rebalance) destroy household wealth.
- **Promising DB benefits without funding them** — raising benefits in good times without contributions stacked underfunding and crowded out services (Rhode Island's 3¢→20¢ per tax dollar).

## Key Takeaways
1. **Start from liabilities.** Define what you owe (future generations, retirees, spending mandates, personal consumption); surplus, not asset level, is the optimization object.
2. **Match asset risk to liability risk** — an owner whose bad times coincide with the average investor's should hold the market; an owner with idiosyncratic bad times (oil wealth, public-sector payroll, family health) tilts away from those risks.
3. **Long horizon ≠ risk-free; it enables illiquidity and factor premia.** Endowments and SWFs can bear illiquidity; individuals and DC plans generally cannot.
4. **Governance is a factor risk at the owner level** — concentration of control (sole trustees, single-family heads) is a leading indicator of abuse; build boards and process *before* delegating to managers.
5. **Individuals must treat labor income as an asset** — bond-like labor income justifies more equity early; risky/stock-like labor income justifies defensive holdings and avoidance of employer stock.
6. **The line between owner and intermediary blurs** — SWFs and pension funds act as owners but manage as institutions; the asset owner chapter sets the stage for the delegation, factor, and implementation chapters that follow.
