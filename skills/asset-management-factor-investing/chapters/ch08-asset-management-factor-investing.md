# Chapter 8: Equities

## Core Idea
Equities earn a historically high risk premium over bonds and cash, but the size of that premium cannot be reconciled with simple consumption-based models — this is the "equity premium puzzle." The chapter explains the premium through four lenses (high market risk aversion, rare disasters, long-run risks, heterogeneous investors), shows equities are a surprisingly poor inflation hedge, and examines the weak, hard-to-detect predictability of the equity premium alongside the much more forecastable nature of equity volatility.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Equity Premium Puzzle (Mehra–Prescott, 1985)** — Under CRRA utility and reasonable risk aversion (1–10), consumption-based models imply an equity premium <1%, vs. the ~6% historically observed; spawns the search for explanations.
- **Lucas (1978) Consumption-Based Asset Pricing** — Prices all assets through exposure to a single consumption factor; the equity premium is a reward for losses during low-consumption-growth "bad times."
- **Rare Disasters / Disaster Risk (Rietz 1988; Barro 2006)** — Low-probability catastrophic crashes (depressions, wars) rationally produce a high equity premium because equities collapse exactly in those disaster states.
- **Long-Run Risks (Bansal–Yaron)** — Persistent consumption growth and stochastic volatility over long horizons carry their own risk premium; value stocks and carry trades load highly on long-run risk betas.
- **Heterogeneous Agents / Market Incompleteness (Constantinides–Duffie 1996)** — Cross-sectional dispersion in idiosyncratic labor income shocks (rising in recessions) makes equities unattractive to unhedgeable agents, endogenously raising the aggregate premium.
- **Equity Premium Predictability** — Valuation ratios (dividend yield, dividend-price, earnings-price) and low risk-free rates forecast long-horizon excess returns, but with weak, statistically fragile, sample-dependent signals.
- **Volatility Timing (VIX-based)** — Time-varying equity weight inversely proportional to VIX beats static 60/40; equity volatility is far more forecastable than the equity premium itself, but requires nimbleness most large investors lack.
- **Excess Volatility** — Equity volatility (~17%) dwarfs consumption-growth volatility (~2%) and dividend/fundamental volatility; equities are "excessively volatile" relative to fundamentals.

## Key Concepts
- **Bad times definition** — low consumption growth, disasters, long-run risks, high inflation; the equity premium is precisely compensation for losses in these states.
- **Sharpe ratio bound** — equity Sharpe of ~0.3 combined with low consumption volatility and <15% equity-consumption correlation makes the puzzle severe.
- **Representative vs. marginal agent** — in heterogeneous models the representative agent is really the *marginal* investor; small-wealth agents with binding constraints can set prices at the margin.
- **Market incompleteness & borrowing constraints** — unhedgeable labor-income risk and leverage limits raise aggregate risk aversion during downturns, forcing fire-sale liquidations.
- **Inflation beta** — a perfect inflation hedge has beta = 1; developed-market equity betas average −0.25 (US −0.42), while T-bills reach ~+0.6; only emerging-market equities approach +1.
- **Hedging terms** (eq. 8.11) — the simple risk-return relation omits liability and long-horizon hedging demands that dominate the representative agent's portfolio choice.
- **Risk as flipside of expected returns** — high volatility coincides with low prices and high forward risk premiums, making volatility-timing and valuation-based timing two sides of one coin.
- **Countercyclical rebalancing** — constant-weight rebalancing is inherently countercyclical and captures mean-reverting premiums more robustly than noisy timing signals.

## Anti-patterns
- **Extrapolating the historical premium naively** — the 2000s Lost Decade shows a $1 stock investment grew only to $1.05 while T-bills reached $1.31; treating ~6–7% as guaranteed is dangerous.
- **Treating equities as an inflation hedge** — equities are a *real* claim in theory but have negative inflation betas in developed markets; do not rely on stocks to protect purchasing power.
- **Over-relying on predictability signals** — dividend-yield predictability is statistically weak, regime-dependent, and dwarfed by hedging terms; naively trading it invites data mining.
- **Assuming the representative agent = average investor** — prices are set by the marginal, often constrained, agent; ignoring heterogeneity misreads when equities are cheap for *you*.
- **Ignoring implementation cost of volatility timing** — weights swing from >1.5 to near zero; large investors cannot move that fast without transaction/market-impact costs eroding the edge.

## Key Takeaways
1. **Equities earn a high long-run premium because they lose money in bad times** — hold them to be compensated for disaster, long-run, and inflation risks, not because past returns are high.
2. **Detect whether your bad times align with the marginal investor's** — if you can absorb losses when constrained/fire-sale agents must sell, hold an above-average equity allocation.
3. **Do not use equities as an inflation hedge** — pair them with TIPS, short rates, or emerging-market equities (beta ≈ 1) when inflation risk is the concern.
4. **Rebalance to constant weights rather than time the premium** — countercyclical rebalancing is robust where predictability signals are statistically fragile.
5. **Forecast volatility, not expected returns** — volatility is far more forecastable (GARCH, realized vol, VIX); embed it in risk management and position sizing.
6. **Link volatility to valuation** — high VIX + low prices = high forward risk premiums and low valuation risk; next-gen risk management should fuse vol models with discount-rate/valuation metrics.
