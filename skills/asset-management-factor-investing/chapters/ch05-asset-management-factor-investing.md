# Chapter 5: Investing Over the Life Cycle

## Core Idea
Labor income is an asset, and for young investors the present value of labor income utterly dominates the rest of their financial holdings. The optimal asset mix therefore evolves as labor income evolves over the life cycle. Investors whose labor income is *bond-like* (safe, stable) should hold more equities when young and de-risk toward bonds as retirement approaches; investors with *stock-like* (volatile, equity-correlated) labor income should hold fewer equities and avoid their employer's stock. While economic theory says annuities are ideal for retirees, few actually hold them.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Labor income as a non-tradable asset** — human capital (PV of future wages) is the largest "position" for most households; asset allocation must include it alongside financial wealth.
- **Asset allocation with no labor income** — pure mean-variance over financial wealth only (the retired case).
- **Asset allocation with risk-free (bond-like) labor income** — safe human capital is a large implicit bond holding; optimal financial portfolio tilts heavily to equity while young.
- **Asset allocation with risky (stock-like) labor income** — volatile or equity-correlated wages are an implicit equity holding; reduce financial equity, avoid employer stock, and consider fixed income or non-cyclical assets.
- **Life-cycle models (Merton-style dynamic programming)** — solve jointly for consumption and portfolio choice over the life cycle; manage the total wealth (financial + human) stock.
- **Life-cycle asset allocation glide path** — high equity weight early (when human capital dominates and bond-like), declining as retirement nears (strips out the now-spent human-capital bond); this is the target-date fund logic.
- **Equities are NOT less risky over the long run** — time diversification fallacy: longer horizon raises cumulative variance; the case for equities when young rests on human capital, not on lower long-run equity risk.
- **Retirement adequacy** — replacement rates (retirement income / final salary), savings adequacy, the 4% rule (Bengen), and annuitization.
- **Annuities theory vs practice** — theory says full annuitization maximizes welfare (insures longevity risk), but few households annuitize (the "annuity puzzle"), driven by bequest motives, illiquidity, distrust, and adverse selection.
- **Consumption puzzles over the life cycle** — consumption is too smooth (retirees don't cut spending), excessively sensitive to income, hump-shaped; behavioral models reconcile.

## Key Concepts
- **Human capital** — PV of future labor income; bond-like (tenured professor, judge) or stock-like (banker, entrepreneur, commissioned sales).
- **Total wealth** = financial wealth + human capital; allocate over total, not just the tradeable part.
- **Glide path** — the equity weight trajectory of target-date funds; steepens with bond-like income, flattens with stock-like income.
- **Employer stock** — doubly concentrated risk (labor + capital in the same firm); almost always over-weighted.
- **Background risks** — labor income risk, health risk, mortality risk, and mortality risk shape the risky-asset share beyond mean-variance.
- **Hump-shaped consumption & excess sensitivity** — retirees do not smooth consumption perfectly; theory and behavior diverge.
- **Replacement rate** — target 70–80% of pre-retirement income; 4% rule roughly finances this on a 60/40 portfolio over 30 years.
- **Dis-saving in retirement** — many retirees *under*-spend their assets (bequest, precaution), contradicting the pure life-cycle model.

## Anti-patterns
- **Holding age as the driver of equity weight** — age is a proxy for *remaining* human capital; someone with risky/stock-like labor income should be more conservative regardless of age.
- **Stock-like labor income + heavy equity** — a banker or entrepreneur with equity-correlated wages doubles down on the same bad times; reduces total-wealth diversification.
- **Owning employer stock** — ties financial and labor fortunes together (Enron); idiosyncratic risk not compensated by any premium.
- **Believing long horizon makes equities safe** — "time diversification" doesn't reduce cumulative risk; the young-equity case rests on human capital, not horizon.
- **Retiring into all-TIPS without equity** — longevity and inflation risk over 30+ years require some growth assets; pure TIPS run out.
- **Rejecting annuities entirely for spurious reasons** — full annuitization is welfare-improving; the annuity puzzle is a real inefficiency, not wisdom.
- **Under-saving and over-spending** — Rhode Island/many households raise benefits or living standards without funding them; the 4% rule is binding, not a suggestion.

## Key Takeaways
1. **Include human capital in the balance sheet** — total wealth = financial + human capital; the human component is the largest for the young and shapes the optimal financial portfolio.
2. **Match financial risk to the *type* of labor income** — bond-like wages support aggressive equity early; stock-like wages demand defensive financial holdings and avoidance of employer stock.
3. **Glide down equity as bond-like human capital is consumed** — the target-date logic is sound *because* it mirrors the spent human-capital bond, not because equities get safer with time.
4. **Do not over-rely on the long-run safety of equities** — the long-run argument is a fallacy; size equity to your human capital, not to vague horizon claims.
5. **Plan retirement adequacy explicitly** — set a replacement-rate target (~70–80%), apply a conservative withdrawal rule (~4%), and consider partial annuitization to hedge longevity risk.
6. **Own the gap between theory and behavior** — consumers fail to annuitize and fail to dis-save optimally; design defaults (target-date funds, annuity options in DC plans) that nudge toward theory without forcing alien products.
