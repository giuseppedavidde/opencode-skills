# Chapter 4: Investing for the Long Run

## Core Idea
Long-run investing is solved by dynamic programming as a series of one-period (myopic) problems. Under i.i.d. returns—the most empirically relevant case—the long-run optimal portfolio equals the short-run optimal portfolio, and the only mandatory long-run action is **rebalancing** back to fixed weights, which is counter-cyclical, buys low/sells high, and earns a rebalancing premium over time.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Merton–Samuelson dynamic portfolio choice** (1969–1971): long-horizon utility maximization solved as nested single-period problems; the long horizon enters through "indirect utility" (the value function), not by acting differently from short-run investors.
- **Wealth homogeneity property**: optimal weights are independent of initial wealth level—starting with $1 or $1M gives identical allocations.
- **i.i.d. equivalence (Long-Run Weight = Short-Run Weight)**: when returns are not predictable, horizon is irrelevant; all investors act myopically.
- **Rebalancing as the foundational long-run strategy**: counter-cyclical trading back to fixed weights; long-run investors are at heart value investors.
- **Transitivity principle**: with i.i.d. returns, rebalanced fixed-weight and buy-and-hold strategies are linked to short-run mean-variance optima via monotonic transformations.
- **Rebalancing premium / diversification return**: extra geometric return from rebalancing, driven by Jensen's inequality (≈ ½σ²), also described as selling volatility / shorting a regime-change option.
- **Kelly rule (optimal growth portfolio)**: log-utility portfolio that maximizes long-run wealth; Samuelson (1979) argues against it for non-log-utility investors—follow Merton–Samuelson, not Kelly.
- **Three-bucket life-cycle framing (Chhabra)**: maps Merton–Samuelson portfolios into personal (safe), market, and aspirational buckets.

## Key Concepts
- **Dynamic programming** is the same optimal-control technique used for rocket navigation and derivative valuation; portfolio choice is literally "rocket science."
- **Myopic weight formula**: x* = (μ − rf) / (γσ²); the one-period mean-variance solution nests equal-weight, risk parity, and market-weight as special cases.
- **Counter-cyclicality of rebalancing**: sell assets that have run up (above target), buy assets that have declined (below target)—mechanically value-tilted.
- **Empirical evidence (1926–1940, 1990–2011)**: quarterly rebalanced 60/40 beat both pure stock and pure bond portfolios and outperformed buy-and-hold through the Great Depression and the 2008 crisis.
- **Rebalancing premium size**: ~1% for U.S. equities (½ × 0.2²); ~3.5% for commodities (Erb & Harvey); Swensen calls it the "rebalancing bonus" (constant-risk-profile maintenance).
- **Short-volatility nature of rebalancing**: equivalent to being short an option on regime change; loses badly if assets permanently explode to zero (irreversible capital destruction) — hence rebalance over *broad* asset classes/strategies, not single stocks/countries.
- **Variance drain**: geometric return ≈ arithmetic return − ½σ²; compounding erodes wealth, and the rebalancing premium is the recovered fraction.
- **Predictability edge**: even when returns exhibit some mean reversion, the amount is very small—rebalancing remains the foundation; a tiny opportunistic portfolio can capture the incremental predictability.
- **Ulysses contract**: the Investment Policy Statement (IPS) acts as a pre-commitment device against panic pro-cyclical exits.
- **Experience-driven risk-aversion shifts** (Malmendier & Nagel 2011): searing losses change *expectations* of future returns more than the utility function itself; if true risk tolerance has changed, redesign the IPS deliberately—otherwise, stay the course.

## Anti-patterns
- **Buy-and-hold drift**: letting winners dominate the portfolio after a bull run concentrates risk exactly when expected returns are lowest.
- **Pro-cyclical panic changes**: ad-hoc de-risking after large losses locks in losses and abandons the rebalancing premium; worst decisions come from pure panic.
- **Short-termism parading as long-run investing**: pretending the long horizon lets you skip disciplined one-period mean-variance risk management—you must first be a successful short-run investor.
- **Kelly-rule maximalism**: chasing long-run wealth maximization (log utility) without acknowledging you can't tolerate the drawdowns or the very long holding period required.
- **Rebalancing narrow winners**: rebalancing across individual stocks or single countries that can permanently disappear destroys wealth—rebattle over broad diversified asset classes only.

## Key Takeaways
1. **Solve the long-run as a series of short-run problems.** First nail the one-period mean-variance optimal portfolio; only then exploit horizon advantages via the value function (indirect utility).
2. **Rebalance to fixed weights as the default long-run strategy**—it is counter-cyclical, value-tilted by construction, and dominant under the realistic i.i.d. assumption.
3. **Capture the rebalancing premium** (≈ ½σ² per asset) by rebalancing across *broad, persistent* asset classes/strategies, never across single securities that can go to zero.
4. **Don't chase the Kelly rule.** Optimize for your own risk aversion and utility; long-run wealth maximization is not the same as risk-adjusted optimality.
5. **Use the IPS as a pre-commitment device** to override counter-cyclical behavioral instincts; only revise it deliberately if risk tolerance has genuinely changed—not after panic losses.
6. **Stay the course through crises.** Rebalancing beat buy-and-hold through the Great Depression and 2008; the foundation is to maintain constant risk exposures, not market-time exits.
