# Chapter 16: Mutual Funds and Other 40-Act Funds
## Core Idea
The Investment Company Act of 1940 ("40-Act") is a regulatory wrapper that lets registered investment companies raise money directly from the public while mitigating (but not eliminating) principal-agent conflicts via disclosure, oversight, leverage limits, and fee rules. Despite these protections, the average active mutual fund underperforms the market after fees; any manager skill accrues to the fund firm, not the investor. Fees, scale diseconomies, and return-chasing flows drive persistent underperformance.
## Frameworks Introduced
- **40-Act registered investment companies** — four structures (mutual funds, closed-end funds, UITs, ETFs) that solicit public money under SEC disclosure/oversight rules.
- **NAV pricing** — 40-Act requires transparent net asset value so investors transact at underlying value (less fees); contrast with ETN opacity.
- **MF / ETF / CEF / UIT structures** — open-end MFs price once daily, CEFs trade at premiums/discounts, ETFs use in-kind creation/redemption, UITs are fixed unmanaged portfolios.
- **Share classes (A/B/C, retail vs institutional)** — same fund sold with different load + 12b-1 + expense schedules; institutional share classes outperform retail by ~1.5%/yr via lower fees.
- **Fulcrum performance fees** — 40-Act forces symmetric, benchmark-centered incentive fees; bans the option-like asymmetric contracts that hedge funds use.
- **Active vs passive expense-ratio math** — fees compound: gross alpha ~0 then subtract expense ratio, 12b-1, trading costs, soft dollars → net underperformance.
- **Fund flows vs performance** — investors chase past returns (buy high, sell low); flows are sticky due to trust, ignorance, disposition effect, and capital-gains lock-in.
- **Survivorship & incubation bias** — dead/merged funds vanish from track records; incubated funds are cherry-picked and juiced with IPO allocations, overstating live-fund returns by 1-4%/yr.
- **Closet indexing / active share** — diseconomies of scale force large funds into index-like hugging; hierarchy costs and illiquid-stock constraints erode alpha.
- **Leveraged & inverse ETF decomposition** — daily-reset compounding causes levered ETF returns to diverge sharply from naively expected multiples (arithmetic vs geometric).
## Key Concepts
- Average active fund: gross alpha ≈ 0 to +0.8% pre-fee, net alpha ≈ -1% to -1.3% post-fee (Jensen, Carhart, Fama-French, Wermers).
- Survivorship bias inflates reported fund returns by ~1-2% vs full universe, ~4%_live-minus-dead gap.
- Incubation bias: incubated funds show ~10% higher returns pre-launch, then outperformance vanishes post-public.
- Larger funds do worse (diseconomies of scale, hierarchy costs, illiquid-name constraints).
- More expensive funds do worse — inverse of "you get what you pay for"; Carhart, Gruber confirm fee inversely predicts return.
- Return chasing: high past-return funds attract inflows that fail to repeat;French estimates ~0.7%/yr gain from switching active→index.
- Trading costs (~1.4%, Edelen-Evans-Kadlec) and soft-dollar arrangements are hidden, beyond stated expense ratios.
- Trust/stickiness: Gennaioli-Shleifer-Vishny — investors trust advisors like doctors; capital stays even after underperformance.
- Trustee-sponsored 401(k) funds underperform non-trustee funds by ~3.6% (Pool-Sialm-Stefanescu).
- Mutual fund companies earn ~30%+ operating margins (Janus 32%); managerial talent accrues to firm per Berk-Green, not shareholders.
## Anti-patterns
- **Chasing star performers** — investors pile into high-past-return funds (Janus Twenty) just as mean reversion/reversion hits; future returns disappoint.
- **Ignoring expense ratios** — 71% of investors (AARP) don't know they pay fees; in-your-face loads drive decisions while operating expenses quietly compound.
- **Survivorship-blind fund selection** — buying "100% of funds beat benchmark" marketing claims that quietly omit euthanized funds.
- **Soft-dollar & revenue-sharing conflicts** — undisclosed brokerage kickbacks hide true costs and inflate advisory profits at shareholder expense.
- **Trustee self-dealing in 401(k) menus** — sponsor adds and retains its own underperforming funds and votes shares per sponsor ties.
## Key Takeaways
1. Treat the 40-Act as a *disclosure and structure wrapper*, not a skill guarantee — it mitigates worst abuses but the average active fund still underperforms after costs.
2. Minimize fees relentlessly: pick low expense ratios, avoid 12b-1 charges, watch share class — cheaper funds persistently outperform dearer ones, and trading/soft-dollar costs compound the drag.
3. Never chase past returns — fund flows are anti-predictive; high-past-return funds attract inflows that fail to repeat, and slow investor learning keeps capital sticky in underperformers.
4. Correct for biases: use survivorship- and incubation-adjusted databases when evaluating fund track records; live-only returns overstate by 1-4%/yr.
5. Prefer institutional share classes or piggy-back on funds with institutional monitoring — institutional oversight improves governance, lowers fees, and adds ~1.5%/yr.
6. Beware structural ETF traps: daily-reset levered/inverse ETFs do not deliver their headline multiple over multi-period horizons due to compounding.
