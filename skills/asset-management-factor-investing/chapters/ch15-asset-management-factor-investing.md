# Chapter 15: Delegated Investing

## Core Idea
What is best for the asset owner (principal) is usually not best for the delegated fund manager (agent). Principal-agent conflicts can be *mitigated* by appropriate governance structures and contracts. Effective boards advocate for principals' interests and — critically — should build *processes* for investment decisions rather than making those decisions directly. Standard linear contracts paired with static market benchmarks fail badly; smarter (especially factor-based) benchmarks, option-like compensation, and strong disclosure materially reduce agency costs.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **General principal-agent problem** — owner (principal) and manager (agent) have different information, effort, and risk preferences; optimal contracts trade off risk sharing and incentives.
- **Agency problems specific to delegated portfolio management** — manager self-interest, benchmark gaming, risk-shifting, hidden action (effort/skill) and hidden state (type), plus the additional public-pension conflict between politicians and taxpayers.
- **Optimal contracts in general agency problems (Holmstrom)** — trade off effort incentive vs. risk-sharing; linear contracts fail when outcomes are informative about effort.
- **Uniqueness of delegated portfolio management** — the agent *manages the benchmark itself*; outcomes are informative about skill but noisy; risk preferences differ across contract states.
- **Irrelevance result (Admati-Pfleiderer)** — linear, symmetric, benchmark-relative contracts cannot induce first-best effort; many standard fee structures are effectively irrelevant to incentives.
- **Optimal design of benchmarks** — rather than a static S&P-500-like benchmark, use **factor benchmarks** (market + size + value + momentum + volatility); adjusting alpha for factor exposure raises the bar and shrinks gaming room.
- **Optimal contracts (nonlinear / option-like)** — option-like compensation (carries / fulcrum incentive fees) optimally motivates skill when paired with the right benchmark; constraints (long-only, leverage, asset) play a key role.
- **Paying financial advisors and asset managers** — the standard 1% retail / ~50bp institutional AUM fee mostly compensates the *advice/relationship*, not skill; incentive fees should be only a fraction of total compensation, paid on factor-adjusted alpha.
- **Boards** — build entry, monitor, and remove processes; do *not* make security-level decisions. Effective boards advocate for owners and hire/fire on process quality.
- **Board membership & duties** — independent, expert, diverse; asset-owner representatives plus investment professionals; clear separation of oversight from management.
- **Agency issues as a factor** — agency frictions themselves carry expected costs (a "bad time") and should be priced into mandated returns, like any other factor risk.

## Key Concepts
- **Principal-agent at two layers** — owner ↔ manager, but (for public plans) also citizen ↔ politician ↔ manager; layering compounds conflicts (NYSCRF sole-trusteur scandals).
- **Static benchmark irrelevance** — a market-cap benchmark plus a linear fee gives the manager slack to harvest factor exposures without contributing alpha; the owner pays for beta.
- **Factor benchmarking** — regressing active returns on market, size, value, momentum, and volatility factor portfolios isolates true alpha; only the residual justifies incentive pay.
- **Constraints as incentive tools** — fewer constraints suit higher-skill agents; constrained contracts (long-only, leverage caps) suit lower-skill or marginal agents.
- **Disclosure is paramount** — transparent contracts, fees, and factor exposures allow the owner to monitor; opacity enables gaming.
- **Linear vs nonlinear compensation** — AUM-based linear fees pay regardless of skill; option-like carry with hurdles and clawbacks aligns upside with skill (with anti-gaming design).
- **Build process, not decisions** — Boards that pick stocks or managers ad hoc underperform; the duty is to design selection, monitoring, and removal systems.
- **Public-pension salary caps** — public plans cannot afford top investment talent in-house, pushing them toward external agents with the same agency costs plus consultant layers.
- **Sole-trustee risk** — concentrating oversight in one elected official is a leading indicator of corruption (Regan, McCall, Hevesi NYSCRF convictions).

## Anti-patterns
- **Paying incentive fees on raw returns or naive market benchmarks** — pays for beta the owner could replicate for 5bp; inflates "alpha".
- **Benchmark hugging & tracking-error gaming** — managers deliver index-like returns while charging active fees; no alpha but no breach.
- **Linear-only contracts** — flat AUM fees mute incentives and reward AUM growth, not skill; without an option-like component, talent leaves or rents the franchise.
- **Sole-trustee / politically appointed boards** — concentrates agency risk; invites pay-to-play and political allocation of capital.
- **Boards making security calls** — directors overruling staff on individual holdings is process failure; set policy, monitor, and remove managers, do not trade.
- **Opaque side letters and placement fees** — hidden MFN terms and pay-to-play placement (Hevesi) corrupt delegation; full disclosure neutralizes.
- **Pretending public-pension salary caps are costless** — agencies forced to hire cheap talent end up paying high external fees net of in-house savings.

## Key Takeaways
1. **Build factor-based benchmarks** — adjust alpha and incentive pay for market + size + value + momentum + volatility exposures; pay only for the residual.
2. **Use option-like compensation with high water marks, hurdles, clawbacks**, paid only as a *fraction* of total compensation; pair with the right benchmark to incentivize skill.
3. **Make contracts transparent** — full disclosure of fees, factor exposures, and side letters is the cheapest and most effective monitoring tool.
4. **Boards design process, not trades** — establish selection, monitoring, and removal systems; resist ad-hoc security or manager decisions.
5. **Govern public plans against political capture** — independent boards, salaries aligned with market talent, and disclosure neutralize the citizen-politician-manager conflict.
6. **Treat agency risk as a factor** — demand an expected-cost premium from delegated mandates comparable to other factor risks; illiquid, opaque, or political mandates warrant especially high premia.
