# Glossary — *Asset Management: A Systematic Approach to Factor Investing* (Andrew Ang)

Alphabetical reference of the key terms introduced across all 18 chapters. Definitions cite the chapter where each concept is most central.

**40-Act (Investment Company Act of 1940)** — U.S. regulatory wrapper for public investment companies (mutual funds, ETFs, CEFs, UITs); mandates disclosure, NAV pricing, leverage limits, and (for incentive fees) fulcrum symmetry. Mitigates but does not eliminate principal-agent conflicts. (Ch.16)

**Active share / closet indexing** — fraction of a portfolio differing from its benchmark; diseconomies of scale force large funds to hug the index, collecting active fees for passive exposure. (Ch.14,16)

**After-tax return** — pre-tax return reduced by the investor's specific tax treatment (LTCG, STCG, income, tax-exempt); the only return investors actually keep. (Ch.12)

**Agency issues (owner-level)** — principal-agent frictions at the asset-owner layer (SWF political capture, pension sole-trustee abuse, family nepotism) that precede and compound delegation to managers. (Ch.1,15)

**Alpha** — average return in excess of a benchmark; a *joint hypothesis* of benchmark and market efficiency, not a measure of skill. Shrinks toward zero as factor sets expand. (Ch.10,14)

**Annuity puzzle** — theory says full annuitization maximizes retirement welfare (insures longevity), yet few households annuitize, driven by bequest motives, illiquidity, distrust, and adverse selection. (Ch.5)

**APT (Arbitrage Pricing Theory, Ross 1976)** — first multifactor model; many non-diversifiable factors priced by no-arbitrage rather than equilibrium. (Ch.6)

**Asset location** — choosing in which account (taxable, tax-deferred, tax-exempt) to hold each asset; separable from allocation once wealth is converted to a taxable-equivalent basis (Huang). (Ch.12)

**Bad times** — the state in which an investor's marginal utility is highest: low wealth, low consumption, losses vs a reference point, underperformance vs peers, or violation of ethical norms. Each factor defines its own bad times. (Ch.2,6,8)

**BAB (Betting Against Beta, Frazzini-Pedersen)** — long low-beta / short high-beta strategy capturing the low-risk anomaly created by leverage constraints. (Ch.10)

**Benchmark** — a replicable, tradeable portfolio against which alpha and incentive fees are measured; must be well-defined, replicable by both sides, factor- and beta-adjusted. (Ch.10,14,15)

**Black-Tepper pension arbitrage** — corporation borrows, deducts interest, and invests in the tax-exempt pension fund to harvest the bond risk premium while holding equity. (Ch.12)

**Bond-like vs stock-like labor income** — safe/stable wages mimic a bond holding (justify more equity when young); volatile or equity-correlated wages mimic an equity holding (justify defensive financials). (Ch.5)

**Break-even inflation** — nominal minus linker yield; *compensation* for inflation risk, not a market forecast, and distorted by liquidity premia. (Ch.11)

**Berk-Green equilibrium** — diseconomies of scale force managerial talent to accrue to the fund firm's profits, not to investors; larger funds do worse. (Ch.16,18)

**CAPM (Sharpe/Lintner/Mossin)** — single-factor model: the market portfolio is the only priced factor; asset risk = beta with the market. (Ch.6,8)

**Carhart (1997) four-factor model** — Fama-French three factors plus momentum (UMD), the standard stock-benchmark set for alpha measurement. (Ch.10,14)

**Carry** — return from holding a higher-yielding asset financed by a lower-yielding one; a style premium recurring across equities, bonds, FX, and commodities. (Ch.7)

**Certainty equivalent** — risk-free return making an investor indifferent to a risky position; cardinal measure of utility. Useful for costing illiquidity, lock-ups, and non-diversification. (Ch.2)

**Closet indexing** — see Active share.

**Committed capital / capital-called-over-commitments** — GP raises commitments and calls capital on demand over a ~10-year PE horizon; the fee base is *committed*, not invested. (Ch.18)

**Commodity returns** — decompose into spot, cash/collateral, and roll; only the energy-linked parts hedge demand-pull inflation. (Ch.11)

**Consumption CAPM (Breeden 1979)** — SDF = marginal utility of consumption; CAPM is a special linear case; bad times = low consumption. (Ch.6,8)

**CRRA (Constant Relative Risk Aversion)** — `U(W)=W^(1−γ)/(1−γ)`; risk aversion constant across wealth; typical γ ≈ 2–4 (revealed preference), up to 8 in surveys. (Ch.2)

**Credit spread** — corporate minus Treasury yield; compensates default risk but is *much* larger than realized defaults (the credit-spread puzzle). (Ch.9)

**Credit-spread puzzle** — only a fraction of IG spread is explained by default losses; taxes, systematic jump risk, and illiquidity fill the gap, with equity correlations rising to 0.65–0.84 post-2005. (Ch.9)

**Crowding** — popular factor strategies lose premia as AUM accumulates; the 1990s size premium's disappearance is the precedent, and quant-meltdown de-leveraging is the extreme case. (Ch.7,17)

**De-leveraging spiral** — when crowded factor strategies all exit at once, illiquidity + leverage + margin calls compound into multi-standard-deviation moves with no fundamental news (Aug 2007). (Ch.17)

**Defined benefit (DB) vs defined contribution (DC)** — DB: sponsor bears investment and longevity risk; DC: employee bears risk. The shift to DC transfers risk from sponsor to individual. (Ch.1,5)

**Disappointment aversion (Gul 1991)** — endogenous-reference cousin of loss aversion: disappointing outcomes weighted more; admits solutions mean-variance cannot. (Ch.2)

**Diversification** — reducing portfolio variance by combining low-correlation assets; the foundational free lunch of mean-variance investing, though impaired when correlations rise in crises. (Ch.3)

**Dutch disease** — resource windfalls swell the local currency and hollow out manufacturing; SWFs combat it by investing abroad and releasing wealth gradually. (Ch.1)

**Dynamic programming / Merton-Samuelson** — long-horizon portfolio choice solved as nested one-period problems; the long horizon enters through indirect utility, not by acting differently. (Ch.4)

**Dynamic factor benchmark** — rule-based, non-cap-weighted portfolio tilting toward chosen factors (value, momentum, low-vol) with dynamic rebalancing. (Ch.7,14)

**Endowment model (Swensen)** — long-horizon owners tilt heavily into illiquid alternatives (PE, HF, real estate) for diversification and illiquidity premia; Harvard 2008 exposed its fragility. (Ch.1,13)

**Equity premium puzzle (Mehra-Prescott 1985)** — consumption models imply <1% premium for γ∈[1,10] vs ~6% historically observed. (Ch.8)

**Equal-weight (1/N)** — robust constrained special case ignoring all mean-variance inputs; empirically dominates unconstrained optimization. (Ch.3)

**ERISA** — U.S. law governing private employer-sponsored pension plans; establishes fiduciary duties and minimum funding standards. (Ch.1)

**ETF (in-kind creation/redemption)** — 40-Act registered, trades like a stock at ~NAV; levered/inverse daily-reset ETFs diverge sharply from their headline multiple over multi-period horizons. (Ch.16)

**Expected utility (von Neumann-Morgenstern)** — `U = E[U(W)]`; combines probabilities of outcomes with utility over outcomes; nests behavioral models. (Ch.2)

**Factor** — a systematic risk defining a set of bad times; investors bearing losses in those bad times earn premiums. Factors, not asset labels, drive returns. (Ch.6)

**Factor Recipe (4 criteria)** — a factor must (1) be academically grounded, (2) exhibit persistent premia, (3) cover bad times in history, and (4) be implementable in liquid traded instruments. (Ch.14)

**Fama-French (1993) three-factor model** — MKT + SMB (size) + HML (value); explains much of the cross-section CAPM misses. (Ch.7,10)

**Flight-to-quality** — capital flight into safe assets during crises; drives Treasury yields *down* even when the country's own rating is cut (U.S. downgrade Aug 2011). (Ch.9)

**Fulcrum performance fee** — 40-Act-mandated symmetric, benchmark-centered incentive fee; bans the option-like asymmetric contracts hedge funds use. (Ch.15,16)

**Funding ratio** — assets/liabilities; ERSRI was 59% funded; U.S. public plans collectively far short of full funding. (Ch.1)

**Garbage in, garbage out (GIGO)** — mean-variance weights are extremely sensitive to input errors; small expected-return errors swing weights by huge amounts (Michaud "error-maximization"). (Ch.3)

**Gates / lock-ups / side pockets** — HF redemption restrictions imposing real illiquidity cost, often exceeding the (bias-free) reported illiquidity premium. (Ch.17)

**Glide path** — equity-weight trajectory of target-date funds; steepens for bond-like labor income, flattens for stock-like income; driven by *spent human capital*, not by horizon. (Ch.5)

**Gold** — poor long-run inflation hedge (real return ≈ 0); co-moves with real rates and safe-haven demand, not CPI. (Ch.11)

**Grossman-Stiglitz (1980)** — markets are *nearly* efficient; costly information creates pockets of inefficiency where active management can earn excess returns. (Ch.6)

**Habit utility (Sundaresan, Constantinides, Campbell-Cochrane)** — bad times = wealth approaching the habit level; risk aversion is endogenous and state-dependent. (Ch.2)

**Hedge fund (HF)** — not an asset class; a dynamic-factor portfolio dominated by volatility risk. After bias-adjustment and fees the average HF does not add value. (Ch.17)

**Hedging demand** — investors beyond the average hold the market plus/minus hedging portfolios against state-variable risks (labor income, inflation, liabilities). (Ch.6)

**HML (high-minus-low, value)** — long high book-to-market (value) / short low book-to-market (growth); large, persistent equity premium across countries and asset classes. (Ch.7)

**Home bias** — overweighting domestic assets beyond market-cap weights; driven by time-varying correlations, FX risk, transaction costs, asymmetric info, and behavior. (Ch.3)

**Human capital** — present value of future labor income; the largest "asset" for the young; bond-like or stock-like depending on wage stability and cyclicality. (Ch.5)

**ICAPM (Intertemporal CAPM, Merton 1971/1973)** — dynamic multi-period extension; state variables drive hedging demand, so investors hold the market plus hedging portfolios per state variable. (Ch.6)

**Illiquidity premium (across vs within class)** — *within*-asset-class premia are real and measurable; *across*-class premia are small after correcting biases and cannot separate factor risk from manager skill. (Ch.13)

**Incubation bias** — incubated, cherry-picked funds show inflated pre-launch returns that vanish once public. (Ch.16)

**Inflation hedging vs long-run return** — hedging is a *correlation* property (corr(return, inflation) ≈ 1); long-run return is a *mean*. A high-return asset can be a poor hedge; T-bills hedge best. (Ch.11)

**Information ratio (IR)** — alpha per unit of tracking error; the risk-free-benchmark special case equals the Sharpe ratio. (Ch.10)

**IPS / Ulysses contract** — Investment Policy Statement acting as a pre-commitment device against panic pro-cyclical exits. (Ch.4)

**IRR (internal rate of return)** — distorted by reinvestment/timing assumptions; routinely inflated by early distributions and manipulated by GPs. Not a comparable return metric. (Ch.18)

**i.i.d. equivalence** — when returns are not predictable, the long-run optimal portfolio equals the short-run; horizon is irrelevant and everyone acts myopically. (Ch.4)

**J-curve** — early PE funds show negative returns as fees and write-downs hit before exits; vintage-year return dispersion is dominated by vintage. (Ch.18)

**Kelly rule (optimal growth)** — log-utility portfolio maximizing long-run wealth; Samuelson warns against it for non-log-utility investors. (Ch.4)

**Level factor (Litterman-Shea PCA)** — the first principal component of yield changes, shifting all bonds together; accounts for ~90% of safe-bond return variation. Duration targets it. (Ch.9,14)

**Life-cycle models** — jointly solve consumption and portfolio choice over the life cycle over *total* wealth (financial + human). (Ch.5)

**Liquidity spirals** — when funding constraints bind, all illiquid assets bid together; the systematic funding-liquidity factor is distinct from trading illiquidity. (Ch.13)

**Long-run risks (Bansal-Yaron)** — persistent consumption growth and stochastic volatility over long horizons carry their own risk premium. (Ch.8)

**Loss aversion (Kahneman-Tversky 1979)** — prospect-theory kink: losses weighted ~2.25× gains; convex over losses (risk-seeking), concave over gains; plus probability weighting. (Ch.2)

**Low-risk anomaly** — low-beta/low-vol stocks earn higher risk-adjusted returns; large, cross-asset, and persistent — "the mother of all inefficiencies." (Ch.10)

**Macro factors** — fundamental economy-wide risks: growth, inflation, volatility, productivity, demographics; each defines its own bad times. (Ch.7,9)

**Market incompleteness (Constantinides-Duffie)** — unhedgeable idiosyncratic labor-income shocks raise aggregate risk aversion during downturns, endogenously widening the equity premium. (Ch.8)

**Mean-variance frontier (Markowitz 1952)** — set of max-return portfolios per variance level; expands with low correlations; all individual assets lie inside or on it. (Ch.3)

**Minimum-variance portfolio** — uses only the covariance matrix; a robust constrained special case that empirically beats unconstrained optimization. (Ch.3)

**Momentum (UMD, Jegadeesh-Titman 1993)** — past winners outperform past losers; large and pervasive but crashes violently (2009); almost certainly behavioral and arbitrage-limited. (Ch.7)

**Muni puzzle / MOB trade** — long-muni yields exceed the simple `y_muni = (1−τ)y_tsy` relation; MOB (munis-over-bonds) is a term-spread trade that crashed in 2008 with volatility. (Ch.12)

**NAV (net asset value)** — 40-Act transparent per-share value; appraisal-based or stale NAVs *smooth* returns, biasing volatility down and autocorrelation up. (Ch.13,16)

**Non-participation** — many households hold no stocks; explained by non-MV utility, participation costs, social factors, and ambiguity aversion. (Ch.3)

**Optimal sovereign wealth (Norway)** — diversify a windfall, immunize against Dutch disease, smooth consumption across generations, and quarantine wealth from political capture. (Ch.1)

**Option-like compensation** — nonlinear incentive fees (carry, fulcrum) that optimally motivate skill when paired with the right benchmark. (Ch.15)

**Overweighting employer stock** — ties financial and labor fortunes in one firm (Enron); idiosyncratic risk not compensated by any premium. (Ch.5)

**Pattern/PCA statistical factors** — pure statistical factors without economic meaning; prefer factors tied to identifiable bad times. (Ch.6)

**PME (Public Market Equivalent, Kaplan-Schoar 2005)** — discounts PE cash flows at realized public-equity returns to benchmark PE fairly; the only honest PE-vs-public comparison. (Ch.18)

**Predictability (equity premium)** — valuation ratios and low risk-free rates forecast long-horizon excess returns, but signals are statistically weak and sample-dependent. (Ch.8)

**Preference / utility** — quantitative characterization of how an investor feels during bad times; the starting point of all asset management ("know thyself"). (Ch.2)

**Principal-agent problem** — owner (principal) and manager (agent) have divergent information, effort, and risk preferences; mitigated by governance, benchmarks, and contracts. (Ch.15)

**Private equity** — not an asset class; public equity risk wrapped in leverage, illiquidity, and opaque contracts; alpha ~0 after size/value/leverage/illiquidity adjustment. (Ch.18)

**Pro-cyclical commitments ("money chasing deals")** — LPs commit at fundraising peaks (low forward returns) and pull back at troughs (high forward returns). (Ch.18)

**Quant meltdown (Aug 2007)** — quant funds lost 30% in three days with no market-wide decline; crowding + de-leveraging spirals in similar factor portfolios. (Ch.17)

**Rare disasters (Rietz 1988; Barro 2006)** — low-probability catastrophic crashes rationally produce a high equity premium because equities collapse exactly in those disaster states. (Ch.8)

**Rational vs behavioral premia** — rational premia persist while the underlying bad-time risk endures; behavioral premia carry regime-change and arbitrage risk that can erode them. (Ch.7)

**Real assets** — inflation-indexed bonds, commodities, real estate; many are not actually "real" (poor inflation hedging); T-bills hedge inflation best. (Ch.11)

**Rebalancing premium / diversification return** — extra geometric return ≈ ½σ² from counter-cyclical rebalancing back to fixed weights via Jensen's inequality. (Ch.3,4)

**Reference Portfolio (CPPIB)** — cheap passive equity/bond benchmark that funds all other investments; illiquid assets are decomposed into factor equivalents and "funded" by transfers from it. (Ch.14)

**Replacement rate** — retirement income as a fraction of final salary; target ~70–80%; the 4% rule roughly finances this on 60/40 over 30 years. (Ch.5)

**Risk aversion (γ)** — curvature of utility; γ≈1 risk-neutral, γ≈3 typical market, γ≈8 high. Reveal it from observed holdings (60/40 ⇒ γ≈2.8). (Ch.2)

**Risk parity** — equal-risk-contribution portfolio; a robust constrained special case. (Ch.3)

**SDF / pricing kernel / stochastic discount factor** — general `m = a + Σb_k f_k`; requires only no-arbitrage; nests CAPM, APT, ICAPM as restricted forms. `P_i = E[m×payoff_i]`. (Ch.6)

**Selection bias** — transaction indices select properties that *trade*; PE databases select funds that *report*; both overstate averages. (Ch.11,13,16)

**Sharpe ratio** — (E(r)−rf)/σ; reward-to-risk of a zero-cost long-risky / short-risk-free position. (Ch.2,10)

**SMB (small-minus-big, size)** — small caps historically beat large caps; premium shrank/disappeared post-1985, possibly due to product creation. (Ch.7)

**Smoothing bias** — appraisal-based indices smooth returns, hiding true volatility; unsmoothing recovers risk but not expected return. (Ch.11,13)

**Sole trustee** — concentrating oversight of a public pension in one elected official; leading indicator of corruption (NYSCRF scandals). (Ch.1,15)

**Sovereign wealth fund (SWF)** — government-controlled investment fund partly in foreign assets; instruments to combat Dutch disease and share wealth across generations. (Ch.1)

**Statistical arbitrage / market-neutral** — quantitative long/short styles; high-frequency (seconds-days) or lower-frequency (weeks-months) using value, size, momentum, vol, and credit factors. (Ch.17)

**Style analysis (Sharpe 1992)** — time-varying factor exposures estimated via constrained regression on tradeable index funds. (Ch.10)

**Survivorship bias** — failed funds/properties vanish from databases, overstating averages by ~1–4%/yr. (Ch.13,16,17)

**Surplus** — assets minus liabilities; the right optimization object for liability-driven owners. (Ch.1)

**Tail risk** — short-vol, carry, and HF strategies earn steady premia then lose 70%+ in a single crisis; losses cluster at the worst possible time (steamroller). (Ch.2,17)

**Taylor rule (1993)** — `i_t = r* + π_t + 0.5(π_t−π*) + 0.5(y_t−y*)`; deviations (Taylor gaps) forecast rate moves and the level factor. (Ch.9)

**Term spread** — long minus short yield; the second PCA factor of bonds (slope); long bonds earn a term premium that compensates for duration and level risk. (Ch.9)

**Time diversification fallacy** — longer horizon does *not* make equities safer; cumulative variance rises with horizon. The young-equity case rests on human capital, not horizon. (Ch.5,8)

**TIPS / linkers** — inflation-indexed bonds; a single linker funds a known real liability, but the *asset class* aggregate has near-zero inflation correlation. Hold in tax-deferred accounts. (Ch.11)

**Total wealth** — financial wealth + human capital; allocate over total, not just the tradeable part. (Ch.5)

**Tracking error** — volatility of active returns relative to the benchmark; the denominator of the information ratio and a constraint that prevents arbitraging low-risk anomalies. (Ch.10)

**Transparency / disclosure** — full disclosure of fees, factor exposures, and side letters is the cheapest and most effective monitoring tool; neutralizes opacity-driven gaming. (Ch.15)

**Uniqueness of delegated portfolio management** — the agent *manages the benchmark itself*; outcomes are informative about skill but very noisy, making contracts hard to design. (Ch.15)

**Unsmoothing returns** — back out true volatility from the autocorrelation of smoothed observed returns; affects risk estimates, not expected returns; an art. (Ch.13)

**Value premium (HML)** — high book-to-market beats low book-to-market; large, persistent across countries and asset classes; rational (distress/long-run risk) and behavioral (overextrapolation) theories coexist. (Ch.7,10)

**Variance drain** — geometric return ≈ arithmetic return − ½σ²; compounding erodes wealth and the rebalancing premium recovers the lost fraction. (Ch.4)

**Volatility risk (HF / corporate bond)** — many styles are *short volatility*; controlling for vol-of-vol factor risk shrinks HF alpha and widens corporate spreads toward zero. The unifying bad time. (Ch.9,17)

**Volatility timing** — time-varying equity weight inversely proportional to VIX beats static 60/40; equity volatility is far more forecastable than the equity premium itself. (Ch.8)

**Vintage year** — vintage-year dispersion dominates PE return; capital committed in a vintage and that vintage's return are strongly negatively correlated. (Ch.18)

**Wash / loss harvesting / tax-timing option (Constantinides 1983)** — realize losses immediately, defer gains indefinitely; separable from portfolio choice. (Ch.12)

**Waterfall / hurdle / catch-up / clawback** — PE distribution mechanics determining how carry flows to GPs vs LPs; the contract design that governs where incentives actually bite. (Ch.18)

**Zombies** — PE investments held at cost (>5% of holdings) to milk fees, upward-biasing reported NAVs. (Ch.18)
