# Chapter 2: Measuring "Joe Options Trader" Sentiment

## Core Idea
Options traders, as a group, make consistently poor directional bets. Their put and call volume — captured in put/call ratios — provides a reliable contrarian sentiment gauge. High put/call = excessive bearishness (buy signal); low put/call = excessive bullishness (sell signal).

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Put/Call Ratio as Bear/Bull Intensity Ratio**: Daily put volume divided by daily call volume. Readings above 1.0 = fear spike; below 0.5 = complacency.
- **Smoothing with Moving Averages**: Raw daily series is noisy; 10-day and 21-day EMA smoothing reveals the sentiment signal.
- **Butterfly Pattern**: The put/call ratio chart forms an inverse relationship with the S&P 500 — valleys at market peaks, peaks at market bottoms.

## Key Concepts
- **Marty Zweig**: Invented the put/call ratio in 1971.
- **Leverage Attracts the Unsophisticated**: Options allow directional bets with 10% of stock cost, attracting undercapitalized, inexperienced traders.
- **Delta and Time Decay**: Options are wasting assets; traders must be right on direction, timing, and volatility.
- **Why It Works**: At extremes, either few buyers or few sellers remain, making reversal inevitable. The identity of the trader (retail vs professional) is irrelevant at extremes.

## Anti-patterns
- Using fixed threshold levels (e.g., below 40 = buy) — trendiness in the data over decades makes static levels useless.
- Ignoring that professionals also get caught in herd mentality at extremes.

## Key Takeaways
- CBOE equity-only put/call ratio is the purest measure of the speculative crowd.
- Smoothed series (10-21 day EMA) is essential to filter noise.
- The options crowd's systematic wrongness is a persistent market inefficiency.
